A frequency sweep is the systematic variation of a stimulus signal's [[frequency]] across a defined range while the response of a system under test is recorded, producing a response-versus-frequency curve rather than a single-point measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> It is the fundamental method behind Bode plots, [[impedance]]-versus-frequency graphs, filter characterisation, EMC scans and mechanical vibration qualification, and the swept stimulus is what makes the [[frequency-response|frequency response]] obtainable at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> The technique is instrument-agnostic: the same result can be had from a dedicated network analyser, a spectrum analyser with a tracking generator, or a bare [[function-generator|function generator]], a sine source and a [[multimeter]] working through the range by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

## Sweep parameters

A sweep is defined by a start frequency, a stop frequency, a number of points, and a sweep time. The range is chosen from what is already known or suspected about the device: a probe circuit expected to roll off around 6.5 MHz is swept from 1 Hz to 20 MHz so the corner sits comfortably inside the span, with 1,000 points being ample resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> An amplifier expected to reach about 300 kHz of [[bandwidth]] is swept from 10 Hz to 1 MHz, giving margin on both sides of the roll-off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup> The axis is normally logarithmic when the span covers several decades, as in an impedance plot running from 100 kHz to 60 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

Common spans follow the physics of the domain rather than the instrument. [[bypass-capacitor|Bypass capacitor]] impedance work runs to 60 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> A programmable filter with a 100 kHz upper limit is swept to exactly that ceiling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> Acoustic measurement uses the audio band, 20 Hz to 20 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IVWhoGFJQAY" title="Measuring Room &amp; Reference Monitor Speaker Acoustics">•</a></sup> [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] measurement of an amplifier front end runs 50 Hz to 10 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) Explained &amp; Measured">1521</a></sup> A spectrum analyser with a tracking generator can sweep the instrument's full span, for example zero to 1.5 GHz about a 750 MHz centre.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup>

## Sweep time and the low-frequency penalty

Sweep time is not free, and the cost concentrates at the bottom of the range. Low frequencies require long dwell per point, so extending a start frequency downward disproportionately lengthens the run: dropping a network analyser's start to 1 Hz can push a sweep into the minutes, and the slowdown is visible as the sweep crawls through the first part of the span.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> For this reason a practical lower bound is chosen deliberately rather than set to the instrument's minimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup>

The same tradeoff governs receiver bandwidth. Narrowing the receiver bandwidth — for instance from 300 Hz down to 30 Hz — cleans up a measurement that is down in the [[noise]] and improves accuracy, at the direct cost of a slower sweep.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> In EMC conducted emissions work the recommended sweep speed for a full scan can imply roughly 16 minutes of acquisition; restricting the frequency range, in one case to 0 Hz to 1 MHz, brings the automatic sweep time down to around 200 seconds, which is far more workable for iterative pre-compliance testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup>

## Establishing the response curve

Interpreting a swept response requires knowing that the fixture itself is flat. The standard procedure is to run the sweep first with a known component in place of the device under test — replacing a [[capacitor]] with a plain [[resistor]] alongside the existing 10 ohm shunt — and confirm the measured response is flat before trusting any structure seen with the real part fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> Cabling, BNC connectors and adapters all contribute at high frequency, and without this reference run their artefacts are indistinguishable from device behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

Where a swept sine source is unavailable, broadband random noise substitutes: driving a filter with 1 V RMS of random noise into a dynamic signal analyser yields the same transfer function without a sweeping generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

## Instruments

**Spectrum analyser with tracking generator.** The tracking generator output follows the analyser's own sweep across its entire span, and it is that synchronised sweep which produces the frequency response of the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup> A swept superheterodyne analyser also differs in kind from an [[fft|FFT]]-based measurement, physically sweeping across frequency rather than transforming a captured record.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup>

**Vector network analyser.** Gain-phase and impedance measurements, including reflected S11, are configured as a start-stop pair with a chosen receiver bandwidth — 10 Hz to 20 MHz, or 100 Hz to 50 MHz over the full range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> Impedance analysers extend the same swept approach to material properties, including dielectric constant and permeability, and to DC bias characterisation of capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup>

**Function generator plus [[oscilloscope]].** A Bode plot can be obtained without a network analyser by driving a sine sweep and tricking the oscilloscope into displaying frequency on its horizontal axis: a generator in sine sweep mode set to a 1 second sweep from 1 Hz to 100 kHz makes the horizontal time axis a proxy for frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> Modern oscilloscopes integrate this directly, pairing a built-in function generator with a Bode plot function, in one case a dual-channel 100 MHz generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> Sweep is a near-universal mode on signal sources, appearing alongside modulation and burst on RF signal generators, bench function generators, and scope-integrated generators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cziiWo6Uh5M" title="$5000 Uni-T UTG9504T 500MHz Function Generator Partial Review (The Lost Tapes)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-351" title="EEVblog #351 - Silicon Chip Magazine - Electronex 2012">351</a></sup> The sweep parameter need not be frequency alone; amplitude, offset, symmetry and phase can each be swept over time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup>

The value of the feature is measured by its controls. An instrument that cannot sweep or modulate its generator, and therefore cannot produce a Bode plot, is functionally rudimentary regardless of price — a criticism levelled at a six-thousand-dollar integrated instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> Sweep implementations that expose only a centre frequency and span, with no settable start frequency, are similarly limited.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LbqnHtNPt9Y" title="Siglent Oscilloscope - Function Gen USB Connection Test">•</a></sup>

**Simulation.** SPICE AC analysis is a frequency sweep in software, specified identically by sweep type (linear or logarithmic), point count, start frequency and stop frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

## Sweeps outside the electrical domain

**Mechanical vibration.** Electrodynamic shakers sweep a [[pcb|PCB]] or product over a frequency span, typically from a few hertz up to around 10 kHz, in each of three orthogonal axes in turn, to identify vibrational modes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> A reference [[accelerometer]] stays on the shaker plate to characterise the system itself, and additional accelerometers are then mounted on the product; the resulting sweeps are compared against a military or in-house vibration standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Dips in the response are candidate mechanical resonances, though the setup must be validated before such features are attributed to the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>

**Acoustics.** Room and monitor measurement sweeps 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and repeating the sweep — eight passes averaged, at roughly 43 seconds total — yields a better result than a single pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IVWhoGFJQAY" title="Measuring Room &amp; Reference Monitor Speaker Acoustics">•</a></sup>

**EMC.** Both emissions and immunity work are swept. A wide conducted-emissions sweep localises where a design is marginal, in one case identifying problems below roughly 2.5 MHz with the spectrum above that dropping off acceptably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> Near-field magnetic loop probes are reciprocal: the same loops used to pick up fields can be driven with a swept signal to generate a field and inject it into a product, testing immunity across frequency rather than at a single point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup>

## Sweeping as a servo mechanism

Sweeping is also used as a dither for locking rather than as a measurement in itself. In a rubidium frequency standard the RF interrogation signal is swept by a couple of hundred hertz around the 6.834 GHz hyperfine transition of rubidium; at resonance the atoms absorb light, the photocell behind the resonance cell sees less of it, and the transimpedance amplifier's output becomes an error [[voltage]] the loop servos on to hold lock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup>
