A serial port is an interface that moves data one bit at a time over a small number of lines — in the simplest asynchronous case just transmit, receive and [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-135" title="EEVblog #135 - Kindle Case Mythbusting">135</a></sup> Because it costs so few pins and so little board area, it is the default way a [[processor]] talks to a peripheral chip, a second processor, a PC, or an engineer holding a terminal program.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Its practical importance today is less about connecting equipment than about access: a serial port is very often the only doorway into a piece of embedded gear that was never meant to be opened.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup>

## Serial versus parallel

The trade is [[bandwidth]] against pin count and complexity. A parallel interface transfers a byte per clock but demands eight or more data lines plus control, and debugging it means watching all of them at once — impractical with a two-channel scope and a nuisance even with a logic analyser, where probing sixteen channels is its own hassle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup> A serial interface reduces the same job to one or two signals whose timing can be verified on a single trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup> Where a display or peripheral supports both modes and development speed matters more than transfer rate, serial mode is the better starting point: a stubborn bring-up problem on a 128 by 64 graphical [[lcd|LCD]] was resolved only after rewiring from 8-bit parallel to [[spi|SPI]], at which point the scope showed the timing was correct and the fault had to be in the driver code.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup> Some peripherals share a single pinout across both modes, with the same pin acting as serial input/output or, in parallel mode, as not-connected or reset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup>

The same cost applies at the interface level. JTAG boundary scan is powerful precisely because it can read live pin state, but it is a serially shifted system, so it is not fast and not real-time; on an [[fpga|FPGA]] with hundreds of I/O pins the entire chain has to be shifted through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup>

## Chip-level serial interfaces

Most of the serial links in a modern instrument never leave the board. A digital step attenuator such as the Hittite HMC624LP4 takes its attenuation setting over a digital serial or parallel interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> A 1.2 GHz PLL such as the Analog Devices ADF4117 is programmed by sending a serial command to select the local [[oscillator]] [[frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup> A [[thermal-camera|thermal camera]] core reads out over a serial interface and signals back over the same path when it decides a shutter compensation is due, leaving the host micro to agree and drive the shutter coil.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> [[vacuum-fluorescent-display|Vacuum fluorescent display]] panels are driven by serial-input, latched-output driver chips, ten outputs apiece, with a [[microcontroller]] behind them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> Even a fully digital pyroelectric sensor packages an ADC, a decimator and a serial interface to the host micro on one part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-275" title="EEVblog #275 - PIR Sensor Teardown &amp; Tutorial">275</a></sup>

The pattern extends to instrument architecture. A [[multimeter]] front-end chip does only measurement — range switching, ADC, generators — and cannot drive a display; it presents a serial output to a secondary processor that handles the LCD and everything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Where the measurement section must be isolated, the serial link is the only thing crossing the barrier, through a reasonably high-speed [[optocoupler]], which is why the count of connections over the boundary is so small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup> That isolation is not automatic: some supplies route both a D9 and a [[usb|USB]] port to the same serial interface with no optical isolation anywhere on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Purpose-built parts exist for the isolated case — the LT6820 carries a full SPI-compliant interface over a single galvanically isolated twisted pair, one device at each end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup>

Isolation matters most on instruments with floating outputs. A bench supply's rear connectors referenced to a PC, which is itself referenced to [[mains-earth|mains earth]], can cause major problems, which is the argument for putting the serial interface on a separate board that can be built as plain 9-pin RS-232 or as an electrically isolated variant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Serial timing tolerance is loose enough that a ceramic resonator, good to about half a percent, is more than adequate for RS-232.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Microcontrollers integrate serial hardware, but never enough of it — bit-banging is inadvisable in most applications and is resorted to when there are not enough serial ports, or not the type wanted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Where a design needs many, the choice of part follows: a Rabbit 2000 was selected for a test system partly because it carried four serial ports on chip alongside a built-in real-time operating system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1632" title="EEVblog 1632 - WHY sell 2CH Oscilloscopes? + Test Engineering">1632</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (4 of 5): Open Source SDCC C Compiler">1306</a></sup> Integrated peripherals also fail in silicon. A PIC24F errata, buried at the bottom of the document, stated that serial port two simply did not work and that the pins on serial port one had been swapped, with no fix — the cure was mod wires on an already-built board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1288" title="EEVblog #1288 - Synology NAS Dumpster Find! (REPAIR)">1288</a></sup>

## Physical ports and level translation

Historically the [[connector]] was a D9 or D25.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> Machines that omitted the standard connector required a purchase: the IBM PC Jr used a proprietary serial connector and needed an adapter cable sold in its own box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> RS-232 signalling levels require a transceiver — a MAX232 sitting next to a TV's service jack marks that jack as a serial service interface,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup> and a MAX202 performs the same job on a supply's D9 board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Because the levels are true RS-232 rather than logic level, such a port cannot be driven directly from a 3.3 V TTL adapter; either a real serial port or a switchable adapter offering RS-232 or TTL levels at 5 V or 3.3 V is required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup>

Modern hosts reach the same ports through USB-to-UART bridges — FTDI FT232 and FT230X parts, STMicro virtual COM drivers, Prolific PL2303 — all of which enumerate as a COM port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-592" title="EEVblog #592 - Mailbag">592</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-776" title="EEVblog #776 - World&#x27;s Smallest Digital Dosimeter">776</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1684" title="EEVblog 1684 - Awesome Logging Software:  Test Controller">1684</a></sup> This is where most practical trouble lives. A driver may assign COM8 while the vendor's own software only supports up to COM6.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup> Automatic detection frequently fails, requiring the port to be typed in by hand after reading it out of Device Manager.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1684" title="EEVblog 1684 - Awesome Logging Software:  Test Controller">1684</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> A port that is visible may still refuse to open, either because the baud rate is unknown or because access is denied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> Typical rates in the corpus run from 9600 8-N-1 on small micros up to 115200 on instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (4 of 5): Open Source SDCC C Compiler">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup>

On PCs the port survives as a lab convenience rather than a consumer feature: its presence dates a machine, and it is one of the reasons a dumpster-found office PC is worth taking home. "Nothing beats having a nice good old D9 serial port on there."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Lhmg1jUNxVc" title="Yet another dumpster dive i7 machine!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1615" title="EEVblog 1615 - Dumpster Dive HP Pro One PC">1615</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1342" title="EEVblog #1342 - Asymmetrical Dumpster PC Stacking">1342</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1212" title="EEVblog #1212 - Dumpster Diving PC Motherlode!">1212</a></sup>

## Instrument control

For older test equipment the serial port is the friendly option. HP-era gear predates Ethernet and USB and is normally controlled over HPIB/GPIB — IEEE 488 — so an instrument that happens to offer a serial port with a string command set is markedly easier to talk to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1232" title="EEVblog #1232 - Add Web Access To Old Instruments!">1232</a></sup> A string-based command set such as SCPI is human-readable, easy to extend, and requires only that a string be sent to the device over the serial interface, with serial libraries available for every platform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GarToEo6ekQ" title="EEVacademy #9 - Implementing SCPI in C++">•</a></sup> Instruments that document their serial command set can be driven from user-written software on Linux or any other serial-equipped machine, which is often preferable to the vendor application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup>

Not every rear-panel serial connector is a control port. On one [[frequency-counter|frequency counter]] the serial port is only for connecting a serial printer, not for data extraction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup>

## Bringing up and reverse engineering hardware

On unfamiliar equipment, locating the serial port is the first move. There is no standard connector or pinout for it — unlike JTAG, which is often standardised — so the search is for a likely-looking header, commonly four pins carrying transmit, receive, ground and possibly power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup> A row of test points on a production board is frequently serial comms or JTAG.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup> Once found, the boot sequence is the payload: a device that reaches a splash screen is almost certainly emitting boot code, and capturing it typically yields U-Boot output and system information.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> Serial output labelled TTYS is itself the tell that a machine runs Linux.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-458" title="EEVblog #458 - Industrial Computer">458</a></sup> The technique does not always pay: some devices emit nothing at all from the boot serial port,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1462" title="EEVblog 1462 - Why Dymo Label Printers SUCK!">1462</a></sup> some emit boot text that carries no useful information and cannot be interrupted,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1154" title="EEVblog #1154 - Surprising 4K Dumpster TV Fault">1154</a></sup> and some stall in self-test and never print anything after power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup>

Consumer hardware exposes the same thing. A third-generation Kindle brought its serial interface out to four external contacts — TX, RX, ground and one further pin — and that access is what hackers used; the successor removed it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-135" title="EEVblog #135 - Kindle Case Mythbusting">135</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-205" title="Amazon Kindle 4 Review &amp; Unboxing - EEVblog #205">205</a></sup> Four external gold contacts on a tablet serve as a remote serial interface for programming, monitoring, debugging and factory [[firmware]] loading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> Digital calipers commonly bring a data port out to bare [[pcb|PCB]] pads, giving a serial output that can be read by a microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup>

## Configuration, firmware and field use

Serial is the routine channel for setting up and repairing equipment. A reflow oven's controller exposes serial commands that make every profile parameter settable, and a terminal session comparing its reported temperature against a reference thermometer showed a reading of 36 °C where 28 °C was correct — a [[calibration]] error found only because the serial console existed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> A clock module's real-time clock was set over serial commands even while its display stayed blank, isolating the fault to a 4.1 V supply rail rather than the communications path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> A solar [[inverter]]'s serial port, once the Wi-Fi dongle proved unusable, was cabled straight to a Raspberry Pi running monitoring software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup> A USB [[soldering]] station enumerates as a serial port for firmware updates and temperature setting, then retains the last temperature for use in the field without a computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> Where trimmer pots have been deleted from a meter's design, the addition of a receive channel alongside the existing transmit [[led|LED]] suggests calibration is now performed over the serial input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup>

Development boards follow the same convention. An [[arduino|Arduino]] identifies as a generic serial port, and choosing the right one is the only configuration step in the upload process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WXJP_CNYt3o" title="#1179 - Stempedia Evive Electronics Learning Kit">•</a></sup> A MicroPython interpreter running on the target is reached over the serial port from a terminal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-el6CB-h9Lo0" title="NES on a 64Bit RISC-V Sipeed MAIX">•</a></sup> Some cheap 8051 variants need nothing more than two pins connected to a serial port and an IDE plug-in to be programmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup>
