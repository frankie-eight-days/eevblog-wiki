Sample rate is the number of discrete samples a digitising instrument captures per second, expressed in samples per second (S/s) or megasamples/gigasamples per second (MS/s, GS/s). Together with [[analog-bandwidth|analog bandwidth]] and [[memory-depth|memory depth]], it determines what a [[digital-oscilloscope|digital oscilloscope]], logic analyser, or data logger can faithfully reproduce: insufficient sample rate produces [[aliasing]], distorted [[waveform]] reconstruction, and misleadingly precise-looking measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup>

## Sample rate versus bandwidth

The Nyquist criterion sets an absolute floor: the sampled [[bandwidth]] can be at most half the sample rate. A 40 MS/s ADC in a spectrum analyser's IF stage, for example, constrains the baseband [[frequency]] to below 20 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup> Nyquist is a floor rather than a target — at 2.5 samples per cycle a signal does not alias, but reconstruction quality is poor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup>

For sin(x)/x interpolation to reconstruct a waveform properly, "You need roughly four times the analog bandwidth in sample rate."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> At five times bandwidth — for instance a 200 MHz [[sine-wave|sine wave]] on a 1 GS/s scope — the display shows only five dots per cycle and looks visibly jaggy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> The long-standing industry benchmark is a 10:1 ratio of real-time sample rate to analog bandwidth, established when the Tektronix TDS220 paired 1 GS/s with 100 MHz bandwidth and set the trend for modern scopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-690" title="EEVblog #690 - TDS220 Oscilloscope Autopsy">690</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup> A working rule of thumb holds that usable single-shot bandwidth is roughly the sample rate divided by ten; a 300 MHz scope sampling at 500 MS/s has a real single-shot bandwidth of only about 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> Where a trade-off is forced, sample rate is generally more valuable than headline bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> At the high end, instruments reach 40 GS/s on 13 GHz-class scopes and beyond, with platforms announced at 40 GS/s and 16 GHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-340" title="EEVblog #340 - USB 3.0 Eye Diagram Measurement">340</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-114" title="EEVblog #114 - Electronex 2010 Electronics Exhibition">114</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5YjS4DHKlQU" title="Tektronix 5 Series Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>

## Aliasing and interpolation artifacts

Signals above half the sample rate fold back into the display as aliases. An 8.1 MHz signal sampled at 5 MS/s aliases immediately, while a slightly offset frequency such as 8.123 MHz can produce a benign beat pattern instead — the distinction between true aliasing and a sample beat frequency depends on the exact relationship between signal and sample clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> Beat artifacts of this kind appear around 250 MHz on a scope sampling near that region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup>

Interpolation can conceal insufficient sampling behind a plausible-looking trace. Sin(x)/x interpolation is only valid when the Nyquist requirement is met; with a 200 MHz bandwidth and only a 200 MS/s sample rate, the reconstruction is "completely mathematically invalid".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> Because scopes ship with interpolation on by default, a smooth but wrong waveform can send [[troubleshooting]] down a false path; switching to dot mode reveals the actual samples and exposes the artifact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup>

## Channel sharing and interleaving in oscilloscopes

The banner sample rate on a multi-channel [[oscilloscope]] is almost always a single-channel figure, because ADCs are shared across channels. Enabling a second channel typically halves the rate; enabling three or four can quarter it. The Rigol DS1054Z drops from 1 GS/s to 500 MS/s with two channels and 250 MS/s with three or four, because a single ADC serves all four channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> The same single-ADC cost-saving architecture appears in the GW Instek GDS-1000B (one HAD1511 converter, 250 MS/s across four channels), the Siglent SDS1104X-E (500 MS/s with all four channels), and the Rigol DHO800 (a single 1.25 GS/s ADC divided among four channels).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup>

Scopes with two ADCs — one per channel pair — let channels 1 and 3 (or 2 and 4) each retain full rate and full memory, while channels 1 and 2 together halve both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1530" title="EEVblog 1530 - Siglent SDS2000X HD 12 Bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1530</a></sup> Interleaving runs the other way: combining converter cores doubles the single-channel rate, so the Agilent 2000 X-Series reaches its advertised 2 GS/s only on one channel and drops to 1 GS/s when a second is enabled, and the 3000 X-Series similarly halves from 4 GS/s to 2 GS/s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> Some designs are worse than the banner suggests: the Tektronix MDO3000 series delivers only 2.5 GS/s at 1 GHz bandwidth even without additional channels enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup> Newer architectures can avoid the penalty entirely — the Keysight HD3 sustains 3.2 GS/s on all four channels simultaneously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> At the silicon level, high rates are built from many slower cores: the Rigol DS1052E interleaves ten 100 MHz ADCs to reach 1 GS/s, and the MDO3000's RF input ties the analog-channel ADCs together to sample at 10 GS/s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-19" title="EEVblog #19 - Rigol caught with their pants down!">19</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

## Interaction with memory depth and time base

Capture duration equals memory depth divided by sample rate, so a scope must reduce its sample rate at slow time bases once memory is exhausted. A scope with 4 Mpts of memory at 5 GS/s holds only 800 µs of data, forcing the rate down to 2.5 GS/s at 50 µs/div and 1.25 GS/s at 100 µs/div; with 400 Mpts it could sustain 5 GS/s far longer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> The same trade-off appears throughout: the Rigol DS1052E's full 1 Mpt memory is available only at 500 MS/s and below, and the Owon SDS series reaches 3.2 GS/s only at 1K or 10K record lengths, dropping to 1 GS/s at 100K and deeper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kdjpbWLi7UI" title="Full Review of the Rigol DS1052E  Oscilloscope">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Zooming out past the captured record, [[fft|FFT]] quality, and phase-[[measurement-resolution|measurement resolution]] are all bounded by this interplay of sample rate, memory depth, and [[time-base|time base]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup>

## Effect on displayed noise and acquisition modes

Higher sample rates admit more high-frequency [[noise]] content into the display, so a digital scope appears noisier at maximum sample rate; shortening the record length or limiting bandwidth visibly cleans up the trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Consequently, meaningful noise-floor comparison between two digital scopes requires matched sample rate, memory depth, bandwidth, intensity grading, and update rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> High-resolution acquisition mode exploits oversampling directly, boxcar-averaging samples taken at the scope's full rate to average out noise in real time at slow time bases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> Peak-detect mode similarly uses the underlying high sample rate to register narrow pulses that the decimated display rate would otherwise miss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup>

## Timing resolution in logic analysers and digital capture

In any sampling system, timing resolution is bounded by the sample period. Measuring a 2 MHz signal at 12 MS/s yields incorrect frequency readings because the temporal resolution is inadequate, and even 24 MS/s shows periodic duty-cycle errors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup> Analysis software can display a resolution finer than the hardware delivers — pulse widths shown to 1 ns resolution from an 8 MS/s capture are fictitious, and at 500 kS/s the true resolution is only 2 µs despite three-decimal readouts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup> Logic analysers distinguish asynchronous timing analysis from clock-synchronous state analysis, and the maximum sample rate for state analysis is generally lower than for timing analysis on any given unit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> Asynchronous timing capture also quantises edges to the sample period, producing inherent one-sample jitter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup>

## Sample rate in other instruments

Power and energy instruments sample continuously at fixed rates: the Voltech PM300 samples near 100 kHz to enable transient capture, the BattLab One's 1 kHz rate is adequate for active-[[current]] energy calculations, and the Uni-T UTE310 integrates [[voltage]] and current at 1 MS/s so short transmit spikes are not missed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> Logging interval choice matters at the slow end too: one sample per minute missed the entire sub-0.8 V knee of a [[battery-discharge-curve|battery discharge curve]], where one sample per second resolved it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Digitising multimeters span from the Gossen Metrawatt Xtra's 0.1 s to 9 h per sample up to the Keithley DMM7510's 1 M readings per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> In function generators the DAC sample rate sets the output ceiling — 500 MS/s in the Siglent SDG5000, 1.2 GS/s for the 16-bit DAC in the SDG2122X, and 2.5 GS/s in 500 MHz-class Uni-T generators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cziiWo6Uh5M" title="$5000 Uni-T UTG9504T 500MHz Function Generator Partial Review (The Lost Tapes)">•</a></sup> Converter resolution trades against speed: the Owon XDS3202A's 1 GS/s rate falls to about 125 MS/s in its 14-bit mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> At the application level, sensor sample rates are often trivially slow — strain gauges need only a few hertz and accelerometers rarely exceed tens of kilohertz — while ADC source [[impedance]], not just the converter, can cap the achievable sampling rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fm13tIe5wSc" title="EEVacademy #4 -  I²C (I2C) Bit Banging">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

## Misleading specifications and verification

Sample-rate claims require scrutiny. The headline figure applies to one channel, and some low-cost handhelds advertise "effective" rates achievable only through equivalent-time sampling: a tablet scope claiming 1 GS/s delivered only 200 MS/s real-time per channel, its two ADCs never interleaved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup> Equivalent-time mode is legitimate for repetitive signals — the GW Instek GDS-2000A switches to it at 1 ns/div and indicates 100 GS/s — but it is not real-time capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Older instruments sometimes concealed the figure entirely; the Fluke 91 Scopemeter manual omitted it on the theory that users did not understand "this sample rate stuff".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> On the Keysight 1000 X-Series, sample rate is set by configuration resistors and can be altered by modification, though pushing it to 5 GS/s produced broken behaviour at slow time bases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-978" title="EEVblog #978 - Keysight 1000X Oscilloscope Hacked!">978</a></sup> Sound bench practice is to keep the current sample rate and memory depth visible on screen — instruments that display both permanently support correct use, while those that hide them invite mismeasurement — and to recheck the rate whenever channels are enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup>
