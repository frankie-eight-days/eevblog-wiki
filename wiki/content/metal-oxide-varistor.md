A metal oxide varistor (MOV) is a [[voltage]]-dependent, nonlinear [[resistor]] used as a transient suppression device: it presents an open circuit at normal operating voltages and collapses to a low [[impedance]] when the voltage across it exceeds its nominal rating, shunting surge energy away from the circuitry it protects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> It matters because it is one of the primary devices by which multimeters, power supplies, and other mains-connected equipment absorb high-energy voltage transients — such as the 6–8 kV impulses a CAT III 1000 V meter is designed to survive — fast enough that slower protection elements like PTC thermistors cannot respond in time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>

## Operating principle

In its normal state an MOV is completely open circuit and has no effect on the circuit across which it is connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> When the applied voltage exceeds the device's nominal rated voltage, the MOV clamps down very quickly to a low impedance and shunts the surge [[current]] through itself, so that only a low voltage appears across the protected downstream circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The response is fast — on the order of microseconds to nanoseconds — which is precisely why MOVs are used where a PTC, which requires time to heat up and rise in [[resistance]], cannot act quickly enough to block a transient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup>

The [[schematic]] symbol is a standard resistor symbol with a small diagonal squiggly line through it, reflecting the hysteresis-like clamping behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Physically, MOVs in instruments are typically round radial disc devices, and they are often large and chunky because disc size relates directly to energy absorption capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>

The clamping action is a threshold phenomenon, not a gradual one. A meter protected by MOVs with an aggregate clamp voltage above the applied voltage can sit on a continuous DC overvoltage indefinitely without the MOVs conducting: a Brymen-based [[121gw|121GW]] meter sustained 1200 V DC continuously because the voltage was not enough to trigger its MOV chain into conduction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-YPUJipe8Loo" title="121GW Meter 1210V Test">•</a></sup> The same behaviour was measured directly on the BM2257, whose three-MOV string left the meter's 10 MΩ [[input-impedance|input impedance]] essentially unchanged (10.02 MΩ) even at 1100–1200 V applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup>

## Ratings and part numbering

MOV voltage ratings are commonly encoded in the part number. A Nippon Chemi-Con device marked 471K is a 470 V nominal part; CNR-brand parts marked 621 and 561 are 620 V and 560 V nominal devices respectively; devices marked 681 correspond to roughly 480 V RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1500" title="EEVblog 1500 - Automatic Transfer Switch REVERSE ENGINEERED">1500</a></sup> The same part carries an energy rating — the failed 471K MOV in a Keysight N8762A supply was a 30 joule device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>

## Series stacking

Placing several MOVs in series is standard practice rather than using a single higher-voltage part. Series stacking both increases total energy dissipation capability and increases creepage distance, since the physical gaps of a few millimetres between successive devices add up and prevent arc-over across the package during high-voltage transients.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Concrete examples:

- The Fluke 27 uses four 430 V MOVs in series, so it does not begin clamping until the input reaches roughly 1700 V — well above its 1000 V rated measurement range, on the basis that the rest of the input circuitry will survive 1700 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>
- The Brymen BM2257 uses two 620 V devices plus one 560 V device in series, giving a nominal 1800 V clamp, theoretically conducting only above that figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>
- [[multimeter|Multimeter]] input stages generally clamp below 2 kV; a typical arrangement might be two 900 V MOVs in series clamping at about 1800 V, after which the remaining [[input-protection|input protection]] can handle the residual safely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup>
- An electric fence controller used strings of three or six MOVs in series across its output because individually higher-rated parts were not used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup>

By contrast, paralleling MOVs is not sensible practice, since matched current sharing cannot be assumed; an apparent second parallel device on one board turned out to be a Y5V [[ceramic-capacitor|ceramic capacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup>

## Role in multimeter input protection

The canonical multimeter voltage-input protection scheme is a [[ptc-thermistor|PTC thermistor]] in series plus MOVs shunting toward the input-jack common (not necessarily the meter's logic [[ground]]).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The division of labour is temporal: the MOV clamps the transient within microseconds and its resulting low impedance drives a large current that heats the PTC relatively quickly; the PTC then rises into the megaohm range and cuts off current flow, while the MOV has absorbed the pulse energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Certification testing of CAT-rated meters applies high-energy input transients of various [[waveform|waveforms]] to verify that the MOVs and the meter survive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup> Gas discharge tubes serve the same clamping function as MOVs in some meters, such as Gossen and Hioki designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

Practical design details observed across instruments include: physical barriers around the MOVs so they cannot arc to nearby components such as the [[range-switch|range switch]]; high-voltage isolation slots coordinated with the clamp level (the Fluke 27's trimmer capacitors are rated 1700 V, matching its four-MOV string); and a direct, low-impedance return path from the MOVs back to the [[input-jack|input jack]] ground for the impulse energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Multiple protection paths are common where extra taps feed the range switch or ADC, with additional MOVs guarding each path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>

The presence, size, and count of MOVs is treated as a baseline indicator of multimeter safety engineering. A properly designed meter is expected to have a PTC, MOVs, a [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]], and HRC fuses; a meter lacking MOVs is judged not to meet its claimed CAT ratings regardless of labelling, because input resistors and PTCs alone cannot dissipate high-energy impulse energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup> Larger MOVs absorb more energy before destruction, so physically bigger devices count as better protection, all else equal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup>

## Mains and other applications

Beyond multimeters, MOVs appear across mains inputs of power supplies, televisions, UPS units, alarm panels, and chargers, typically ahead of the common-mode choke and bridge rectifier, alongside X/Y-class capacitors and fuses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-764" title="EEVblog #764 - New Lab Ceiling LED Lighting Installation">764</a></sup> Two distinct protection topologies are used because there are two distinct fault mechanisms: MOVs connected from each AC line down to [[mains-earth|mains earth]] clamp earth-referenced surges, while a separate device across the line pair clamps differential overvoltage; a large differential voltage can exist across the input without stressing the earth-referenced MOVs at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup>

MOVs are frequently combined with other devices:

- A gas discharge tube in series with an MOV, as found in a [[microinverter]] front end and in a Keysight bench multimeter's clamp-to-earth path (spark gap, MOV, and 100 Ω resistor in series to mains earth).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yOJ7xPugsdc" title="Hoymiles Microinverter Reverse Engineering CORRECTION">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup>
- A thermal [[fuse]] thermally coupled to the MOV body (heat-shrunk against it) so that if the MOV overheats under sustained conduction the fuse opens before the MOV explodes — used in UPS and appliance designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1168" title="EEVblog #1168 - Dumpster Diving UPS">1168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup>
- MOVs on floating outputs and sense inputs of bench power supplies, referenced to chassis earth rather than to the output common.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup>

Multilayer varistors (MLVs) are the surface-mount counterpart: zinc-oxide devices built like ceramic capacitors, available down to 0201 size, that become conductive in the presence of an excessive [[electric-field|electric field]]. Early MLVs wore out because inconsistent grain sizes caused uneven field stress; the failed grains became resistive and then flamed. Modern devices with tight grain-size control do not wear out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup>

## Failure modes

MOVs protect sacrificially. Under a high-energy overload they can blow apart violently — in one case the explosion of a meter's input MOV generated enough internal pressure to blow the range knob clean off — while leaving the rest of the instrument undamaged, which is exactly the intended behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-84" title="EEVblog #84 - High Energy Multimeter Destruction">84</a></sup> A 400 V-per-phase input applied to a Keysight supply blasted one 470 V MOV apart and sprayed its contents against a [[heat-sink|heatsink]] while the fuses and remaining MOVs protected everything downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup> Conversely, if the surge energy is low and fast enough, an MOV absorbs it internally with no external sign of damage and continues to work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup>

[[leakage-current|Leakage current]] is a real but manageable characteristic: MOVs shunted across a meter's 10 MΩ input will leak at high applied voltage, but device selection can keep leakage negligible even at 1200 V, as verified on the BM2257.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup>

An intact MOV measures as a very high resistance — on the order of several megohms — in-circuit, and a blown one typically reads open after failing; confirming full functionality, however, requires an actual overload test, since a visually undamaged device may be degraded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1424" title="EEVblog 1424 - Fluke 23 Multimeter Repair">1424</a></sup> On repair, a blown MOV should be replaced as a matter of course even though the equipment can function without it, and equipment may be powered under controlled conditions with the MOV removed since it exists purely for protection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>
