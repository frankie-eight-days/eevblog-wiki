A current shunt resistor is a low- or precisely known-value [[resistor]] placed in series with a [[current]] path so that the current can be determined by measuring the resulting [[voltage-drop]] and applying Ohm's law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> It is the near-universal basis of [[current-measurement]]: a [[multimeter]]'s current ranges, a [[power-supply]]'s current-limit loop, an [[electronic-load]], and a smart energy meter all reduce to a shunt resistor and something that measures the [[voltage]] across it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Its value is a fundamental engineering trade-off, because the same voltage drop that constitutes the measurement signal also subtracts from the circuit under test ([[burden-voltage]]) and dissipates power as heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup>

## Operating principle and value selection

The measured quantity is simply V = IR, so the shunt value is chosen to give a convenient scaling between current and voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> For a direct 1 mV-per-unit mapping, 1 mV per amp requires 1 mΩ, 1 mV per milliamp requires 1 Ω, 1 mV per microamp requires 1 kΩ, and 1 mV per nanoamp requires 1 MΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> Choosing values that preserve a matching relationship between millivolts and milliamps avoids any conversion in the readout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> The shunt value also determines the voltage dropped from the circuit under test; with a 0.1 Ω shunt, for example, a 2 A load produces only a 0.4 V drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

[[power-dissipation]] follows I²R and frequently drives the design downward in value. A 100 A smart meter phase limited to 2 W of shunt dissipation requires a shunt of only about 200 µΩ — so impractical that such meters use current transformers instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> At the other extreme, a 1 kΩ shunt suitable for microamp resolution would drop 100 V if the circuit under test suddenly drew 100 mA on waking from sleep, which is why no single shunt can cover microamps in the presence of amps and ranging is mandatory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

## Burden voltage

Burden voltage is the voltage dropped across a current-measuring instrument's terminals, comprising the shunt resistor's drop plus the [[resistance]] of the internal [[fuse]] and any switch contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> In a typical multimeter the shunt produces about 50 mV at full scale on each range, which may appear on a [[datasheet]] as a maximum burden-voltage specification, but ranges that share a shunt across two full-scale currents can reach 500 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Near full scale the drop grows, and in low-voltage circuits — a 3.3 V or 1.2 V logic rail — a half-volt or volt of burden can disturb or shut down the circuit being measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-i069j6S80OU" title="uCurrent">•</a></sup> In one demonstration, switching from a low-burden adapter to a meter's internal shunt raised the burden enough to change the behaviour of the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

The fuse contributes as much as the shunt: a 600 mA fuse is about 1 Ω, and on a 50 mA range a roughly 2 Ω fuse dominates over a 1 Ω shunt, so reducing the shunt value alone does not fix the burden on ranges routed through a small fuse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

The standard remedy is a much lower shunt value followed by a precision amplifier. Dropping each shunt by an order of magnitude and adding a ×10 amplifier, or by two orders of magnitude with a ×100 amplifier, preserves the output scaling while cutting the burden proportionally; with ×100 gain, 10 mΩ, 10 Ω, and 10 kΩ shunts give 1 mV per mA, µA, and nA respectively with a 100× lower burden than a standard multimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> The amplifier's [[input-offset-voltage]] becomes the limiting error, which is why low-offset chopper amplifiers (e.g. the MAX4238/MAX4239 class, with offsets in the tens of microvolts) are used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> A design guideline is to take the largest tolerable shunt drop and the lowest amplifier gain, since pushing gain higher to compensate for a tiny shunt pushes the measurement down into the noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> The offset can coincide with the smallest signal of interest: 500 µA of resolution across a 0.2 Ω shunt is 100 µV, the same order as the input offset of a typical current-sense amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

## Shunts in multimeters

A typical handheld multimeter uses a 100 Ω shunt for the microamp ranges, a 1 Ω shunt for the milliamp ranges, and a 10 mΩ shunt for the 5 A/10 A ranges, selected by the [[range-switch]] and the input jack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Values are often combined so ranges sum to round numbers: a 0.99 Ω resistor in series with the 10 mΩ amp shunt gives a 1 Ω milliamp shunt, and the Fluke 27 uses a 4.995 Ω resistor in series with the 5 mΩ amp shunt to total exactly 5 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Manufacturers commonly mark low values with the "R" replacing the decimal point — 0R01 for 10 mΩ, R010, R100 for 0.1 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup>

The 10 A range is limited by shunt dissipation and self-heating, which is why meters often allow only a 20 A peak for about 20 seconds before a cool-down is required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Current accuracy is correspondingly hardest to maintain on the 10 A range, and datasheets typically show a worse current-accuracy figure there than on the lower ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Because shunts three orders of magnitude apart can be left in series or parallel without materially affecting each other, cascaded decade chains (10 mΩ, 100 mΩ, 1 Ω, 10 Ω, 100 Ω) can be [[mosfet]]-switched so that every range presents the same 50 mV burden with no amplifier needed, provided the sense amplifier's [[input-impedance]] is high enough that upstream resistors in the tap path drop no voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

## Protection

A fuse takes time to blow, and a gross overload can destroy the shunt resistor first. Multimeters therefore clamp the voltage across the current input with a [[bridge-rectifier|diode bridge]] — sometimes a bridge plus additional series diodes — limiting the shunt voltage to a few volts so the fuse clears first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup> In the Fluke 27 the clamp totals about 3.6 V, which across the 5 Ω milliamp shunt dissipates only about 2.5 W in a 4 W resistor, comfortably survivable for the fuse's clearing time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup> The same protection philosophy separates the current jack with isolation slots and HRC fuses in CAT-rated meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

## Construction, tolerance, and tempco

Shunt construction ranges from a bent length of nichrome resistance wire in an ordinary handheld meter, through copper straps soldered at an exact point to set the value, hand-wound and heat-shrunk elements in analog meters, and tapped or notched adjustment mechanisms, to precision four-terminal SMD parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> Absolute tolerance is often unimportant because instruments calibrate the gain out in software; 1% shunts are common even in precision gear, and what actually matters is low [[temperature-coefficient]] and low drift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Keeping self-heating low also keeps tempco errors low: paralleling ten resistors to share dissipation (e.g. ten 10 Ω parts for a 1 Ω shunt, giving 5–10 W capability) means even low-grade resistors barely change with temperature, and provides survival margin if range-switching software ever leaves the full output current on the small shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Precision low-value parts are disproportionately expensive: a 0.1% 10 mΩ shunt runs about $4–5 in thousand-piece volume, and 0.5% in a four-terminal package may be the best off-the-shelf option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

At milliohm values the shunt resistance is a significant fraction of [[pcb-trace]], switch, and [[contact-resistance]], so the voltage must be sensed directly at the resistor junctions — the four-terminal (Kelvin) connection, with dedicated sense taps on the element itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> Kelvin shunts and four-wire sense pairs appear in power supplies, [[soldering]] stations, electronic loads, and SMUs for exactly this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Specialised milliohm meters exist to verify such parts in or out of circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

## High-side versus low-side sensing

A shunt in the supply line (high-side) requires a differential or dedicated current-sense amplifier (e.g. the MAX4080 family with fixed gains of 5, 20, or 60) that tolerates the common-mode voltage and outputs a ground-referenced voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> A shunt in the ground return (low-side) is simpler to amplify but introduces a current-dependent offset between the load's ground and true ground, which is generally considered undesirable in power-supply outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> With 1 Ω shunts, a modest precision op-amp gives milliamp-class accuracy, but at 0.1 Ω general-purpose and even precision op-amps have too much offset and a proper instrumentation or current-sense amplifier is required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> Cheap differential-amplifier approaches built from jellybean op-amps are workable where cost dominates, as in some [[usb]] power supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

## In power supplies and constant-current circuits

The current-limit loop of a bench supply is an op-amp comparing the shunt voltage against a reference, driving the series pass [[transistor]] so the drop stays constant; the constant output current is then simply I = Vref ÷ Rshunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> A 1 V reference across a 1 Ω shunt regulates 1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The same trick turns an [[lm317]] into a constant-current source, since its 1.25 V internal reference divided by the series shunt sets the current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Practical shunts in the pass path are very low (10–100 mΩ) to avoid dissipation in normal operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Shunt voltage also eats regulator headroom: a 1 Ω shunt drops a volt per amp, which must be added to the pass element's dropout when sizing the pre-regulator tracking differential, though powering a regulator's control pin from the downstream side of the shunt can recover some margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup>

## Other measurement uses

- **In-circuit current checks**: measuring the voltage across an existing [[series-resistor]] (e.g. a 1 Ω current-limiting resistor) yields current without breaking the circuit — a standard troubleshooting shortcut.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup>
- **[[impedance]] and LCR analysis**: an impedance analyzer measures the generator voltage and the voltage across the device under test in series with a known shunt, deriving complex impedance from the two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup> Component curve tracers use the same principle — a [[function-generator]] through a series shunt with an XY [[oscilloscope]] display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup>
- **[[battery]] and energy measurement**: watt-hour and amp-hour capacity rigs place a shunt (usually low-side) with the load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>
- **Femto/picoamp measurement**: the same principle scales upward in resistance — a 250 GΩ shunt serves a 2 pA range, with decade-spaced shunts for each range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup>
- **Analog meters**: the lowest current range of an analog multimeter is the meter movement itself with no shunt; a 50 µA movement corresponds to the classic 20 kΩ/V sensitivity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Shunt resistors fail open under overload: a 1 Ω shunt that has taken several amps of fault current can measure kilohms afterwards, while the surrounding circuit survives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-233" title="EEVblog #233 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 6 - LT3080 Testing">233</a></sup> Deliberate protection design ([[diode]] clamps, adequate fuselet clearing, oversized or paralleled elements) exists precisely because the shunt is often the most thermally stressed precision part in the front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Intermittent or poor connections in current paths are likewise exposed by measuring across the shunt rather than breaking the loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>
