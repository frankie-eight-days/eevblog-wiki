High resolution mode, commonly called hi-res or boxcar averaging, is an acquisition mode on digital storage oscilloscopes that averages consecutive real-time samples within a single acquisition to produce each displayed sample point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Trading surplus [[sample-rate|sample rate]] for vertical bits, it turns an 8-bit front end into an effective 9, 10, 12 or even 14-bit converter and lowers the displayed [[noise-floor|noise floor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-223" title="EEVblog #223 - Agilent Oscilloscope High Res Mode">223</a></sup> It is the correct [[noise]]-reduction tool for single-shot, jittering or modulated signals, where conventional mathematical averaging fails outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup>

## Mechanism

The mode operates entirely inside one capture. It takes a single-shot acquisition in real time, collects a run of consecutive samples — ten or twelve, for instance — and averages them into one sample period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> Mathematical averaging mode works on a completely different axis: it takes multiple whole [[waveform]] captures, and with an average count of four takes the four data points at each position along the waveform and returns one averaged value per position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> The two are entirely different ways of averaging, and the distinction is subtle enough that it is routinely misunderstood.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup>

Because the averaging happens between samples rather than between acquisitions, the mode is effectively a moving-average low-pass filter applied to the acquisition record, which is what produces both its benefits and its principal trap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup>

## Sample rate is the raw material

High resolution mode consumes samples that would otherwise be discarded, so it can only work where the scope is sampling far faster than the [[time-base|timebase]] requires. At the fastest sweep speeds it does almost nothing, because there are not enough samples in the record for the boxcar function to operate on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> As the timebase is slowed, the effect grows progressively stronger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

This is why headroom well beyond the usual four-times-[[bandwidth]] rule is worth designing in. The Agilent 4000X uses its excess sample rate for a 12-bit high resolution mode on the analog front end, performing the boxcar averaging of those samples in real time; putting 5 GSa/s on a 200 MHz scope is a sound decision for exactly this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup> The resolution gained also depends on the bandwidth left available: a 12-bit Rigol HDO4000 in high res mode with bandwidth limited to 50 MHz reaches 14 or 16 bits, short of the 18 bits an MXO4 can produce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup>

## Resolution gained

Most digital scopes carry only eight-bit converters, but with high resolution mode enabled the effective resolution can rise to nine, ten, twelve, or as much as fourteen bits depending on sampling conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> The Rohde & Schwarz HMO1202 annotates the display with the resolution actually achieved, showing 10 bits when high resolution is switched on, and up to 16 bits when combined with its smoothing mode — from an 8-bit converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Bits beyond about eight cannot be seen on an ordinary scope screen; they become useful only through [[fft|FFT]] analysis, exported data, or on-screen math.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> The improvement in a math result is visible as reduced blockiness, since the calculation has more than eight bits to work with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Applied ahead of an FFT, the mode drops the displayed noise floor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup>

## Versus mathematical averaging

On a well-triggered repetitive waveform carrying random noise, the two modes can be nearly indistinguishable — the difference bordering on zero for that particular application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> The gap becomes stark as soon as the trigger is not stable. Averaging mode relies on repeatable triggering; on a [[square-wave|square wave]] with FM modulation, doubling the deviation from 10 kHz to 20 kHz changes the on-screen artifacts entirely, producing sampling and mathematical averaging artifacts that make the waveform look nothing like the real signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> High resolution mode shows none of that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup>

The resulting rule is straightforward. Averaging mode is for random noise on a stable trigger, where it works extremely well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> High resolution mode is the one to reach for with single-shot signals, or anything that is jittering, modulated, or otherwise not triggering repeatably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> High resolution mode is also the more forgiving of the two, lacking the gross failure mode of a forgotten averaging setting silently distorting a waveform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> A further practical constraint: averaging is unavailable at very slow timebases and does not function in roll mode at all, whereas high resolution mode remains available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>

## The trap at slow timebases

The mode's filtering is indiscriminate, and at expanded timebases it will quietly remove high-[[frequency]] noise superimposed on a low-frequency signal. A single-shot capture at 100 ms/div of two slow-changing signals appears entirely smooth with high res enabled, and only zooming in reveals the noise; on a 500 MHz scope the same capture in normal mode shows that noise plainly at the slowest timebase and identically when zoomed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-223" title="EEVblog #223 - Agilent Oscilloscope High Res Mode">223</a></sup> Anyone using high resolution mode at slow sweep speeds should confirm it is not averaging away content that matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-223" title="EEVblog #223 - Agilent Oscilloscope High Res Mode">223</a></sup>

The same behaviour is what makes the mode useful in the opposite direction. On a wide-bandwidth instrument the trace is thick and fuzzy at every timebase simply because the scope is showing real high-frequency content an analog scope would have averaged out on its phosphor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-223" title="EEVblog #223 - Agilent Oscilloscope High Res Mode">223</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> Switching to high resolution mode cleans that up markedly at the slower timebases, restoring an analog-like display of a [[power-supply|power supply]] [[ripple]] waveform on a digital scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> It is worth being aware of when the mode is on for exactly this reason: an apparently clean supply rail may reveal following noise once the mode is switched off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

## Practical applications

The mode is standard equipment for low-level and low-noise measurements:

- Measuring power supply ripple and noise, AC-coupled and bandwidth-limited, where it reproduces the analog-scope view of the waveform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup>
- Low-[[current-measurement|current measurement]] with a [[current]] probe, where a reading of 25.7 mA sits down in the noise and requires noise-reject triggering alongside the boxcar averaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup>
- Integration and energy calculation on a [[microcontroller]]'s power-up current waveform, where a cleaner, higher-fidelity record directly improves the accuracy of the computed result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup>
- Capturing current-draw signatures for analysis, such as the packet structure in an electronic safe lock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup>
- Long-record capture combined with a deep memory setting and a 20 MHz input [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]], so the data can be zoomed into afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-665" title="EEVblog #665 - Polar Wearlink Heart Rate Transmitter Teardown">665</a></sup>
- Resolving small reflections on a [[transmission-line|transmission line]] against a background of mains-frequency interference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup>
- High input sensitivity operation, such as 500 µV/div, where digitiser noise otherwise dominates and low-millivolt signals need to be resolved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup>
- Observing op-amp waveforms on a 1 GHz instrument, where full bandwidth in sample mode gives a fuzzy trace that regular acquisition mode is simply the wrong choice for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

## Nomenclature and availability

Vendors name the function inconsistently. Boxcar averaging, rolling average and moving average all describe the same operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Siglent labels it e-res, for enhanced resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Rohde & Schwarz pairs it with a separate smoothing mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> It is normally found in the acquire menu alongside normal, peak detect and average.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-223" title="EEVblog #223 - Agilent Oscilloscope High Res Mode">223</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup>

Modern digital scopes are generally expected to have it, including inexpensive models — a $399 Rigol DS1054Z includes it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup> Its absence is treated as a genuine deficiency: the GW Instek GDS-2000A and GDS-1000B both lack it, leaving only conventional averaging, and the Rigol MSO5000 omits it as its predecessor 7000 series did.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup>

Outside oscilloscopes, the same idea appears in bench multimeters. The HP 3457A, a six-and-a-half-digit meter, is technically capable of seven and a half digits with averaging in high resolution mode, but that resolution is reachable only over GPIB and not from the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup>
