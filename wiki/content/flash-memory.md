Flash memory is non-volatile solid-state storage that can be electrically erased and reprogrammed in blocks, holding its contents with the power removed. It is the technology that made the modern [[microcontroller]] what it is, because it put reprogrammable program storage on the same die as the CPU.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> Outside the microcontroller it serves as the [[firmware]] store for almost every piece of digital equipment — the chip that holds the operating system, the boot image, the [[calibration]] constants and the [[fpga|FPGA]] bitstream — and as bulk mass storage in everything from tablets to spacecraft.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tWW56LAnT1Y" title="Pluto: New Horizons Talk">•</a></sup>

Flash was developed in 1980 by Toshiba, but it was not until 1988 that Intel released its first NOR flash memory chip, and it took some years beyond that before the technology had displaced what came before.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-937" title="EEVblog #937 - Retro Canon Still Camera Teardown!">937</a></sup>

## What came before

Before cheap non-volatile memory existed, program and user data lived in volatile [[sram|SRAM]] kept alive by a [[battery-backup|backup battery]]. Pocket organisers, PDAs and portable computers of the 1980s were built this way: everything was held in SRAM because there was no flash to hold programs and data in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> A 1986-vintage machine might rely on nothing more than a pair of axial electrolytic caps — a 100 µF 40 V part among them — to hold the charge on the [[battery]]-backed SRAM, with no lithium cell on the board at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> The Apple Newton used five Mitsubishi 128k × 8 SRAMs for 640k total and stored nothing in flash, which is precisely why it needed battery backup; later versions of the Newton moved to flash.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-418" title="EEVblog #418 - Mailbag Apple Newton Teardown">418</a></sup> Machines with a memory-protect switch made the dependency explicit — switching the backup off lost the contents outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup>

For fixed program code the alternatives were mask [[rom|ROM]], UV-erasable EPROM and E²PROM. Mask ROM was the cheapest option because the die does not carry the expense of reprogrammable flash, and it remained attractive in high-volume consumer parts long after flash existed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup> EPROM required the familiar clear window in the package and an ultraviolet lamp to erase, which is why in-circuit emulators substituted fast volatile [[ram|RAM]] for the ROM rather than waiting on an erase-and-program cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> E²PROM allowed byte-level modification and enabled the early reprogrammable micros, but it never scaled to large capacities and was supplanted by flash within about five years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup>

## Flash in microcontrollers

Microchip moved from EPROM to E²PROM — the PIC16C84 being an E² product that could be byte-programmed a single byte at a time — and then introduced flash-based products around 1995, where the part is bulk-erased and bulk-programmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup> The 16F84 was essentially the first flash-programmable microcontroller on the market, and it changed the expectation of what a microcontroller is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> Other families followed with their own on-chip programming, removing the need to apply an external high-[[voltage]] pulse as the early 16C-series parts had required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup>

The three process technologies did not replace one another in the fab so much as accumulate: EPROM, E² and flash remained distinct technologies running in parallel on the same manufacturing floor, so mask sets for a 16C54, a 16C84 and a flash part could be started on successive days, or all on the same day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup>

Flash capacity is now one of the primary selection axes for a microcontroller, alongside I/O count and package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Representative figures across the range: 2K on an MSP430F2013 with 128 bytes of RAM but a 16-bit sigma-delta ADC;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> 8K on an ATmega8A<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> and on a low-end STM8S103;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup> 16K with 2K of SRAM on a 48 MHz RISC-V CH32V003;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1524" title="EEVblog 1524 - The 10 CENT RISC V Processor! CH32V003">1524</a></sup> 32K on a Cortex-M0 STM32F070 running at 48 MHz;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ag-MjKAfATw" title="Rode AI-1 USB Audio Interface Teardown">•</a></sup> 64K on the 8051 inside a 5100 W laboratory supply;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup> 128K on an NXP LPC2364 ARM7 and on a TI CC2530F128 ZigBee controller;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup> 256K with 64K of SRAM on an ultra-low-power Cortex-M4;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1336" title="EEVblog #1336 - DT71 LCR Tweezer Destructive Teardown + Lab Update">1336</a></sup> 1 MB on an STM32F103;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup> and 2 MB with 256K of RAM on the STM32F439.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup> Very small flash is not automatically disqualifying: C compiles perfectly well onto 16-bit PICs with only 1 or 2 kB of flash and around 300 bytes of SRAM given a good compiler.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-63" title="EEVblog #63 - Microchip PIC vs Atmel AVR">63</a></sup> At the bottom of the market, quoted headline prices such as 25 cents in volume typically buy a half-kilobyte flash part with no ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-96" title="EEVblog #96 - The TI LaunchPad MSP430 Development Board">96</a></sup>

Where reprogrammability is not needed, one-time-programmable parts remain cheaper still: three-cent microcontrollers are OTP, with only one or two models in the family carrying flash at all, and development is done with an in-circuit emulator before committing the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup> The same economics applied historically — a one-time-programmable CMOS PIC16C61 was cheaper than the flash F84, so the F84 was used for software development and the C part shipped in production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-C1on-LaIsCA" title="EEVblog #b10000000000 - 1K Micro Magic">•</a></sup>

## Capacity as a design constraint

Flash size is a hard limit that regularly forces a redesign. On the µSupply, a 20-pin TSOP STM32 with 32K of flash was chosen on the assumption that 32K was ample for [[usb-power-delivery|USB power delivery]] configuration, serial comms and HID.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> Both the Richtek and ST [[usb|USB]]-PD libraries turned out to be enormous — the ST one on the order of 2 megabits — and neither would fit alongside everything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> Working with the vendor's design engineers eventually pruned the Richtek library down enough to fit, but the fallback was a larger micro, and because the largest part in that TSOP package was the 32K device, moving to 128K meant moving to an entirely different package with prototype boards already built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> Estimating flash and SRAM requirements against candidate micros before committing is normal practice at the design-document stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

Where code executes from also matters for power. [[datasheet|Datasheet]] run-mode [[current]] is quoted separately for code executing from flash and code executing from RAM, and the flash figure is the one that applies to ordinary operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> Flash is also a natural DMA source: a bootloader can start a DMA transaction that moves an image from flash or external memory straight into program memory without running interrupts, which keeps the bootloader small, costs no CPU time and runs as fast as the part can manage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eUA2TRX1NTc" title="STM32 ARM Development - DMA &amp; ADC Discussion">•</a></sup>

## Configuration memory for programmable logic

SRAM-based FPGAs are volatile — they have no flash in them the way a microcontroller does, so a JTAG-programmed design vanishes the moment power is removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-635" title="EEVblog #635 - FPGA&#x27;s Vs Microcontrollers">635</a></sup> An external configuration flash is therefore mandatory for an FPGA to be useful in a product: typically a 4 or 8 megabit serial (or parallel) flash holding the state of every configurable interconnect, logic-block and I/O-block bit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> Even a small, cheap FPGA needs a comparatively large configuration flash because of the sheer number of bits to be programmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> On power-up the configuration logic loads the data from that flash and programs the fabric, which is why FPGAs are not instant-on and can take seconds to boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> A 4 megabit flash sitting next to a Spartan-3E is exactly this configuration store.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> Parts that boot instantly from internal configuration memory and need no external bitstream memory exist and remove the requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup> CPLDs with built-in flash likewise need no external configuration PROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup>

## Programming, erasing and updating

JTAG is not only a boundary-scan interface; on a modern microcontroller it is also the path used to program the built-in flash, talking to the on-chip flash controller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup> Universal programmers handle flash alongside ROM, non-volatile RAM and old-style EEPROMs, and some offer automatic detection of 8- or 16-pin flash devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup> Programming time scales with size — a 256K flash part takes a noticeable while — so verification is normally folded into the same operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-448" title="EEVblog #448 - New PICkit 4 &amp; AVR Dragon">448</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup> The standard confidence test on a recovered or reflashed device is to program the entire part and read it all back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup>

Field firmware updates follow a download-verify-erase-program-verify sequence, and a single device may hold several separately updatable firmware images for different processors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1151" title="EEVblog #1151 - Dumpster Dive Yamaha Receiver">1151</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> The erase and write phases are the point of maximum risk: interrupting an update in progress is how a working unit is lost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup>

Flash also serves as small-scale non-volatile configuration storage. Wi-Fi credentials entered once into an ESP8266 captive-portal setup are saved in flash and reused on every subsequent start-up, so the configuration page reappears only when those details fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yabl87SD-3M" title="Guest Video: Brian Lough - ESP8266 Libraries">•</a></sup> The same modules carry 4 megabytes of flash and enough RAM to run the application outright, making an external micro unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-998" title="EEVblog #998 - How To Program ESP8266 WiFi With Arduino">998</a></sup>

## Failure modes and reliability

Corrupted flash presents as a device that powers up and gets partway through boot but never runs. Where a bootloader clearly executes and reaches a boot screen but the application program never loads, the fault is localised to the flash holding that application rather than to the supply rails or the processor itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup> The same reasoning applies to a calibrated instrument that passed factory test and later stopped working: the leading explanation is flash inside the internal processor that was corrupted or incorrectly reprogrammed after calibration, since the calibration itself still measures correct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Marginal flash can be probed by thermal soaking — days at 60 °C is a harsh test, and a part that retains its contents through it is not marginal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup>

Long-term reliability is a genuine concern as cell geometries shrink. Multi-level cell flash is reported to distinguish its levels on the order of 20 electrons while operating at around one volt, which places device reliability, not just software quality, among the fundamental limits on how long a product remains functional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup>

## Consequences of non-removable flash

Because flash is the storage medium in modern portable and all-in-one machines, and because it is soldered down rather than socketed, its wear-out ends the life of the whole product. An all-in-one desktop made thin enough that neither the RAM nor the storage could be made upgradable becomes an aesthetically excellent piece of scrap the moment its flash memory fails, and designing for that outcome is treated as a design failure rather than an inevitability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k4zrmTsZj_s" title="Australia Right to Repair Summit - Session 1 of 4">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1407" title="EEVblog 1407 - Right to Repair with iFixit Founder Kyle Wiens">1407</a></sup> The reprogrammability of flash is also not always exposed to the owner: instruments whose manufacturers will not disclose the chipset, or will not say whether the memory is reprogrammable at all, cannot receive user firmware updates regardless of what the silicon supports.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>

## Beyond conventional equipment

The capacity and robustness of solid-state storage make it the recording medium where no other option exists. New Horizons collected roughly 5,000 times as much data at Pluto as the first missions to Mars and Venus, buffered all of it in large on-board flash memories, and then spent 16 months trickling it back to Earth over a deliberately low-rate radio link.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tWW56LAnT1Y" title="Pluto: New Horizons Talk">•</a></sup> The same pattern — FPGAs plus a large local flash — appears in instrumentation destined for places that cannot be serviced, such as optical sensor modules deployed in deep ice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1gfM_9EdLSs" title="Antarctic Neutrino Detector!">•</a></sup>
