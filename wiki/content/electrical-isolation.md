Electrical isolation is the deliberate absence of a conductive path between two parts of a system, so that energy or information crosses between them by some other means -- magnetic coupling, light, or a small deliberate [[capacitance]] -- while no [[current]] can flow directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup> In an isolation [[transformer]] the primary and secondary are wound in separate plastic bobbin sections and share only the laminated core, giving physical separation and therefore electrical separation between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> The consequence that matters is that an isolated output floats: it has no fixed relationship to [[mains-earth]], so touching one point of it does not complete a circuit through a person or through a grounded instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup>

Isolation is what makes a supply behave, from a safety point of view, exactly like a [[battery]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> A supply with electrical isolation and no earth connection at all is equivalent to a battery-powered one, whatever the topology behind it -- half-wave [[bridge-rectifier]], [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching power supply]], switching plug pack, or a conventional transformer-based plug pack all make no difference so long as the isolation barrier is intact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup>

## Isolation as a test-equipment feature

The defining selling point of a handheld [[oscilloscope]] is that its channels are isolated from one another: the BNC shells are not electrically connected, and on a fully isolated instrument the [[multimeter]] input is isolated from both scope channels as well, giving three independent front ends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> That independence is what carries the safety rating -- 300 V CAT III on the scope BNCs and 600 V CAT III on the multimeter inputs on one such instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Isolated channels are also what let a scope make high-side measurements: an older isolated mini-lab could measure across a primary, a secondary and the high side simultaneously at up to 1,000 V isolation, 660 V RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup> Purpose-built isolated scopes push this further, with a kilovolt of isolation between each channel, the other channels and ground on one four-channel design,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> and 2 kV per channel at 200 MHz [[bandwidth]] on a later one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1753" title="EEVblog 1753 - Designing a 2000V Isolated Oscilloscope (Cleverscope)">1753</a></sup> The practical payoff is probing the innards of a live motor drive -- gate drive and [[transistor]] saturation [[voltage]] on a rail swinging 320 volts -- without shorting anything through the scope's earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup>

Where two isolated channels exist, they allow two points of a circuit to be probed at the same time without either measurement disturbing the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Isolation must extend to the accessories: isolated probes with a full plastic shroud surrounding the BNC prevent accidental contact with the connector when it is plugged in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup>

Not every probe is isolated, and some are conspicuously not. A high-voltage differential probe with a consistent 4 megohms measured between either input terminal and the output ground is not isolated -- the [[resistance]] is the same on both inputs and does not change when the leads are swapped, which rules out active circuitry behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> An active FET probe with a ±25 V input range is isolated neither from its [[usb]] connection nor from its output, and is not intended to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>

## The USB port as the isolation problem

Adding a USB socket to a handheld meter creates the safety question that dominates its design: whether the port's ground is isolated from the measurement common.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> A meter measuring 5.8 kilohms between USB ground and the input common has no isolation and will not pass a safety standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> The compliance question is unavoidable for any battery-powered process meter or multimeter that exposes an external USB connector -- the port has to be isolated for the product to be certifiable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> A cheap USB-C-equipped meter without that isolation is an exposure risk in normal handheld use, and proper USB electrical isolation is expensive enough that its absence is a reasonable default assumption on very low-cost gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup>

When the isolation is present it is worth having for reasons beyond safety: an isolated USB-C port can be left permanently connected to a computer for [[data-logging]] while the meter is in use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Measurement distinguishes the cases -- one instrument shows genuine isolation between the multimeter common and the [[function-generator]] and oscilloscope grounds, and at least megohms to the USB-C port, while its oscilloscope ground remains connected straight through to USB ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> An $80 scope-meter with the scope ground electrically isolated from the multimeter common is acceptable from a safety point of view at that price, and the useful test is the breakdown voltage of the common jack relative to the isolated scope input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup> A USB-C-powered bench scope is only technically isolated: 1 megohm from earth to the shield through a bleeder rather than a direct connection, which is why such an instrument ships with a low-[[impedance]] earth lead for the chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup>

Isolation is also lost by accident through power connections. Adding 5 V USB charging to an otherwise isolated instrument breaks the isolation the moment it is powered from a non-isolated 5 V source such as a PC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Conversely, isolation designed into an instrument is wasted effort when the source is already floating: a supply built as a completely isolated design so it could be run from anything is crippled for no reason if it is normally run from a battery pack, which is isolated already.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MecbPOPeovs" title="Whatever Happened to the uSupply Project?">•</a></sup> Isolation costs output power as well -- the fully isolated version of that design managed only five to ten watts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MecbPOPeovs" title="Whatever Happened to the uSupply Project?">•</a></sup>

## Interfaces across the barrier

Instruments are routinely split into an isolated analog measurement section and a digital processing section, with the split visible in the layout as a break in the ground planes and an absence of traces across it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> A bench multimeter's digital and display section sits electrically isolated from the analog measurement part below, with a shield over the boundary and optocouplers as the only crossing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup> The same split appears in a programmable [[power-supply]], where the Ethernet, USB, RS-232 and logic output grounds are all electrically isolated from the supply outputs, with optocouplers carrying serial data across and a separate small supply for the isolated side derived from extra taps on the mains transformer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> Output circuitry in a distortion analyser is likewise kept totally electrically isolated from the generation circuitry, with a [[serial-port|serial interface]] crossing through optical isolators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup>

The crossing mechanisms are few. Optocouplers, sometimes visible as a discrete infrared [[led]] and infrared phototransistor pair facing each other across the barrier, carry signals;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1489" title="EEVblog 1489 - Mystery Teardown!">1489</a></sup> modern instruments use packaged digital isolators for the same job, with further isolation between the serial and GPIB interfaces and the analog section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> An unusual variant pairs an LED with a CdS photocell rather than a phototransistor, which provides isolation and was also used as the stabilising element in precision oscillators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-709" title="EEVblog #709 - EDC 4601 AC Voltage Standard Teardown">709</a></sup> Power crosses through isolated DC-to-DC converter modules -- the transformer inside is what provides the isolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MecbPOPeovs" title="Whatever Happened to the uSupply Project?">•</a></sup> These are used even where the isolation is not strictly needed: a current probe whose sensing head makes no electrical contact with the circuit still uses an isolated ±5 V DC-to-DC converter for its supply, purely as belt and braces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> Where isolation must be kept, a separate board carrying an isolated USB-to-serial interface, or a wireless link, keeps a controlling computer's earth reference out of the instrument's grounds -- rear-panel connectors referenced to a computer that is referenced to mains earth cause major problems in a power supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

## Layout, clearance and construction

Isolation is enforced physically as much as electrically. An isolated section shows up on a board as a visible routed path or gap around the region, with the [[ground-plane]] starting on one side and absent on the other so the mains area is totally separate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-164" title="EEVblog #164 - Agilent Fly To The Moon">164</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Slots milled through the board increase the creepage distance; isolation slots between input divider resistors are what let a differential probe meet its voltage rating, alongside custom sealed plastic enclosures giving complete separation between the two input paths.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> The same technique appears at very low currents for the opposite reason: generous isolation, including a slot around a free-standing low-leakage reed [[relay]], keeps leakage current out of a sensitive node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> At the extreme, components in an electrometer front end are mounted on Teflon standoffs and must not touch the [[pcb]] or other components at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup>

A multimeter input path with no traces at all around the input sockets -- the connection going straight through -- is about as good as isolation gets, when there is board area to spare for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup> Good practice extends to mechanical detail: a rubber insulating mat inside a transformer prevents sharp metalwork from piercing the winding wrap and lowering its electrical isolation,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> and stick-on insulating feet stop a board shorting to raised pins behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup> Bad practice is equally visible. A mains flyback supply whose outputs are isolated from the mains can still have inadequate clearance around individual pads, which is unlikely to meet any standard;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup> a mains switch-mode section with a DIP switch sitting next to the mains input filter capacitors shows no real thought given to separating low voltage from the mains side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup> In quality meters the high-voltage [[input-protection]] -- HRC fuses, spark gaps, high-voltage axial resistors and a PTC -- is itself isolated from the main electronics, with a long path around the barrier before anything reaches the inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15" title="EEVblog #15 Part 1 of 2 - Fluke 189/289 multimeter review">15</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup>

## Isolating heat from electricity

A large class of isolation problems is thermal rather than electrical in origin: the metal tab of a power device is usually a live pin, so any attempt to use metalwork to cool it must break the electrical connection while keeping the thermal one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Because a device's tab is typically V out, V in or some other electrical node rather than ground, it cannot simply be [[via]]-stitched to a ground or power plane -- doing so shorts it out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> This is the reason sil pads exist and why insulation is used on the majority of thermal devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> A mica washer or sil pad between the tab and the case or [[heat-sink|heatsink]] electrically isolates the device while still transferring heat, at the cost of some thermal performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup> Plastic spacers holding a heatsink down serve the same purpose, with a sil pad underneath transferring heat while leaving the heatsink electrically isolated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Where multiple devices share a heatsink they generally need isolating from each other as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup>

The mounting hardware is a trap of its own: screws are metal, so a board needs a clear isolation ring around each screw hole to stop the screw from touching copper it should not touch, while still allowing enough clamping pressure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> The problem can also be designed away. If a free-standing TO220 has enough clearance to the enclosure that they cannot touch, no insulating washer is needed at all;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-644" title="EEVblog #644 - How To Design Front Panels On Extruded Enclosures - µSupply Part 14">644</a></sup> equally, a device whose thermal tab is isolated internally, or genuinely grounded, removes the conflict entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> SMD thermal jumpers address the same trade-off in surface mount: they look like ordinary chip resistors in various aspect ratios, contain no resistance and are electrically isolated, but conduct heat, using the standard footprint for their package size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> The alternative -- devoting a large isolated copper area on the top or bottom layer as a dedicated heatsink for one device -- works thermally but consumes routing area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup>

## Medical, industrial and mains-side isolation

Isolation requirements scale with what is on the other side of the barrier. Equipment connected to a person is built around it: a medical-grade isolation transformer, complete ground separation, and high-value high-voltage resistors in series with each electrode lead make it safe to connect leads to both wrists.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup> An ultrasound machine's ECG front end has its own isolated analog ground plane, a large routed gap, an isolation amplifier and a DC-to-DC converter across a barrier rated on the order of 2.5 kV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup>

At mains potential the barrier is often the whole architecture. A hybrid solar [[inverter]] isolates the house side so that when the grid fails the inverter does not feed power back into the grid, automatically isolating the grid side while continuing to run an emergency output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5JT8M8v_zNg" title="EcoFlow Delta Pro - RTFM Dummy!">•</a></sup> In such an inverter the battery, the PV input and the [[boost-converter]] share a bus but sit behind isolation, so the house cannot tell and does not care whether it is being supplied from the battery or from the array.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> Modems have always had to provide isolation from the telephone line, and the requirements have become considerably stricter over time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup>

Not every product needs it, and pretending otherwise is worse than omitting it. A very cheap 2 kW supply with its output shunt directly connected is simply not isolated -- unbeatable for high voltage, high current and high power cheaply, but not a candidate where an isolated supply is required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Its auxiliary [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] is described as being "isolated in quote marks", sitting on one big flood fill with no specification given, useful only for interface purposes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Where isolated rails matter, as on a controller board accepting external power, they are worth providing so that arbitrary non-isolated rails cannot be plugged in and break things.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-843" title="EEVblog #843 - David&#x27;s rPrint 3D Printer Design">843</a></sup>

## Verifying isolation

Isolation is a claim that is cheap to test and should be tested. A continuity or [[resistance-measurement]] between the connectors in question settles it: a panel meter whose common measures open to the supply ground is confirmed floating,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9lIC3ZzIht4" title="Junk box Digital Panel Meter Analysis CX101">•</a></sup> and probing between BNC shells on a handheld scope demonstrates channel-to-channel isolation directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Resistance also exposes the failures -- 5.8 kilohms across a supposed barrier,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> or a constant 4 megohms from input to output ground regardless of which terminal is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> For high-voltage barriers a megohmmeter applies a realistic test voltage, chosen below the device's own maximum rating so the test does not destroy what it is measuring.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup>

Once everything in a setup is genuinely floating, ordinary bench prohibitions relax: it becomes possible to solder on live equipment, provided the whole system including the [[soldering-iron]] is confirmed floating and the iron is not earthed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup>
