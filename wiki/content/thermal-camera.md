A thermal camera is an infrared imaging instrument that builds a temperature map of a scene rather than reporting a single number: functionally it is a large array of point thermometers, each pixel behaving like an individual infrared spot measurement, assembled into a picture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-403" title="EEVblog #403 - IR Thermometer Calibration Testing">403</a></sup> For electronics work this converts [[temperature-measurement|thermal measurement]] from a serial, contact-based chore into a single glance — an overall thermal map of a product plus individual component temperatures, without attaching a [[thermocouple]] to anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> It is a general-purpose diagnostic instrument, useful for locating faults, characterising thermal design, and verifying that a product behaves as advertised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup>

## Resolution, sensor arrays and instrument classes

Detector array size is the dominant specification. A FLIR E60 with a 320 by 240 detector represented roughly $12,000 of handheld instrument and the top of that manufacturer's E-series at the time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-403" title="EEVblog #403 - IR Thermometer Calibration Testing">403</a></sup> The E8 offers a 320 by 200 array.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> The bench-mounted ETS320 combines a 320 by 240 screen with a stand that fixes the camera above a board, and can measure up to 260 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-989" title="EEVblog #989 - FLIR ETS320 Thermal Camera Teardown">989</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> At the low end, the Lepton core used in cheaper products is 80 by 60.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup>

Resolution is not merely a matter of image quality; it determines whether a fault can be found at all. A single-spot infrared thermometer is inadequate for tracing a fault distributed across a plane, where the temperature is fairly evenly spread and detecting the anomaly is close to a needle in a haystack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Conversely, resolving individual SMD components for design, manufacture, repair and rework is the specific capability that makes a high-resolution imager useful in electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup>

A larger array does not automatically produce a visibly better image — a comparison between a high-end camera and a low-cost unit with a much smaller detector produced images that were not that different — so the array size alone is not the whole story.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-403" title="EEVblog #403 - IR Thermometer Calibration Testing">403</a></sup> Optics matter too: getting a usable reading on something as small as a [[via]] requires a good close-up macro lens, and without one a measurement of a 2.2 °C rise is not the most accurate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup>

## Visual thermometers versus true thermal cameras

A distinct product class sits between the infrared spot thermometer and the true imager. The FLIR TG165 contains the same 80 by 60 Lepton core used in the smartphone-attachment cameras, but the temperature it reports does not come from that core at all — it is taken from a pyroelectric spot sensor behind the front window, laser-targeted, and the Lepton image serves only as a visual indication of where the spot is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup> Because the reading is not derived from the array, there is no scale on the image and the core needs no [[calibration]]; the device looks like a thermal camera but functions as a spot thermometer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup> Its advantage over a full camera is simplicity for users who only want to point and measure a temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-668" title="EEVblog #668 - Mailbag + FLIR TG165">668</a></sup>

The same distinction shows in the calibration hardware. A proper thermal camera carries a large internal shutter — an aluminium plate painted matte black with a [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]] embedded directly on it, so its uniform temperature is known precisely — whereas a cheaper instrument's shutter may be plastic with no sensor on it, which limits how good the calibration can be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup>

Sensor and lens quality separates products more sharply than marketing does. In a side-by-side test against a FLIR i3 using a half-matte-black can of hot water, the Fluke VT02 resolved a human figure only as a blob, its performance judged the worst seen in a thermal camera.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-O3LP2entyNQ" title="Fluke VT02 Visual Thermometer vs Flir i3 Thermal Camera">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-668" title="EEVblog #668 - Mailbag + FLIR TG165">668</a></sup> A secondary ergonomic complaint applies to that design: the screen sits at one angle and the sensor at another, forcing the user to mentally offset the aim point, whereas keeping the screen parallel to the sensor avoids the problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-O3LP2entyNQ" title="Fluke VT02 Visual Thermometer vs Flir i3 Thermal Camera">•</a></sup>

## Emissivity and reflection — the principal measurement traps

Thermal cameras measure emitted infrared, so the surface finish of the target governs the reading. Matte black gives an emissivity of about 0.95 or thereabouts, and the camera itself has to be configured with an emissivity figure to convert radiance to temperature; a typical working setting is 0.95.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-O3LP2entyNQ" title="Fluke VT02 Visual Thermometer vs Flir i3 Thermal Camera">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup> Bright shiny metal cans, clear anodised aluminium heat sinks and shiny [[solder]] pads all violate that assumption and produce readings that are simply wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> The standard workaround is a piece of black electrical tape on the surface of interest — not the best heat transfer, but it establishes a known emissivity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup> Where that is impractical, a thermocouple on the part is the better instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

Reflection is the second trap and the more deceptive one, because a reflected source appears as a genuine temperature difference. Shiny solder pads showing as a different temperature are reflecting, not running hot; a hand brought near shiny BNC connectors changes the apparent temperature of the metal; and overhead lighting can produce a reflection that has to be worked around by moving the camera.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3zAWgNjx5Ew" title="Intel MCS-85 Design Kit THERMAL IMAGING">•</a></sup> Anything nearby can reflect off a target, though cameras provide a reflected-temperature setting to compensate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-989" title="EEVblog #989 - FLIR ETS320 Thermal Camera Teardown">989</a></sup> Knowing the reflective properties of what is being viewed is a prerequisite to trusting the image — it is described as "a real trap for young players".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

Absolute accuracy should be treated with suspicion in the absence of a calibration check, and the camera itself is generally not calibrated by its user.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-403" title="EEVblog #403 - IR Thermometer Calibration Testing">403</a></sup> Where a survey across a heated plate read 55.2, 55.3, 55.8 and 56.4 °C at different points, the spread was real and located the heater in one corner, but the underlying calibration remained unverified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-403" title="EEVblog #403 - IR Thermometer Calibration Testing">403</a></sup> Neglecting emissivity means the number is indicative rather than exact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup>

## Fault finding

The core diagnostic use is locating a component that is dissipating power it should not. On boards where a rail is drawing excessive [[current]], a failed part with a low-[[impedance]] or latch-up fault shows a thermal difference against its neighbours, and the whole board can be surveyed before a probe is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup> This replaces the traditional method of wetting the back of a finger and touching each device in turn — a method still used in parallel, and one that has the merit of working where the camera has no line of sight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> Access is the practical constraint: probing a board in a chassis may require extender cards before either method is possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>

The camera is equally valuable for negative results. On a LeCroy 9384C drawing roughly 11 A where 6 A was the maximum expected, a $12,000 imager found no hot spot anywhere on the board; the power was dissipated uniformly across four ASICs, and since a specific short could not be localised the board was judged unrepairable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> The same survey ruled out a suspected mounting hole and showed the [[connector]] wiring heating from current flow with no hot spot at the hole, contradicting a widely held theory about the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> On an HP 35670A, the survey found the op amps at data-sheet quiescent currents and the [[asic|ASIC]] at about 55 °C — nothing scorching or out of the ordinary — which cleared them as suspects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>

Some faults are too small to resolve. A shorted SOT-23 hidden under a component produced nothing obvious on the image, even though significant current through it should have shown as a hot spot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> Conversely, gross failures are unmistakable: on a Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 board, the Ethernet chip sat at 70 °C and the DC-DC converter at over 80 °C, both too hot to touch, with the chip staying hot after power-off — while the [[processor]] drawing six watts barely got warm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup>

## Thermal characterisation and design verification

Because heat sinks are vastly more efficient with air flowing over them and are inefficient in still air, meaningful thermal performance must be measured with the lid on and all ducting in place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> The obstacle is that a closed case is opaque to infrared. Cling wrap solves this: it is both visually and thermally transparent, so a product can be sealed with it and imaged through the covering while retaining essentially correct [[airflow]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-623" title="EEVblog #623 - See Through Thermal Camera Followup">623</a></sup> The penalty is a 4 to 5 degree drop in indicated temperature, which can be compensated; the heat map and its shape are not distorted, individual chips and energised [[relay]] coils remain visible, and with luck the airflow itself can be seen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Not all visually transparent materials behave this way — transparency to visible light does not imply transparency in the infrared.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-623" title="EEVblog #623 - See Through Thermal Camera Followup">623</a></sup>

Typical characterisation results include an 80 W [[led|LED]] light settling at 45 °C in the centre with the case bottom not much cooler, matching an advertised 20 °C rise above a roughly 24 °C ambient;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup> an LED strap light whose hottest parts were the metal strips between sections at 45 to 47 °C, with the anodised [[heat-sink|heat sink]] backing in the 40s;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup> a bicycle light reaching 57 °C on the bench at 1500 lumens, acceptable because riding provides airflow;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gTTsvYbQOT4" title="Olight RN1500 Bicycle Bike Light REVIEW + TEST">•</a></sup> a fast [[battery]] charger at 55 °C after a couple of minutes with the diodes hottest;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> a PoE hat whose secondary-side output [[diode]] exceeded 110 °C at rated 12.5 W output;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup> a [[boost-converter|boost converter]] whose [[inductor]] reached 100 °C dissipating 7.6 W;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup> and a mains LED driver whose hot spot was its rectifier diode at 74 °C and still rising.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup>

Component derating work uses the same technique. [[ripple|Ripple]]-current heating in electrolytic capacitors can be calculated, but empirical measurement on prototypes with a thermocouple and a thermal camera is the usual practice, and the comparison between one [[capacitor]] and ten paralleled units of the same total [[capacitance]] — the latter having far greater surface area — is directly visible as a temperature difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> The result feeds a decision about whether the capacitors will be the reliability weak point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

Where a device barely warms, the camera can be skipped; where a load test cannot reach the rated point because the available electronic loads top out below the required [[voltage]], the thermal test cannot be performed at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1263" title="EEVblog #1263 - Mailbag">1263</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup>

## Interpretation thresholds

Human touch supplies the calibration for what matters. Around 60 °C and above the reflex is to pull the finger away, so anything that cannot be held is in that region; roughly 50 °C is the practical too-hot-to-touch boundary used as a design ceiling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3zAWgNjx5Ew" title="Intel MCS-85 Design Kit THERMAL IMAGING">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup> A differential is often more informative than an absolute: one heat sink running 3 or 4 degrees hotter than four identical ones alongside it points at the device on it having lower [[resistance]] than the others.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Similarly, cable runs sitting about 10 degrees above ambient under load, with the bends measurably warmer, is a normal and interpretable pattern rather than a fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-31gkF0q9VfE" title="Zappi EV Charger &amp; Cable Thermal Imaging">•</a></sup>

## Applications beyond board-level debugging

Electrical installations are surveyed for hot joints — every junction is a potential failure point, and a scan every six months is a reasonable maintenance interval.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1651" title="EEVblog 1651 - Home Electrical Switchboard UPGRADE!">1651</a></sup> Fixed wiring for high-current loads such as EV charging can be checked in situ at full charge rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EYx46kRv2Bw" title="Myenergi Zappi 7kW EV Charger Installation &amp; Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-31gkF0q9VfE" title="Zappi EV Charger &amp; Cable Thermal Imaging">•</a></sup> [[solar-power-system|Photovoltaic]] arrays develop hot spots where a shaded cell, still carrying the series string current, is forced into reverse and dissipates power instead of generating it; these are detectable with a thermal camera on the panels and degrade individual cell life, with a broken internal connection taking the whole string out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> Panel and connector inspection is limited in practice by physical access to the array.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I7wMkLJgnko" title="Home Solar Power System Re-install is now completely working!">•</a></sup>

Electronic keypad locks leak information thermally: pressing the buttons leaves a thermal signature that persists for a considerable time, and the order of pressing can be recovered from how quickly each mark fades, narrowing the code space substantially.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1509" title="EEVblog 1509 - The Art of High Security Safe Locks (TEARDOWNS)">1509</a></sup> The availability of phone-attachment thermal cameras makes this a practical attack in public environments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1509" title="EEVblog 1509 - The Art of High Security Safe Locks (TEARDOWNS)">1509</a></sup>
