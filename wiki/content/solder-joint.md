A solder joint is the fused metal connection between a component lead or termination and a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] pad, and it is simultaneously an electrical path and a structural one. [[solder|Solder]]'s primary job is to be a mechanical connection — a solid mechanical connection for components — and construction techniques such as right-angled board-to-board assemblies rely on the rigidity a joint provides rather than on any separate fastener.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1263" title="EEVblog #1263 - Mailbag">1263</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup> Because the same small volume of alloy carries both the [[current]] and the load, a joint that is mechanically compromised is an electrical fault, and the mechanical failure mode is the more insidious of the two: it is intermittent, temperature-dependent, and can lie dormant for decades before it surfaces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup>

## Anatomy of a good joint

A good-quality joint made with standard leaded solder is bright, shiny, smooth, and drawn into a clean concave fillet where the lead meets the pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> The same criterion applies regardless of package: through-hole pins, chip resistors, SO and QFP leads, and a wire seated into a solder cup are all judged by the same shiny surface and even fillet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> A joint that has been correctly executed uses a surprisingly small quantity of solder — close to the minimum needed to form the fillet — and well-made assembly work shows a perfect amount of solder and perfect fillets across every passive on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup>

The failure of that standard is the cold joint. It appears as a ball or a blob rather than a fillet, with a craggy, messy, dull surface, and typically has not wetted or flowed through to the far side of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup> Excess solder produces the same visual signature — a big fat blob with no visible fillet — and is "a dead giveaway for amateurs".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> On a plated-through hole, an absent fillet on the underside means current must travel through the thin plated walls and around the edge of the pad rather than through a proper connection, which is a defect even when the joint appears to work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-U_QoWa6qXeo" title="EEVsmoke #1 - Magic Component Smoke">•</a></sup>

The shiny-joint criterion is specific to leaded alloys. In a lead-free process, every joint looks frosted under the same inspection conditions that would demand a perfectly mirror finish from leaded work, so the traditional visual standard cannot be carried across; lead-free and leaded work are different types of [[soldering]], and tools are kept segregated between the two stations to avoid cross-contamination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1041" title="EEVblog #1041 - Keysight Calibration Lab Tour">1041</a></sup>

## Making the joint

The iron tip must contact the pad and the lead at the same time, with the chisel face flat against the board for maximum contact; a joint that refuses to heat is usually a joint the tip is not sitting flat on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup> Solder is fed onto the far side of the joint, not onto the tip: solder placed on the tip and carried across burns off its [[flux]] on the way, producing a cold joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup> Feeding a consistent amount onto each joint, and leaving the iron on the joint for a moment before starting to feed, gives repeatable fillets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup>

Solder diameter is the dominant control variable. The biggest mistake beginners make is feeding too much solder onto the joint, and thick 1 mm wire makes the quantity very difficult to control; 0.46 mm is a working default, with anything at or below 0.5 mm (0.02 in) recommended, and heavier wire kept on hand only for large joints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> Fine solder is what makes controlled surface-mount work possible at all, and it is a large part of why some people's joints look better than others'.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Flux is the other half — a flux pen applied before the iron — and additional liquid or gel flux helps on stubborn or large-mass joints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-433" title="EEVblog #433 - Mailbag">433</a></sup>

[[thermal-mass|Thermal mass]], not nominal iron temperature, sets the difficulty. A large component or a pad tied directly into a [[ground-plane|ground plane]] sinks heat away and takes several seconds to come up, sometimes requiring solder fed toward the iron to establish thermal contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Iron capability shows up directly in the dial setting needed: a low-capacity station has to be run at 400 °C to flow a heavy joint properly and quickly, where a high-capacity unit does the same joint at 270 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup> Joints on high-thermal-mass parts stay hot well after the iron is removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

For [[surface-mount-technology|surface mount]], the dab method — solder carried on the tip and touched to each lead in turn — applies no lateral force to the pins and deposits close to the right amount for a fillet on each one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> Drag soldering across a row of fine-pitch leads works on packages down to quad flat packs, sometimes requiring two passes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> At 0.4 mm pitch, inspection gear is not optional: the work cannot be done reliably without viewing it afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-408" title="EEVBlog #408 - Schmart Board 0.4mm QFN SMD Soldering">408</a></sup> A jeweller's loupe or inspection microscope is used to confirm the fillets and to check for shorts and solder splash between pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Where joints are uncertain, systematically reheating them will pull solder further up the lead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

## Through-hole wetting and lead trimming

On through-hole work, the solder must wet all the way around the lead and, with an adequately heated joint, will wick through to the top side of the board even from a small feed of 0.46 mm wire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Checking the opposite side is part of the process, because heat applied from one side may wet only that side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Absent flow-through is a recognised long-term defect: joints on aged equipment that never had adequate solder flow-through can crack completely decades later, and reheating such a joint may reveal that the original solder never flowed through the [[via]] at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1572" title="EEVblog 1572 - Haefely 16kV ESD Tester TEARDOWN + Repair Part 1">1572</a></sup>

Trimming leads is where an otherwise sound joint is destroyed. Cutters should be brought in just above the surface of the joint rather than down onto it, angled slightly, and never used to shear several leads at once, because the shock puts stress on the joint and cracks it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> Cutting into the fillet works in practice and is sometimes unavoidable — boards are occasionally [[ground]] flat for enclosure clearance — but it is not best practice, because it introduces stress fractures that cannot be counted on to stay benign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Non-flush cutters exist for exactly this reason, though flush cutters plus a deliberate standoff distance achieve the same end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CLD5NsCJdpQ" title="What are the BEST Electronics Side Cutters?">•</a></sup> Similarly, through-hole pins left protruding under a part that must sit flat will crack their joints when the part is forced down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1241" title="EEVblog #1241 - Power Up Display Counter Project - Part 1">1241</a></sup>

## Mechanical and thermal failure

Solder is brittle enough that some ultra-high-reliability industries avoid it entirely, using press-fit connectors instead, precisely because solder joints can crack under stress and vibration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup> On an electrodynamic shaker a TO-220 regulator can be sheared completely off its solder joints — an accelerated version of a failure that otherwise plays out over months.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> Connectors are the usual stress concentrators: repeated insertion loads [[usb|USB]] ports' joints heavily, a spring-loaded socket puts continuing stress on its joints, and a mechanically loaded [[connector]] that can be felt wobbling is a strong indication of a failed joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-234" title="EEVblog #234 - Agilent U1733C LCR Meter Teardown">234</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1727" title="EEVblog 1727 - REPAIR: Casio CT-X700 Keyboard">1727</a></sup> Good practice removes the load before it reaches the metal — anchoring wires with tape so no flexion or vibration reaches the joint and causes fatigue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-395" title="EEVblog #395 - World&#x27;s Most Expensive Hard Drive Teardown">395</a></sup> Board handling matters too: a board should be flat against the bench when chips are reseated so it is not flexed and its joints not cracked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup>

Thermal cycling is the other major mechanism. A classic thermal fault is a dry joint that opens as the component heats and expands, most often on large through-hole power components; over thirty-five years of powering up and down, expansion and contraction produce a micro-crack in the joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> Cracked joints around parts that get hot are a common enough failure mode that visual inspection of the joints is a standard early step on any heat-cycled supply, and equally a reason to suspect microcracks on thermally active parts even when the rails all measure correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Failed joints of this kind have been found on power-amplifier pins that cracked over years of heating and cooling, on an output choke, and on an AC mains connection where the crack produced audible arcing to the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1091" title="EEVblog #1091 - LED Studio Light Repair">1091</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup> Sufficient overheating simply melts joints outright, at which point components such as diodes fall off under gravity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup>

## Diagnosis

Visual inspection is the first tool and the least trustworthy. Light and shadow at particular angles will make a perfectly sound joint look dry, and a joint that looks poor on a high-definition image is very often fine; conversely, some joints cannot be inspected at all until the board is removed, so the initial evidence has to come from measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> A 3D stereo microscope, which allows the head to move and view the joint from varying angles, resolves far more about a joint than a flat digital image at high magnification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup> Where doubt remains after inspection, reflowing the suspect joint as a matter of course is the cheap resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup>

Physical provocation supplements inspection: wiggling suspect joints, flexing the board, and applying local pressure while watching a measurement can expose an intermittent, and freezer spray or deliberate heating is used to force thermally-dependent joints to show themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1091" title="EEVblog #1091 - LED Studio Light Repair">1091</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> A fault that reproduces on touch but resets identically on every power cycle is electronic rather than a mechanical joint fault — [[capacitive-coupling|capacitive coupling]] from a finger, not a cracked connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

Probing technique can itself hide a bad joint. Where a lead is not properly soldered, probing the pad shows the signal and probing the top of the pin does not; pressing hard enough on the pin makes contact and restores the signal, disguising the defect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Probe pressure must be enough to penetrate the oxide coating on the joint, which is why sharp probes matter for low-[[resistance]] measurements and why sprung probe holders are weighted to sink slightly into the joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup> Thermal imaging has its own trap: shiny joints and metal cans reflect heat, producing hot spots that are reflections rather than dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

## Production processes

In volume manufacturing, joint quality is a yield metric. Moisture-sensitive parts that are not baked before placement cause a range of solder joint problems that ruin yield.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> The reflow profile matters on both slopes — cooling too slowly lets the solder sit in its plastic region and produces dry joints — and heavy copper aggravates it further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Individual joints that fail to reflow are traceable to a large thermal mass on the pad reaching insufficient temperature, or to the flux in the paste failing to clean the joint properly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup> Automated optical inspection examines the individual solder joints alongside component identification and lead-bank short detection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>

Process leaves signatures. Solder dragged up at the end of a pad indicates a hand drag-soldered job rather than reflow, and thieving pads on the larger terminations indicate [[reflow-soldering|reflow soldering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-582" title="EEVblog #582 - HP41CV Calculator Teardown">582</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup> Boards frequently show a mixture, with hand rework after [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]] visible in the finish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup> Alloy composition also determines long-term robustness: in one oil-filled system the composition of the solder on the joints made them brittle enough that chips could be pushed off by hand without heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2G7Z9IIQoIs" title="IBM BGA Thermal Futility...">•</a></sup>

Bulk reflow is sometimes attempted as a repair on suspected cracked [[bga|BGA]] joints, using a toaster oven at around 230 °C or hot air over a specific device, on the reasoning that any bad joints will reflow. The technique sometimes changes the symptoms but is unreliable as a fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1154" title="EEVblog #1154 - Surprising 4K Dumpster TV Fault">1154</a></sup>

## Electrical properties

A solder joint is not a zero-resistance connection. Joints have resistance, as do PCB traces and component leads, and all of them dissipate I²R losses when current passes through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> In high-power applications those connection losses — lead resistance plus joint resistance — become a real heating contribution alongside a [[capacitor]]'s internal ESR, which is why very large capacitors use bolted lugs instead of leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> The joint is also a defined point on the thermal path: [[led|LED]] thermal calculations use the [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] from die to solder junction point, taken from the [[datasheet]], as the first leg between the die and the [[heat-sink|heatsink]] fins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup>
