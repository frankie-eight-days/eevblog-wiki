Bluetooth is the short-range wireless link that has become the default way a [[battery]]-powered device talks to a phone, and by extension the default way test instruments, locks, speakers, inverters and sensors get a user interface without a cable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1719" title="EEVblog 1719 - 75kWh Home Storage Battery Expansion!">1719</a></sup> Its appeal to designers is cost and power: complete assembled modules sell for a few dollars, low enough that the [[serial-port|serial interface]] chip used to program one can cost more than the entire Bluetooth board it talks to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> Its cost to users is a connection layer that is slow to pair, breaks on operating-system updates, and puts an app between the operator and the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Q_RYG_5cQk8" title="Brymen BM787BT BLE Module Replacement">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> The name derives from Harold Bluetooth, a Nordic king.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup>

## Power budget

The low-power variants draw little enough that a coin cell is a realistic supply. A continuous connection at a once-per-second interval averages roughly 80 microwatts, which is about four times the operating [[current]] of the host device in a smartwatch-class product — significant in relative terms, negligible in absolute ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-945" title="EEVblog #945 - Thermal Powered Smartwatches Are GIMMICKS!">945</a></sup> At a combined 100 microwatts, a CR2450 of around 2,000 mAh capacity runs for roughly 20,000 hours, about 2.3 years, with the radio connected 24 hours a day and no deep sleep at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-945" title="EEVblog #945 - Thermal Powered Smartwatches Are GIMMICKS!">945</a></sup> This is the arithmetic that makes energy-harvesting schemes for such products unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-945" title="EEVblog #945 - Thermal Powered Smartwatches Are GIMMICKS!">945</a></sup>

The draw is pulsed rather than continuous: the radio takes short bursts of current on top of the host's steady consumption.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XOuYJ1nxVJQ" title="BM787BT Bluetooth CYBLE-212006-01 Fimware OTA Update">•</a></sup> In a Bluetooth-equipped meter this changes end-of-life behaviour — the instrument detects the low-battery condition under the transmit pulses and shuts itself down or drops into sleep, and on the next power-up gives no low-battery indication at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XOuYJ1nxVJQ" title="BM787BT Bluetooth CYBLE-212006-01 Fimware OTA Update">•</a></sup> Logging over the link costs measurable extra power in the host; with transmit mode switched off the current [[waveform]] falls back to peaks of around 3 milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> Where the radio is a design constraint on physical layout, [[battery-capacity|battery capacity]] wins: a meter built around an integrated Bluetooth module was made about five millimetres thicker to move from AAA to AA cells purely for run time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RDUn3CoVmuc" title="EEVblog Multimeter 3D Printed SLA case">•</a></sup>

Range is the price paid for that power budget. A Bluetooth link is viable for a short hop to a nearby [[relay]] but cannot reach far, which rules it out as the primary link wherever the far end is not close by.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup> Metal enclosures compound the problem — an aluminium chassis forces the [[antenna]] outside to get any reception at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-885" title="EEVblog #885 - Audi Quattro Lunar Rover Unboxing!">885</a></sup>

## Modules and silicon

Bluetooth is almost always bought in as a module or a combi chip rather than designed from scratch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup> Nordic Semiconductor BLE parts turn up repeatedly in commodity products, from proximity-tracing badges to consumer devices whose radio exists only to reach a phone app.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1290" title="EEVblog #1290 - Hapbee: Anatomy of a Snake Oil Product">1290</a></sup> The nRF52 family is cheap enough — a dollar or two — that it is worth selecting as the general-purpose [[microcontroller]] even in a design that does not use the radio, on the grounds that any future connectivity requirement is then already covered; the same argument applies to the ESP32 and Wi-Fi.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1694" title="EEVblog 1694 - Scarlet Rae Teardown + Design Discussion with Xentronics">1694</a></sup> This is a case for keeping the option open, not for standardising on one part regardless of its trade-offs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1694" title="EEVblog 1694 - Scarlet Rae Teardown + Design Discussion with Xentronics">1694</a></sup>

On the low-power side, ultra-low-power Bluetooth development kits built around Gecko processors have sold at around $99 US with an e-ink display and control board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-899" title="EEVblog #899 - Extreme Mailbag!">899</a></sup> At the integrated end, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth commonly share one combi chipset — a dual-band 802.11n and Bluetooth 2 part, for instance, in an action camera.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup> Integration has a failure consequence: when the shared silicon is disturbed, both radios go down together, as with the Raspberry Pi 3, where a camera photoflash locks up the Wi-Fi and, because the chipset is integrated, almost certainly the Bluetooth with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-901" title="EEVblog #901 - Raspberry Pi 3 Photoflash Problem">901</a></sup> Devices from an era before that integration carried separate chipsets for each of GPS, Bluetooth and the rest, and the chip count reflected it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup>

## In test instruments

Bluetooth in a meter is a data path, not a feature in itself, and the corpus treats it that way. A top-of-the-line handheld [[multimeter]] that advertises PC and tablet automation but omits an internal radio, offering instead the same old external module as a paid optional extra, is a design failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup> The same criticism applies to well-built instruments that put the Bluetooth icon on the [[lcd|LCD]] but ship the radio separately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup> Where the radio is fitted, its behaviour still needs to be right: on one clamp meter the Bluetooth annunciator appears only after pressing a mode button rather than being present as expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UiSlVsvjDR0" title="Testo Clamp Meter FAIL?">•</a></sup> Elsewhere a Bluetooth link is quietly wasted — an instrument with internal memory, an app and a radio that still cannot record its trend chart to either.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup>

A recurring design idea is to use the wireless link to separate the display from the meter: integrating Bluetooth, ZigBee or similar for a second display that a user buys only if they need it, magnet-mounted and separately battery-powered, which also serves as the PC or phone interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> Commercially, the option can simply never arrive — one meter series was sold from 2020 with a promised Bluetooth option, a footprint on the board and a production marker where the module belonged, and the option remained unreleased for about five years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TkmMitYnHUA" title="New Brymen BM787BT Bluetooth Multimeter Teardown (Live Recorded)">•</a></sup>

The counter-example is the instrument that is nothing without the phone. A Bluetooth multimeter that reduces its own front panel to indicator LEDs is borderline useless for anything unless connected, and loses the immediate, obvious information a rotary function switch gives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> Its only redeeming property was a reasonable Bluetooth update rate, everything else being unimplemented or unreliable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> Bluetooth connectivity in such products functions as a funding pitch rather than an engineering decision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>

## Pairing, apps and reliability

BLE instruments typically do not use the operating system's pairing flow at all: the device broadcasts continuously and the app receives it, so attempting to pair through the phone's Bluetooth settings returns a pairing rejection and is unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Q_RYG_5cQk8" title="Brymen BM787BT BLE Module Replacement">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ruxzp0OMicg" title="Brymen BM787BT Bluetooth App First Impressions">•</a></sup> Connecting from inside the app rather than through the Android connection dialog is the better arrangement in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ruxzp0OMicg" title="Brymen BM787BT Bluetooth App First Impressions">•</a></sup> Classic-Bluetooth devices still use the older flow, discoverable by scan with a default passkey of 0000 and an RSSI reading — around −57 dB at bench distance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wSdkpx4BjzY" title="BM787BT Apple Shoe Phone Test">•</a></sup>

Android apps for these devices ask for location permission. This is a requirement imposed by the Bluetooth driver stack rather than anything the application needs, and it applies equally to meter apps and to unrelated Bluetooth accessories.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1567" title="EEVblog 1567 - Wireless ESD Monitor? - The IONA StatIQ Band TESTED">1567</a></sup> Requiring an account login merely to operate a multimeter is unnecessary; a Bluetooth meter app should connect and work without one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>

Reliability is the standing complaint. A link that was rock solid on a given Android handset can stop connecting entirely after an app and OS update while continuing to work on iOS, with no change at the device end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Q_RYG_5cQk8" title="Brymen BM787BT BLE Module Replacement">•</a></sup> Setting up one Bluetooth product required disabling other Bluetooth devices in range first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gsfDx7B9sks" title="Bluetooth Enabling a Security Safe!">•</a></sup> Bluetooth stacks on some accessories never work at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7iYcpiY2T2U" title="TS80 USB-C Portable Soldering Iron Follow-up">•</a></sup> The judgement is blunt: "Bloody Bluetooth, it's pain in the ass. Always has been."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Q_RYG_5cQk8" title="Brymen BM787BT BLE Module Replacement">•</a></sup> In development terms, Bluetooth and gesture handling are the two components that consume the porting effort when moving a meter app to a second mobile platform, even when the underlying libraries claim support for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

[[firmware|Firmware]] in the radio module is itself field-updatable over the air. Updating a Bluetooth multimeter's module from within the app is done wirelessly; the process gracefully shuts down the meter, and on reconnection a waveform capture and data log resume from where they left off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XOuYJ1nxVJQ" title="BM787BT Bluetooth CYBLE-212006-01 Fimware OTA Update">•</a></sup> Such updates fix real defects — one release addressed a long-running logging bug that cut off notifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XOuYJ1nxVJQ" title="BM787BT Bluetooth CYBLE-212006-01 Fimware OTA Update">•</a></sup>

## Interference

A transmitting Bluetooth phone is an EMI source at bench distances. Placing a phone that is transmitting over Bluetooth on top of a powered monitor speaker produced audible popping and crackling that disappeared when the phone was moved away and returned when it was brought back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> The general lesson is that the radio's energy has to go somewhere, and nearby analogue circuitry is a candidate path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

## Applications and hazards

Bluetooth is the standard data-out route for equipment that must be configured or logged in place. Solar [[inverter]] output has been extracted over the Bluetooth interface by a PC-side program and uploaded automatically to a public logging service, run continuously and without trouble for years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1086" title="EEVblog #1086 - 5 Year Solar Power Results - Payback?">1086</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1385" title="EEVblog 1385 - 8kW Home Solar Power System EXPANSION!">1385</a></sup> The limitation is proximity — commissioning an inverter over its Bluetooth link means physically standing next to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1719" title="EEVblog 1719 - 75kWh Home Storage Battery Expansion!">1719</a></sup> Other instruments offer Bluetooth to a phone alongside a direct [[usb|USB]] connection, which is the more reliable of the two for extended runs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UE_srZZdPjM" title="Radiacode 103 Gamma Ray Spectrometer">•</a></sup>

In audio, Bluetooth is the cheap universal input. It is what a hacked smart speaker is reduced to when its cloud dependency is stripped out, and it is present on essentially every consumer boombox and party speaker.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - FREE Your Sonos Speaker! (HACK)">1519</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1513" title="EEVblog 1513 - Dumpster Tossers Have No Shame!">1513</a></sup> It is not free of trade-offs: on cheap hardware the Bluetooth path is mono and limited to speech-grade quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1590" title="EEVblog 1590 - Cheap $33 Boombox Teardown">1590</a></sup> On serious monitors a physical switch to disable the radio is preferable to a software toggle, and a balanced input remains the better interface where one is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1162" title="EEVblog #1162 - Little British Monitors">1162</a></sup>

For access control the concern is dependency. A Bluetooth padlock can be well-executed hardware and still a poor product, because habitual phone use erodes memory of the backup combination; when the coin cell dies, the phone fails, or the link does not come up, the fallback is gone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1014" title="EEVblog #1014 - Masterlock Bluetooth Padlock Teardown">1014</a></sup> Whether the radio's own security has been implemented correctly is a separate and generally unverified question.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1014" title="EEVblog #1014 - Masterlock Bluetooth Padlock Teardown">1014</a></sup> The inverse argument holds for devices with no radio at all: a camera with no Wi-Fi, no Bluetooth and no cloud upload cannot be attacked remotely, and its files are reachable only by physically connecting to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup>

The proximity-detection application uses the radio as a range proxy rather than a data pipe. Contact-tracing badges built on a Nordic BLE part ping other badges nearby, carry a serial number rather than personal data, and hold no wireless connectivity beyond that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup>
