Duty cycle is the fraction of a repeating [[waveform]]'s period during which the signal is in its active state, conventionally expressed as a percentage of on time to total period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> For a periodic pulse train at a fixed repetition rate, the [[frequency]] stays constant while the duty cycle varies from 0 % — the output never leaves the low state — up to 100 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A signal at 20 % duty cycle is on for 20 % of the time and off for the other 80 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup>

It matters because it is the control variable behind pulse-width modulation, and through [[pwm|PWM]] it sets average [[voltage]], average [[current]], brightness, motor and fan speed, and [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]] output — all from a purely digital output pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src--9l41zNWlxY" title="Guest Video: Voltlog #142 - Mailbag">•</a></sup>

## Duty cycle as an analogue control variable

A PWM signal is nothing more than a digital waveform with a varying duty cycle, so it can be generated by a dedicated PWM peripheral in a [[microcontroller]] or, failing that, bit-banged on any general-purpose I/O pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A hardware PWM module is preferred where available, since it generates the waveform without software intervention.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

Passing that waveform through an [[rc-filter|RC filter]] averages the on time into a linearly proportional DC voltage spanning the full logic swing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A 10 % duty cycle on a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] filters to one tenth of 5 V, or half a volt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> This makes a PWM output plus a filter functionally a digital-to-analogue converter: a value from 0 to 100 % is converted into a duty cycle from 0 to 100 %, and the filter turns that into a proportional voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> In a lab [[power-supply|power supply]] control loop driven from a 3.3 V microcontroller rail through a gain-of-two amplifier, a 20 % duty cycle produces the expected 1.3 V from a 6.6 V full-scale range, and the relationship holds down to 1 % and 0.1 % duty cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

The averaging behaviour also underlies power ratio arguments. Where the on current is fixed, shrinking the duty cycle — a short active burst against a long sleep interval — reduces average current proportionally, and [[battery-life|battery life]] rises accordingly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> The same reasoning applies at extreme peak powers: accelerating structures energised only for 10 microsecond bursts draw very high peak power but, because the duty cycle is very low, dissipate a relatively low average.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-836" title="EEVblog #836 - Tour Of The Australian Synchrotron">836</a></sup>

Duty cycle also enters switching converter design directly, where the on-time to off-time ratio T_on/T_off is the quantity computed from the [[datasheet]] formula and the desired [[output-voltage|output voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

## Applications

**Display brightness.** Multiplexed vacuum fluorescent displays are driven with bursts, and adjusting the duty cycle and frequency of that drive sets display brightness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> [[led|LED]] brightness control by PWM behaves the same way, with a 555-based modulator sweeping illumination smoothly across the range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup>

**Fan control.** A thermal fan controller with a low and a high temperature threshold ramps the PWM duty cycle on its output pin linearly from 10 % up to 100 % between the two setpoints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--9l41zNWlxY" title="Guest Video: Voltlog #142 - Mailbag">•</a></sup>

**Signalling.** The pilot signal on an electric vehicle charger is a 1 kHz [[square-wave|square wave]] whose duty cycle encodes the available current; 53 % corresponds to the full 7 kW, roughly 32 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup>

**Phase control.** In a triac circuit, adjusting the timing resistors shifts the conduction duty cycle of the current through the device, which is equivalent to controlling its turn-on and turn-off points within the AC cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup>

**Clock timing constraints.** Duty cycle is occasionally a hard requirement rather than a control knob. DMA memory processing in a Tandy 1000 required a fixed 4.77 MHz clock at 33 % duty cycle; substituting a different clock crashed the machine, so a turbo modification had to synthesise the correct signal regardless of the CPU speed selected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-32" title="EEVblog #32 - Tandy 1000 Retro Computer time!">32</a></sup>

**[[noise|Noise]] rejection.** Threshold-based detectors can exploit duty cycle as a discriminator: a Schmitt-trigger stage biased so that only a correctly shaped input crosses the thresholds will reject noise that arrives at the wrong duty cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup>

**Thermal lifetime.** Equipment specified for a long service life is rarely run continuously, and the fact that a public-address amplifier would never be driven at 100 % duty cycle for speech was part of the argument that its electrolytics would not spend their rated hours at peak temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>

## Where duty cycle causes damage

Driving a liquid crystal segment with a plain digital output technically works but is an incorrect method.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1045" title="EEVblog #1045 - How To Drive an LCD">1045</a></sup> A 50 % duty cycle from a 5 V drive signal leaves a 2.5 V DC average across the cell, and DC offsets on an [[lcd|LCD]] will eventually destroy it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1045" title="EEVblog #1045 - How To Drive an LCD">1045</a></sup> The correct approach avoids any net DC component rather than relying on duty cycle balance alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1045" title="EEVblog #1045 - How To Drive an LCD">1045</a></sup>

## Generating and setting duty cycle

Older function generators built square waves by slewing a triangle wave into a [[comparator]], which limited them to a handful of fixed slew rates and a minimum duty cycle of about 10 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 3 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Direct waveform synthesis lifts that to below 1 % with continuously variable slew rates, which materially widens what the instrument can be used for on the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 3 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> A typical analogue [[function-generator|function generator]]'s duty cycle control covers roughly 20 % to 80 %, and 20 % is a common lower stop in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5" title="EEVblog #5 - Maxim product marketing, Function Generator Review">5</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Vendors marketing pulse-specific modes cite lower phase noise and jitter along with finer resolution in setting duty cycle and pulse width.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup>

Wide-range modulation is achievable with very simple hardware. A 555 astable driving an LED reaches only about 8.8 % maximum duty cycle when taken from the output pin, but moving the measurement and drive to the discharge pin — the not-Q output of the internal flip-flop, of which pin 3 is a buffered inverse — yields a smooth sweep from roughly 0.3 % to better than 99 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> The [[oscillation]] frequency shifts somewhat across that range, which for a brightness control is inconsequential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup>

Extremely asymmetric duty cycles have diagnostic uses. [[multimeter|Multimeter]] continuity latching is probed with a pulse whose duty cycle is 99.9 % or higher, so the input goes low for only 0.1 % of the period — enough to reveal whether the meter latches on a brief open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1671" title="EEVblog 1671 - Beware of Multimeter Continuity Latching">1671</a></sup> [[battery|Battery]]-powered instruments with automatic power-off are similarly tested with a slow, low-duty stimulus: a 100 mHz signal at 10 % duty cycle, one 0 V to 5 V event every 10 seconds, checks whether periodic activity resets the sleep timer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BuFoA-qt1PY" title="Brymen BM2257 Intelligent Auto Power Off (iAPO) tested">•</a></sup> Discharge-load experiments run the other way: a low discharge [[resistor]] value drives a self-oscillating load to essentially a 50/50 duty cycle, and at 130 ohms the circuits did not work at all, requiring an increase to 180 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup>

Some instruments carry a duty-cycle-defined output for no obvious purpose, such as an industrial handheld meter's 600 Hz, 50 % duty cycle pulse output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup>

## Measurement

Duty cycle is a standard horizontal measurement on digital oscilloscopes, sitting alongside frequency, period, rise and fall time and pulse width.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Some instruments break it out further, offering negative duty cycle as a separate reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Logic analysers offer the same set — width, period, duty cycle and frequency — over digital captures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup> Universal counters provide duty cycle among their phase and timing modes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup> Function and pattern generators expose it as a definable parameter on synthesised clocks, alongside frequency, idle time and output type.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f_SdM6sXHD4" title="FNIRSI SG-003A Process Meter + DSO-TC2 Oscilloscope &amp; Transistor Tester">•</a></sup>

Duty cycle measurement is also common on handheld multimeters, generally grouped with the frequency function and reached from the same button in voltage or current modes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Applied to a 1 kHz square wave at 20 % duty cycle, such a meter reads back 20 % and 80 % correctly as the source is varied, with the corresponding 0.2 ms and 0.8 ms high and low pulse widths.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> The pulse width reading, however, degrades in the presence of large DC offsets — accurate at low offset, but jumping at an offset of 2.5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup>

Adjusting the source duty cycle is a standard way to exercise a probe or front end. Sweeping the duty cycle of a GaN switching stimulus while observing it through an optically isolated probe demonstrates common-mode rejection on the order of 100 dB, a measurement a differential probe could not make cleanly because of [[ringing]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>
