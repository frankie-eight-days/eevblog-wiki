A dropper resistor is a [[resistor]] placed in series with a load to absorb the surplus between a fixed supply [[voltage]] and the voltage the load actually wants, thereby setting the [[current]] through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> It is the cheapest possible current-setting element, and it is the reason a single [[led|LED]] can be run from a 9 V plugpack, a 24 V strip, a 170 V nixie supply, or a 240 V mains rail without any active circuitry at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-435" title="EEVblog #435 - 3D Rocker Teardown">435</a></sup> Its ubiquity is matched only by its inefficiency: everything the load does not use is dissipated as heat in the resistor, and the current it sets holds only for the supply voltage it was calculated at.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

## Sizing

The calculation is Ohm's law applied to the leftover voltage. The voltage across the resistor is the supply voltage minus the voltage the load drops, and the [[resistance]] is that difference divided by the desired current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> For an LED run from a 3.3 V rail with a 1.8 V forward drop, the resistor must absorb 1.5 V at whatever current is wanted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> A red LED is taken at roughly 1.8 V for this purpose, which leaves enough headroom to light it even from a 2 V supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Where an exam or a [[datasheet]] supplies only a nominal LED drop, that figure is what the dropper calculation has to be run with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

In a Zener shunt regulator the same arithmetic gives the dropper value RZ from the input voltage minus the Zener voltage, divided by the Zener test current, since essentially all the current flows through the Zener.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> The Zener's own dynamic resistance — 5 ohms at a 50 mA test current for a typical 5.1 V part — is at least an order of magnitude below the dropper value and can be dropped from the equation without material error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

The tolerance demanded of a dropper is usually low. When a resistor's only job is to set [[led-current|LED current]], its exact value is not critical and a nearby preferred value is acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> Where the current does matter, a decade resistance box substituted for the final resistor lets the working value be found empirically before the design is committed: at 170 V, a 20 K box setting produced 1.67 mA through a [[nixie-tube|nixie tube]], which lit and switched cleanly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>

## Driving LEDs and displays

Good practice gives every LED, or every segment of a multi-segment display, its own individual dropper resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> Sharing one resistor across paralleled LEDs is explicitly avoided; a second LED added in parallel gets its own dropper rather than being tied directly across the first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-182" title="EEVblog #182 - Rode Videomic Shotgun Microphone Hack">182</a></sup> Boards with no dropper resistors at all on the display lines light up, but segment brightness then varies with how many segments are turned on at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> Where a display is multiplexed, effective brightness depends jointly on the dropper values and on the fixed rail feeding them, so a sagging [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] shows up directly as a brightness change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup>

Practical implementations follow the same pattern across widely different products. A digital clock ties a permanently lit decimal point through its own individual dropper straight to the rail, and drives a colon as two diodes in series with a single dropper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup> A shotgun microphone indicator uses 1 K droppers with 4.5 V across them, giving 4.5 mA per LED — high for a 9 V [[battery]], though [[battery-life|battery life]] remains acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-182" title="EEVblog #182 - Rode Videomic Shotgun Microphone Hack">182</a></sup> An infrared proximity sensor drives its two transmitter LEDs through a pair of droppers, whose junctions double as convenient scope tap points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-377" title="EEVblog #377 - Mailbag Monday">377</a></sup> A mains socket tester is nothing more than a [[diode]] in series with an LED and a series dropper, three such branches arranged in a triangle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> An LED panel lamp may contain no circuitry whatsoever beyond one dropper per LED fed from the plugpack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> [[microcontroller|Microcontroller]] I/O pins driving indicator LEDs get a dropper each in the ordinary way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

Some drivers make the resistor unnecessary. Older counter/display chips such as the 4026 have buffered outputs able to drive LED displays directly without droppers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup> The LM3914 bargraph driver has programmable LED current, adjustable from 2 mA up to 30 mA, so no dropper resistors are needed and the component count falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

Omitting the resistor without such a driver is destructive. A 1 W LED connected straight across a switched output with no dropper drew 250 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-135" title="EEVblog #135 - Kindle Case Mythbusting">135</a></sup> Applying 9 V to a seven-segment display without a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] destroys the segment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s2KkgI-kyK0" title="The Signal Path Lab">•</a></sup> Some hobby kits shipped with the dropper resistors physically mounted on the display's own little board, visible on top, precisely because the omission was such a common way to lose a part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s2KkgI-kyK0" title="The Signal Path Lab">•</a></sup> A display drive scheme that relies on splitting a fixed source current between segments rather than using droppers should be treated as having no [[current-limiting|current limiting]], and would not survive a design review on that point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup>

## Nixie tubes and high-voltage loads

High-voltage gas displays are driven the same way, with the dropper doing the work of turning a stiff HV rail into a defined tube current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> A 22 K dropper on a 170 V supply gives about 1.6 mA through a lit digit; the same 22 K on a 120 V rail gives roughly 2 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Because a nixie digit is a single shaped [[cathode]] rather than an assembly of segments, one dropper serves one digit at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> Where high-channel-count drivers of 32 or 64 outputs are used, each tube still needs its own separate dropper, which constrains how outputs may be allocated across tubes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup>

Not every electrode tolerates the same current. A nixie decimal point has a smaller surface area and a limit around 0.3 mA, so an additional series resistor is added on that line beyond the digit dropper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> On the [[pcb|PCB]] these resistors are numerous and awkward, and are typically massaged into the leftover space, including underneath the tubes themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

Extreme supply voltages push dropper values into unusual territory. A homebrew [[optocoupler]] in an electric fence controller uses a 1 megohm LED dropper, which by the rule of thumb of 1 mA per kilovolt sets the LED current from the fence [[output-voltage|output voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup>

## Mains and off-line supplies

Where a small amount of low-voltage housekeeping power must be pulled from the mains, a large dropper resistor feeding a rectifier and Zener is the traditional approach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-435" title="EEVblog #435 - 3D Rocker Teardown">435</a></sup> Such a board sits at mains potential throughout and cannot safely be probed with an earth-referenced scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-435" title="EEVblog #435 - 3D Rocker Teardown">435</a></sup> A resistive dropper of this kind is wasteful by construction: power is burned in the resistor and again in the Zener to deliver very little [[output-current|output current]], which is why a capacitive dropper is preferred where orders of magnitude less waste is wanted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> The substitution has its own failure mode — an X2 class metallised film [[capacitor]] serving as the dropper for a Zener is a documented failure point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1486" title="EEVblog 1486 - What you DIDN&#x27;T KNOW About Film Capacitor FAILURES!">1486</a></sup>

Purely resistive dropping also appears in cheap mains-powered lighting and chargers, where a [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] plus dropper resistors and an [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] stands in for any regulation or constant-current drive at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1278" title="EEVblog #1278 - Mailbag">1278</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-592" title="EEVblog #592 - Mailbag">592</a></sup> A battery charger built this way has no regulation whatsoever, and leaving cells on it too long can cook them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup> The same shortcut appears on lower rails: a 17.5 V rail feeding logic through nothing more than a dropper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup>

## Other roles

The dropper idea extends beyond LEDs. High-value input dropper resistors set the attenuation ratio and limit input current on the front end of a high-voltage differential probe, four megohms per side, in place of [[galvanic-isolation|galvanic isolation]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> A power analyser attenuates its inputs with four dropper resistors per leg, attenuating so heavily that no additional [[input-protection|input protection]] is required regardless of transient voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> A dropper resistor in a lab supply's reference chain sets the current range from 0 to 1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> A 10 ohm resistor in series with a device's supply is enough for basic powerline analysis with nothing but a scope probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup> Changing an indicator's dropper value also changes an [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]] where the LED shares a node with a timing capacitor, so the resistor cannot always be treated as an isolated component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup>

## When not to use one

A dropper resistor holds current constant only against a constant supply and a constant load drop. Where LED current must be well defined, a constant-current driver removes both the resistor and the need to calculate and specify it, reducing the design to a two-chip solution with no droppers anywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-513" title="EEVblog #513 - Mailbag">513</a></sup> Where 145 RGB LEDs must be run from a 12 V rail whose [[compliance-voltage|compliance voltage]] forces many short parallel strings, the alternative is a dropper in every string.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-928" title="EEVblog #928 - Jaycar DMX RGB LED Lights Teardown">928</a></sup>

The tradeoff is not one-sided. On 24 V LED tape, a strip using a single dropper per segment measured 19.2 W per metre and met its specification precisely, while a strip of the same form factor using an active constant-current chip driver failed to reach its rated 32.6 W per metre even after cable losses were accounted for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5mueaTsus8o" title="Wall Wash LED Strip Measurement - Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> The resistive strip is also expected to dim more linearly as the supply is reduced, though it still exhibits a compliance voltage below which the string drops out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> For a quick hack running LEDs from a 7.2 V camera battery, where consistent output, low-[[cutoff-voltage|voltage cutoff]] and protection are all unnecessary, a series dropper calculated for the wanted current is the appropriate answer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> Decorative LED reels driven through a plain dropper are cheap and plentiful but do not deliver the high brightness a single long series string can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup>
