A constant current source is an active circuit that forces a fixed [[current]] through whatever load is connected to it, instead of presenting a fixed [[voltage]] and letting the load decide how much current it draws.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> It is the dual of the [[voltage-source]], and although textbook DC theory introduces it alongside Thevenin and Norton equivalents, that treatment is theoretical rather than practical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> In real hardware it appears everywhere: biasing precision references, driving LEDs and laser diodes, measuring [[resistance]] and [[capacitance]], and setting the operating points inside almost every analogue integrated circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## What it actually is

A current source cannot exist on its own. Drawn practically, it is a voltage source followed in series by active constant current circuitry, and the circuitry has to be active — no arrangement of passive components will do it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Adding external circuitry to an ordinary voltage source is precisely how one is built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

The behaviour is easiest to see at the extremes of load resistance. If a source is designed for 1 amp, then shorting its terminals gives 1 amp through 0 ohms; the current stays where it was set even as the load resistance changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> A [[short-circuit]] is the best possible load for a constant current source, the exact opposite of a voltage source, where shorting the output is a very bad idea.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

## Compliance voltage

The voltage source behind the current circuitry sets the limit on what the source can achieve, and that limit is called the [[compliance-voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> It can be thought of as the supply rail available to the circuit, and it is the maximum voltage the source will put across a load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> The constant current circuit is not a voltage multiplier: with only 5 volts of compliance, no more than 5 volts can appear across the load, and beyond that point regulation is lost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

Compliance also has to be matched to the load, not merely to the current. A 1 microamp source on a 100 volt supply still cannot bring a [[zener-diode]] into conduction, because the current itself is insufficient regardless of how much compliance is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Conversely, an IEPE accelerometer needing a few milliamps also demands compliance somewhere between 18 and 30 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Instrument specifications state the figure as an open circuit compliance voltage — 180 volts in the case of one TENS-style stimulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup>

## Current source and current limiter

The same circuit can be read two ways. A bench [[power-supply]] is a constant current source as well as a voltage source; which behaviour is observed depends on the application and the load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The current knob is normally thought of as a limiter, and that view and the current-source view describe the same hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The [[lm317]] makes this explicit: with a [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] between output and adjust, and the adjust pin tied to the output, the classic adjustable [[voltage-regulator]] becomes a constant current source, or a current limiter, depending on the point of view.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

## Circuit implementations

The LM317 arrangement is the minimal discrete approach — the regulator, a couple of resistors and a [[capacitor]], with the [[resistor]] tied between the output pin and the adjust pin — and it is good for around a watt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-992" title="EEVblog #992 (Part 2) - How To Clean &amp; Service A Microscope">992</a></sup>

The LM334 is a purpose-built two-terminal part in a TO-92 package that programs its current with a single resistor. It runs from 1 volt to 40 volts, covers 1 microamp to 10 milliamps, and has 3 percent initial accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> Temperature is the limiting factor: a crude two-[[transistor]] source built from jelly bean 2N3904s drifts about 15 percent over a 50 degree C rise, roughly 0.3 percent per degree C, and the LM334 has essentially the same [[temperature-coefficient]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup>

Higher performance comes from op-amp feedback. The improved Howland current pump uses an op-amp, five resistors and a [[voltage-reference]] to source a few milliamps up to tens of milliamps for driving an [[led]] or a sensor, where [[efficiency]] is not a concern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup> Its principle is that a difference amplifier holds a constant voltage across a resistance, and a constant voltage across a resistor is a constant current — so the current through a series load stays at 1 mA regardless of the load value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup> The same idea scales up: holding a reference voltage of 1.25 V across a four-terminal [[current-shunt]] yields 1 amp, with essentially all of it flowing through a high-gain Darlington NPN pass device that needs only a milliamp or two of base current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> A power FET in an op-amp feedback loop, with a DAC setting the current and an ADC monitoring the voltage, is the standard arrangement for a laser [[diode]] driver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-bg6QsTT0Plw" title="Laser Driver PCB &amp; Circuit Review">•</a></sup> A 10 kW argon ion laser supply goes further and sets the current for each parallel pass transistor individually with its own op-amp, rather than paralleling the transistors behind a single sense resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup>

A JFET above a certain drain voltage behaves as a constant current source in its own right, and that saturation region is where the device should be operated — below it, transconductance collapses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-611" title="EEVblog #611 - Electret Microphone Design">611</a></sup> Whatever generates a constant current across a resistor is a current source in effect: a 1.25 V bandgap reference across a 10 K resistor sets 125 microamps, and because base current contributes nothing, the same current flows in the downstream branch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-329" title="EEVblog #329 - Tracking Pre-Regulator LTspice Simulation Part 2">329</a></sup>

## Inside integrated circuits

Constant current generators are ubiquitous inside analogue chips. A typical [[comparator]] may contain three or four of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> In the 555 timer they bias the trigger comparator circuitry and feed the flip-flop, and the [[current-limiting]] they provide removes the need for a [[series-resistor]] in the cross-coupled latch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Constant current generators are frequently arranged as current mirrors, with the current in one branch set equal to the current in another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The LT3080 uses an internal 10 microamp constant current generator in place of the LM317's internal voltage reference; that current must flow out of the set pin into the programming resistor, and it contributes an offset error at low output settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> A device whose [[quiescent-current]] is held by an internal current source shows the same quiescent draw across a 25 V to 450 V input range — 7.5 microamps typical, up to 14 microamps worst case over temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Current mode logic biases its transistors from constant current sources to prevent saturation, giving extremely rapid switching and high noise immunity despite low logic levels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-867" title="EEVblog #867 - The Search For The First TTL Chip">867</a></sup>

## Biasing voltage references

A high-stability Zener reference needs a known constant current through it to produce a stable voltage, which is why reference circuits so often power the Zener from a current source — it makes the design far simpler than the resistor-fed case, where supply voltage, resistance and temperature all interact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> A DC voltage standard sets 6.5 milliamps through its Zener [[via]] an adjustment pot, trimming the current until the desired voltage appears; with the diode held at constant temperature by an internal heater and a constant current flowing, stability is very high, even though the diode's initial tolerance may be 2 percent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

## Measurement applications

Four-wire [[resistance-measurement]] is done by passing a known constant current through the unknown resistance via drive leads and measuring the [[voltage-drop]] with separate sense leads, so the drop along the drive leads does not appear in the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> This can be done manually with a current source and a [[multimeter]], or automatically by an instrument such as the HP 3478A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> A practical fixture is a low-value resistor with the current leads and the sense wires landed directly on the resistor's contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup>

Inside a multimeter, the ohms ranges work by pushing a constant current out through the same switched range network used for DC and AC volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> [[capacitance-measurement]] uses the same trick differently: a constant current output charges the capacitor and the chipset times how long it takes to reach a threshold, from which the capacitance follows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> The linear voltage ramp that a constant current produces across a capacitor is also the basis for deriving the half-CV-squared energy relationship.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1618" title="EEVblog 1618 - The Equation For Energy In A Capacitor">1618</a></sup>

Current sources also serve as [[calibration]] stimuli. Feeding a precision current adapter 99.9 nanoamps and reading 99.7 confirms the correct sense resistor is fitted, and repeating the check on each range verifies the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> Production test does the same against programmed pass-fail limits, so that tweaking the source current off nominal flags a failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ux7WdK6oym4" title="uCurrent GOLD Kickstarter First Manufacture">•</a></sup> A reference voltage on a test fixture can generate the constant current itself, removing the need for a separate bench current source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> One limitation of series-connecting boards under test to share a single current source is a common output ground, which prevents the current input from floating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## Driving loads

LED strings are the classic application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> An LED strip salvaged from an [[lcd]] television can be driven directly from a laboratory current source at 10 milliamps with the compliance raised to cover several volts per LED in the series chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-916" title="EEVblog #916 - Work Bench Lighting From A Dumpster LCD TV">916</a></sup> Many LED assemblies include their own current source behind the emitter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-338" title="EEVblog #338 - DIY Electric Bike">338</a></sup> A charge-pump LED driver achieves the same result by switching at some [[frequency]] with a [[current-sensing|current sense]] resistor setting the level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> A constant current feed can even be [[pwm]]-dimmed with a 555, although the usual approach is to PWM a voltage source and set the current with a dropper resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup>

[[battery-charging]] exploits the source's indifference to load voltage. In a 15 minute charger, the current comes from a DC-DC converter operating as a current source into a [[mosfet]] totem pole; when a body diode adds its 0.6 volt forward drop, the node rises to 2.2 volts and the charge current is unaffected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> The consequence is that the charge cannot be stopped by pulling the MOSFET gate low — the output of the constant current converter itself must be turned off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup>
