A shift register is a chain of flip-flops that accepts data one bit at a time on a serial input and moves that bit along the chain on each clock edge, so that a long parallel word can be delivered over two or three wires instead of one wire per bit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup> Its dominant practical use is line saving: a controller drives a clock and a data line, and the register expands them into eight, sixteen or forty outputs, which is why the part turns up wherever a board has more things to switch than the [[processor]] has pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> The jellybean 74HC595 and the 4000-series 4094 are the versions most often encountered, and the 595 in particular has been in continuous bench use for decades.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup>

## Operation

Data is presented on the serial input and clocked in. On a positive-going clock edge whatever is on the data pin is transferred to the first output stage, Q0; on the next edge that bit moves from Q0 to Q1, and so on down the chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> Some devices clock on the negative-going edge instead, so the direction of the active edge has to be read off the timing diagram rather than assumed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup> A bit fed in at the input therefore takes as many clock pulses to reach the last output as there are stages: in an eight-bit part, a one placed on the input appears on Q7 only after the eighth clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup>

The 74HC164 is the archetypal plain serial-in, parallel-out device. It has a clock, two data inputs combined through an AND gate, and a clear line, and nothing else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup> The 4094 and the 74HC597 are the equivalent eight-bit parts from the 4000 and 74HC families.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup>

## The output latch

The defect of a plain shift register is that its outputs are live while the data is moving. Every intermediate state of the shift appears on the pins, so an eight-bit word intended to switch eight loads simultaneously instead ripples across them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> With relays on the outputs and a slow enough clock the relays can be heard clunking on and off as the data walks through, and on a multiplexed display the effect is visible as the outputs change in sequence rather than together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> Where the outputs drive a bank of transistors, the transitional states are also a supply problem: switching all the outputs on at once can draw hundreds of milliamps just to drive the output transistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

The fix is a second, parallel register between the shift chain and the output pins. The 74HC595 carries this structure: the shift register part with its own shift clock, serial data input and clear, plus an output latch clocked separately by the R clock line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> Data is shifted in with the outputs frozen at their previous state, then a single pulse on the latch clock transfers all eight bits to the pins at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup> [[firmware|Firmware]] for such a chain accordingly maintains two separate pulse routines, one for the data clock exercised on every bit and one for the latch clock pulsed once at the end of the frame.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup> Latched variants exist at wider widths as well, including parts combining a 16-bit shift register with a 16-bit latch, though the wide devices tend to arrive in awkward packages such as 0.5 mm pitch 32-lead QFN.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>

## Cascading

Registers are daisy-chained by feeding the last stage of one device into the data input of the next, so that a string of chips behaves as one long register: five eight-bit devices form a 40-bit chain, and the first bit sent lands at the far end after 40 clock pulses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup> The chain is driven from the [[microcontroller]] into the first chip and passed chip to chip to the end, with no data read back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> On an [[oscilloscope]] the traffic appears as five packets of eight clock pulses, one packet per digit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup>

Not every part is designed for this. A device intended to be cascaded provides a buffered serial output pin dedicated to the purpose; the 74HC164 does not, and its Q outputs go straight to the output buffers, so a cascaded design has to tap the last output, Q7, and route it back as the data input of the following chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> Cascading also has an electrical cost at the board level, since a single driver on the source end may not be able to drive the accumulated [[capacitance]] of the clock and data lines across several daisy-chained boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

## Driving displays

Display driving is the classic application. A digit-based display such as a Nixie needs ten or eleven separate lines per digit rather than the four of a BCD-to-seven-segment decoder, which rules out the traditional decoder and points instead to a latched shift register clocked in and then latched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> For high-[[voltage]] displays the register only carries the data; external high-voltage transistors and their resistors still have to be provided, which for ten lines per digit means a large number of discrete parts to assemble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>

Two arrangements are possible. In a static drive the data is shifted in once and stays on the outputs, sourcing [[current]] continuously through whatever is enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup> In a multiplexed drive the display is blanked, new data is shifted, and the display is unblanked, repeatedly — many devices provide an output-enable or blanking input intended for exactly this, so the shifting is invisible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> [[multiplexing|Multiplexing]] is often forced by current budget rather than chosen: with eight segments in each of five digits, 40 segments at a per-output maximum of 25 mA cannot all be lit at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> A related pattern drives the digit-select transistors from the register itself, shifting a single active bit along so that exactly one FET, and therefore one display, is on at any instant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

Serial addressable [[led|LED]] strips use the same principle at the module level, each LED passing data on to the next, which makes the strip length arbitrary and the driving code modular.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> Character displays such as VFD modules can behave the same way, each latched byte pushing the character to the next position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup>

## Elsewhere on the bench

Test equipment uses shift registers extensively for slow control paths where speed is irrelevant and pin count is not. Oscilloscopes carry 4094s per input channel to distribute channel control data without running a bundle of lines across the board, and the 595 recurs in scope designs of every generation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> A power analyser's interface board can be little more than a stack of eight-bit shift registers, with the eight-bit parallel output of the ADC shifted through registers so that it can cross an [[optocoupler]] barrier on a single line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> In a logic analyser front end, mask and invert registers loaded serially from the host set the individual bits of the trigger mask and [[comparator]] arrays, which is a cheap way to make a wide configuration word settable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup>

The structure also appears outside data distribution. A three-stage shift register with feedback clocked from a 14.318 MHz source acts as a divide-by-six, yielding 2.38 MHz along with a second output at a defined [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-32" title="EEVblog #32 - Tandy 1000 Retro Computer time!">32</a></sup> Internally, processors may need extra clock cycles simply to move a word from one register to another, which can show up on a programming interface as a burst of clock pulses that does no visible external work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup>

## Practical notes

Level compatibility is the trap when a register drives something other than plain logic: the input [[threshold-voltage|threshold voltage]] of the driven stage has to be checked against what the register actually outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> When debugging a display chain, the fact that the data is visibly changing at the register input proves nothing on its own — the clock has to be confirmed as well, since a register receiving data but no clock produces no output at all while looking alive on the data line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

Layout benefits from the topology. A serially driven display board reduces to a few common control lines plus data in and data out, which allows each display segment to have its own driver placed beside it with almost nothing crisscrossing the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup>

Because the internal state is a visible, ordered thing, the shift register is also a natural teaching device: with LEDs on the outputs and a manual clock button, a bit can be pushed in and watched moving one position per press.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1720" title="EEVblog 1720 - Mailbag: USSR Bonanza + Logic Training + $69 Logic Analyzer">1720</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1080" title="EEVblog #1080 - Gigatron TTL RISC Kit Computer Review">1080</a></sup>
