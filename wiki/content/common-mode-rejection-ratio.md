Common mode rejection ratio (CMRR) is the ratio of a differential amplifier's differential gain to its common mode gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> Every differential amplifier circuit has one, whether it is built from an [[op-amp]] or from discrete transistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> The figure matters because a differential amplifier's job is to amplify the difference between its positive and negative inputs while rejecting whatever signal is applied commonly to both wires, and CMRR states how completely it succeeds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> In theory the rejection would be infinite — the amplifier would measure only the difference and have no gain at all for a common mode signal — but in practice the design of the amplifier itself, and above all the matching of the input [[resistor]] network, sets a finite limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

## Units and sign convention

CMRR is usually quoted in dB, though as a pure ratio it does not have to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> Differential gain divided by common mode gain comes out positive, yet data sheets frequently print a negative number, and there is no standard settling which is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> The consequence is that the sign determines which direction is better: with a negative convention, −60 dB is better rejection than −40 dB, while on a positive convention such as an op amp [[datasheet|data sheet]] might use, a higher number is better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

A further trap is the reference point. A probe specification may be input referred — stated at the input of the amplifier inside the probe, before the internal gain stage — which makes the number look considerably better than an output-referred figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> A gain of ×10 inside the probe is 20 dB, ×100 is 40 dB, and ×1000 is 60 dB, and that offset must be accounted for when a bench measurement is compared against the sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> A well-written data sheet states which convention it uses; declaring a figure input referred is an easy way for marketing to make rejection sound better than it is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

## What limits rejection in practice

The dominant limit in a resistively divided differential front end is matching. In the classic single-op-amp difference amplifier the two divider pairs must be equal — the upper resistors to each other and the lower resistors to each other — and they must match precisely to get good rejection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> In a high-[[voltage]] differential probe the same principle applies to the whole attenuator string: if the string on one input is unbalanced relative to the other, the CMRR is ruined.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup>

Because matching cannot be achieved by tolerance alone, probes are trimmed. The DP10007 carries two additional trim pots on its positive input that the negative input lacks, one of them a CMRR trimmer used to balance the positive path against the negative, with a separate gain trimmer to match the differential gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> The same idea appears in older analogue instrumentation, where a CMRR pot sits alongside an input level pot on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup> Matching is also fragile in service: moisture creeping under the input components will unbalance them and degrade CMRR, which is why an O-ring seal and removed [[solder-mask]] appear around that region of a probe's board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

Scope channel math is the cheapest differential measurement available — subtracting channel 1 from channel 2 in the math menu gives a differential [[waveform]] on analogue and digital scopes alike — but it is a poor man's differential probe precisely because the CMRR is terrible, being set by how well the two probes' input impedances happen to match.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

## Frequency dependence

CMRR is not a single number; it varies with [[frequency]], so a product will typically specify it at several spot frequencies, and occasionally as a response curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> Rejection degrades as frequency rises. On a 70 MHz high-voltage differential probe the degradation becomes visible from around 5 MHz upward.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> Measured with a [[frequency-response]] analyser and corrected for the ×10 setting, that probe gave −51 dB at 1 MHz and −44 dB at 10 MHz against a −40 dB specification at 10 MHz, but only −21 dB at 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> The steepness of the roll-off above 10 MHz explains why data sheets commonly stop quoting at 10 MHz rather than at 50 or 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> Extending the sweep far enough also shows the phase response reverse, which is ordinary amplifier behaviour rather than anything specific to differential circuits, and the phase plot carries no useful information for the CMRR figure itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

Independent confirmation with a vector network analyser reproduced the shape but not the exact value, returning 47.5 dB at 1 MHz where the specification was 50 dB and an earlier [[oscilloscope]]-based measurement had given 51 dB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Dez9KG6whb0" title="CMRR Followup Micsig DP10007 vs HVP70">•</a></sup>

## Measuring it

The measurement drives both inputs with the same signal and compares the amplitude at the input against the amplitude at the output; the ratio of the two, expressed as 20 log of output over input, is the CMRR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> Test setup is decisive, because the quantity being measured is almost down in the [[noise-floor]] of the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Leads should be short, twisted and shielded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> A 12-bit oscilloscope helps, as does high-resolution acquisition mode — with HD mode giving an effective 16 bits — and selecting the probe's highest attenuation range to maximise the available signal level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Long input leads are a liability: external signals can couple into them and wreck the apparent rejection outright, which argues for direct banana-plug inputs when such measurements are routine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Dave Jones's view is that a factory CMRR test should not use long leads at all but should crack the probe open and solder directly onto the input points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

Worked examples follow the same arithmetic. A measured common mode residue of 22.67 mV against an 811 mV input gives −31 dB at 100 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> A residue of 26.2 mV against 804.5 mV works out to −29.7 dB, taken as meeting a −26 dB specification at 100 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup>

Measuring the CMRR of a bare op amp is harder than measuring a probe, because the external resistor network built to apply the common mode signal dominates the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> A technique exists that removes the resistors from the measurement and yields the amplifier's own rejection, using a step difference in the [[power-rail]] voltage instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

## In op amps

Op amp data sheets quote CMRR as a positive dB figure, with precision parts reaching 140 dB typical at a 2.25 V supply and chopper-stabilised parts around 130 dB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-476" title="EEVblog #476 - Opamp Offset Voltage Measurement">476</a></sup> Whether the parameter matters depends entirely on the topology: it is not a leading concern for a [[current]]-measurement front end whose resistors are not directly coupled into the input, where [[power-supply]] and common mode rejection enter only indirectly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup> CMRR is also one of the specifications watched when a manufacturer re-releases an existing part on new silicon; a revised NE5532 kept it unchanged while other specifications moved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup>

## High-voltage differential probes and their ceiling

For mains work and higher-voltage power supply work a high-voltage differential probe is essential kit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> Its limitation is that CMRR falls with frequency exactly where modern switching designs operate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> The problem case is a high-side gate-source measurement: the source terminal itself swings the full rail, so the larger the swinging voltage and the higher the [[switching-frequency]], the more rejection the probe needs to keep that swing out of the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> With a 300 V rail switching at 1 kHz a high-voltage differential probe is fine, and the waveform is unaffected even at full voltage; at 1 MHz the rejection has dropped far enough that ringing and switching artefacts dominate the gate drive waveform entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> Since the [[efficiency]] case for GaN rests on switching fast, the measurement problem is intrinsic to the application rather than incidental.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

Probe CMRR is also where cheaper high-voltage differential probes have failed to deliver. One unit matched every other specification but missed its CMRR specification and was not even close, a result others confirmed independently, and it took years and a hardware revision before the manufacturer addressed it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1414" title="EEVblog 1414 - MicSig DP10007 High Voltage Probe - Turning it up to 11">1414</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> The revised model still did not match the benchmark Sapphire-designed HVP70 on CMRR, though its [[bandwidth]] was better than that probe's 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

## Optically isolated probes

Optically isolated probes sidestep the matching problem rather than solving it. Power is transferred one way and signal the other entirely in the analogue domain with no sampling and no metallic conductor between probe and oscilloscope, giving galvanic isolation up to 60 kV together with the bandwidth of an active FET probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> The resulting rejection is over 100 dB — 106 dB for a 200 MHz version, held across the full 200 MHz bandwidth — high enough that measuring it on a normal bench is itself difficult.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> Architecturally these are single-ended active high-frequency probes rather than differential ones; the rejection comes from the isolation, not from a matched input network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

At that level of rejection the high-side gate-source measurement that defeats a differential probe simply stops being a problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> The same argument drives isolated instrument design generally: an isolated 1 kV oscilloscope front end has two requirements, that it be isolated and floating, and that it have very high CMRR to keep noise from the fast-slewing signal out of the measurement, specified there as 100 dB at 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> Beyond power electronics, the combination of roughly 100 dB rejection with tens of kilovolts of isolation is what makes these probes the only option for high-energy physics work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>
