Efficiency is the ratio of useful output power to input power, expressed as a percentage, and it governs how much of the energy entering a circuit does the intended job rather than becoming waste heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> Any time power is dissipated, efficiency is the question that follows, because the shortfall has to go somewhere and almost always ends up as a thermal problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> The concept is arithmetically trivial and practically unforgiving: it is the single figure that decides whether a converter runs cool on a scrap of aluminium or cooks itself, and whether a [[battery]]-powered product lasts hours or minutes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

## The definition and its two forms

Efficiency in percentage is output power divided by input power, times 100.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> Because the difference between the two is loss, the same quantity can be written as output power divided by output power plus losses, times 100 — the same number approached from the loss side rather than the input side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>

A pure [[resistor]] is, by this definition, 100% efficient: one watt in is one watt of heat out, and heat is exactly what a resistor is for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> Efficiency only becomes less than 100% once the intended output is something other than the heat being produced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> The equation applies cleanly when both sides are electrical — electrical power in, electrical power out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> It does not apply directly when the output is a different form of energy altogether: an [[led]] producing lumens rather than watts has a lumens-per-watt figure and its own component-level conversion efficiency, so its losses sit outside the power-in-power-out equation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> The same caveat holds for mechanical output such as force, where conversion losses, internal resistances, and wiring losses all subtract from the useful result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>

## Efficacy is not efficiency

The distinction between efficacy and efficiency is a persistent trap in lighting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup> A figure such as 132 lumens per watt typical efficacy is not an efficiency number and tells nothing about how efficient the LEDs actually are, nor how much waste heat the design must handle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup> Extracting a true efficiency figure requires going to the theoretical response curves rather than the headline specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup> The two terms are often used interchangeably by people unaware of the difference, and depending on the luminous efficiency and the context they can genuinely coincide, which is what makes the confusion durable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> The practical consequence is thermal: an LED emitting on the order of 0.8 watts of light energy into a couple of square millimetres will burn a finger placed directly on it even while the [[heat-sink|heatsink]] and base beneath remain cool to the touch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup>

## Efficiency curves and how they mislead

For a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]], efficiency is not a number but a curve — efficiency in percent on the Y axis against output [[current]] on the X axis — and it is the first thing to look at when selecting a part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> A headline claim such as a 96% efficient synchronous [[boost-converter]], or 94% for a competing part, describes one point on one curve under one set of conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

Datasheets carry different efficiency curves for different output voltages, and reading the wrong one is a common and costly error: a curve showing 85% at 1 mA still holding 85% at 100 mA before tailing off may turn out to be plotted for a 1.8 V output rather than the 5 V output actually being designed for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The correct procedure is to find the curve for the intended output [[voltage]], where the family of traces for various input levels shows how much output current is available — a higher input voltage supports more output current, so a 3.6 V battery input may allow a full amp out where a 1 V input allows only 200 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Parts must be checked by exact part number, since devices in the same family carry different graphs, parameters, and specifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

Some converters offer more than one operating mode, with a normal mode that preserves efficiency at very low or near-zero load currents alongside a [[pwm]] mode intended for higher continuous loads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Substituting a different brand of external component around a switcher can move operation to a different part of the efficiency curve, alongside effects such as increased output ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup>

## Measuring it on the bench

Measurement is a matter of instrumenting both sides simultaneously — input voltage, output voltage, and output current — and stepping the load across the range of interest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Sweeping 0 to 100 mA in 10 mA steps produces a workable performance curve for a small converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Constant power mode on an [[electronic-load]] is useful when plotting efficiency against output power rather than output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> The arithmetic is direct: 52.4 W out from 69.7 W in gives roughly 75%, and the 17.3 W difference is what the heatsink must dissipate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

Real curves share a characteristic shape. Efficiency rises from a poor value at very low current, peaks somewhere near the current the designer optimised for, then declines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> A small unregulated 2 W brick specified at up to 80% delivers 80% or better across the upper half of its range from 1 W to 2 W, around 75% at half a watt, and drops away sharply at very low loads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> A general-purpose brick that stays above 80% from 20 mA to its rated 100 mA but never exceeds 90% anywhere is unremarkable but adequate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Quiescent behaviour is part of the picture: a module drawing 0.390 W with its output switched off draws 0.6 W switched on with no load at all, and that difference is part of its efficiency story.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

Plotting the results requires a scatter plot rather than a line chart if the X axis is to be scaled correctly, and expanding the Y axis rather than starting it at zero exposes detail that would otherwise be compressed into the top of the frame.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

## What real hardware achieves

Characterisation of a wide-range buck module illustrates how strongly efficiency depends on the operating point rather than the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> With 15 V in, a 12 V output reaches roughly 96% right up to 3 A, and 5 V out from the same input peaks near 90%, tapering to a still-respectable 82% at low currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> The same module at 1.5 V out from 15 V in peaks at only about 68% and is worse at high current, despite delivering less total power — 4.5 W against 15 W for the 5 V case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> With 30 V in, the losses grow and the 5 V output falls under 80% at 3 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> At 40 V in, the 32 V output again returns about 95%, which is where the designers evidently optimised it, since that is the maximum-power worst case of about 70 W they must design the thermals around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> Efficiency at the bottom of the current range is poor across every configuration, but at low output current it matters little because very little power is being dissipated in absolute terms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

Other measured examples fill in the range. A wide-range DC-to-DC regulator running from 48 V delivers around 75%, but the same unit at 15.2 V in and 5 A reaches about 86%, running the heatsink at a much cooler 56 °C as a result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> A 360 W bench supply measured about 86.8% efficient at 60 W output, dissipating around 9 watts without getting hot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> A discrete switchmode design built as a teaching example peaked just under 60%, which is unimpressive but not inherently wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> At the other end, an LLC resonant converter reference design measured about 97.5% and held 94% at worst case across its input voltage range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup> An isolated brick specified for 1000 V isolation and 2 W output claims up to 80%, typical for that class; parts optimised for a specific load and exceeding 90% exist but cost considerably more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

## Efficiency as a design constraint

The 80% figure of a small isolated converter propagates straight into a system budget. A [[usb]] port supplies at most 500 mA, so 500 mA in through an 80%-efficient converter yields about 400 mA out, which is why a 2 W converter is the correct match for a nominal 2.5 W USB port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

Component choice sets the achievable number. Schottky diodes have a much lower forward drop than ordinary PN junction diodes — around 0.142 V against roughly half a volt for a 1N4001 or 1N4148 — which is precisely why they are used in switchmode supplies where efficiency matters and power should not be thrown away in [[diode]] losses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Magnetics matter as much: 90%-plus efficiency is achievable in a DC-to-DC converter if the [[inductor]] is chosen correctly, and changing an inductor to 47 µH in a working design raises the peak above 65% while moving the peak to a lower current and causing a steeper drop beyond it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Inductor series [[resistance]] and output filtering diode choice both feed into the resulting curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

Efficiency also decides topology. A [[zener-diode]] as a shunt regulator is acceptable for powering a circuit only where efficiency is not a concern; its more defensible uses are in niche roles inside larger circuits, such as reference voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Where a design already has a modification applied for another purpose, such as passive power-factor correction with added inductors, the unknown efficiency impact is a reason for caution — switching-[[transistor]] losses and IC heating cannot be assumed unchanged without full characterisation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup>

In battery-powered products, a DC-to-DC converter makes the load essentially constant power rather than constant current, so battery-life estimation should be based on constant-power discharge curves — with the converter's efficiency across the load range folded in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Higher converter efficiency directly minimises waste from the battery, which is why it ranks high even when many other selection parameters can be ignored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The same logic scales up: drawing DC directly from a battery pack rather than passing through an [[inverter]] to 240 V AC avoids the inverter losses and yields higher efficiency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFhk1aRqLD8" title="AllPowers R1500 1500W 1150Wh LiFeP04 Battery Teardown + Review">•</a></sup>

Digital control of power conversion has widened what is achievable, allowing correction of anomalies that analogue control could not address and, in high-performance industrial inverters, digital characterisation of individual transistors to extract maximum efficiency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Efficiency as a debunking tool

Because efficiency is bounded and multiplies through a chain, it is a fast test of extraordinary product claims. Ultrasonic power transfer through air loses about 50% over one metre from air absorption alone, leaving 25% at two metres before any other loss is counted, and that figure already assumes 100% efficient transducers, no nonlinearities, and perfect focusing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup> A venture still designing acoustic experiments to measure efficiency, output pressure, and transducer performance five years in is not close to a practical product; the basic engineering guarantees it cannot be made practical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rLly4WdRAjY" title="More uBeam Ultrasonic Wireless Charging LOL&#x27;s!">•</a></sup>

Similar reasoning applies to a battery-extending boost converter: even granting that useful extra output can be extracted in favourable circumstances, the converter's own efficiency — perhaps 90% at the optimal point on the curve but more realistically 70 to 80% — plus a few hundred microamps of [[quiescent-current]], sets a hard ceiling well below marketing claims of large percentage improvements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Even a mechanical energy source is bounded the same way: an average person cycling flat out might generate around 500 W, and conversion efficiency reduces that to a couple of hundred watts of usable charging power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup>

Efficiency claims for whole systems deserve the same scrutiny. A solar-powered electric car priced at 267,000 US dollars is not dramatically more efficient than far cheaper alternatives — one competitor at roughly a tenth the price is only about 40% better than a conventional production EV — and no amount of on-body solar makes all-day solar driving realistic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 - Lightyear Zero Solar Powered Electric Car">1480</a></sup>

Efficiency is also conditional on operating point in non-electrical systems. A heat pump is only efficient within a narrow window of flow rate; too slow, too fast, or too large a temperature differential all degrade it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tA2qm1-BVCY" title="Pool Heat Pump Calibration Explained (Madimack)">•</a></sup>
