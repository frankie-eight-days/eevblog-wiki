An LCR meter is a test instrument that measures [[inductance]], [[capacitance]] and [[resistance]] by exciting a component with an AC signal and analysing the resulting [[voltage]] and [[current]], and it ranks alongside the [[multimeter]] and the [[oscilloscope]] as core lab equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> A multimeter will measure resistance and will usually make some attempt at capacitance, but inductance measurement is rare on multimeters and the capacitance ranges are comparatively crude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Beyond the three primary values, an LCR meter returns the secondary parameters that describe a real component — quality factor, dissipation factor, [[equivalent-series-resistance]] — and it does so at a choice of test frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup>

## How the measurement works

The instrument is essentially an AC signal source of selectable test [[frequency]] feeding the device under test through a series range [[resistor]], with the voltage across the terminals amplified and digitised for a [[microcontroller]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> That range resistor is effectively the source [[impedance]] of the internal AC [[voltage-source]], and its value differs between models, which is why nominally identical meters behave differently on awkward loads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> From the voltage and current magnitudes and their relative phase, everything else is computed: series and parallel equivalent circuits, Q and D — where D is the reciprocal of Q — and parallel resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> Even bench instruments costing ten or twenty thousand use the same fundamental technique.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup>

Phase angle is also what drives automatic component identification. A positive series reactance on the complex plane is inductive and a negative one is capacitive, so an LCR meter in auto mode decides what has been connected to it from the sign of the phase angle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup> The same architecture appears in impedance analyser form on general-purpose hardware, where a generator drives the DUT in series with a [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] and impedance is derived from the two measured voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup> An older relative is the LCR bridge, which reaches the same values through Wheatstone bridge nulling against internal reference components and, with fine vernier controls, can be both accurate and precise — good enough for component matching, though well past its prime against a modern handheld costing under a couple of hundred dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-677" title="EEVblog #677 - Mailbag">677</a></sup>

## Test frequency

Standard test frequencies step through 100 Hz, 120 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz and 100 kHz; the 120 Hz point exists to suit 60 Hz mains regions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup> A proper LCR meter produces a clean [[sine-wave]] at the exact selected frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup> The few multimeters that measure inductance, and multimeters measuring capacitance generally, do not: one produces a triangular test [[waveform]] at a little over 100 Hz whose frequency shifts with the capacitance being measured, though behaviour varies between meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup>

Frequency choice is not cosmetic, because a component's measured value depends on it. A 1,000 µF electrolytic measured out of circuit reads 855 µF at 100 Hz, essentially the same at 120 Hz, 820 µF at 1 kHz, refuses the measurement at 10 kHz — presenting as a short to a capacitance meter and reading OL — and comes back as 1,700 nF at 100 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> A loudspeaker woofer measures 3.6 Ω at 1 kHz and 8.8 Ω at 10 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - FREE Your Sonos Speaker! (HACK)">1519</a></sup> ESR is conventionally specified and measured at 100 kHz, which is the practical argument for insisting on a meter that reaches 100 kHz when buying one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> Combined 100 kHz and DC resistance capability can add substantially to the price, sometimes doubling it, but for anyone doing [[capacitor]] work it is the option worth paying for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

## Accuracy and specification

Accuracy is quoted as a table of figures per range, per frequency, rather than as a single number, and the best accuracy is obtained in the middle ranges — a headline basic accuracy such as 0.7% plus three digits applies only there, and this is true of almost every LCR meter on the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> Class figures span a wide range of instruments: an entry-level handheld at around $199, a 0.3% class handheld at about $330, and a 1990-vintage 0.1% class bench meter capable of [[transformer]] parameters and open/short compensation, compact enough at 4.4 kg to sit on a bench rather than in a rack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-229" title="EEVblog #229 - IET DE-5000 LCR Meter Teardown">229</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> Impedance analysers extend the same idea to characterising components across frequency, with versions reaching 10 MHz and 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup>

For [[calibration]] work the rule of thumb is to measure with an instrument an order of magnitude better than the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1496" title="EEVblog 1496 - Winning Mailbag">1496</a></sup> This is what makes vendor-measured values on reference capacitor sets only as trustworthy as the meter used: a mid-range handheld quoted at better than 0.5% may in practice be far better than that, but the buyer has no way to know, and it is not the same thing as a 0.1% benchtop instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1496" title="EEVblog 1496 - Winning Mailbag">1496</a></sup>

## Fixtures, leads and compensation

Test lead and fixture parasitics dominate at the extremes of both the resistance and capacitance ranges, so nulling before measurement is routine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Long thin test leads contribute on the order of an ohm of series resistance, which must be removed before an ESR reading means anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Fixture residuals also appear as capacitance: on one meter roughly 100 pF sits between the shorted sense point and the front banana terminals, and about 10 pF of residual remains with external leads fitted, which is why the supplied compensation procedure matters when working through the banana jacks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> Four-terminal Kelvin connection is available on some handhelds and is the reason a dedicated short-lead measurement fixture gives the more trustworthy result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Banana plug quality is itself a measurable variable — a soldered copper short across two plugs read 0.243 Ω and swung wildly with the slightest movement of the plug.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1052" title="EEVblog #1052 - BEWARE Crap Banana Plugs!">1052</a></sup>

## Measuring in circuit

Capacitors can be measured in circuit with an LCR meter more successfully than expected, which matters because failed electrolytics in power supplies are a dominant failure mode in consumer equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> The limiting mechanism is clipping: the meter's test voltage is applied to whatever else surrounds the capacitor, and 2 V peak-to-peak at 100 Hz is more than enough to clamp on back-to-back silicon junctions representing nearby active circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> In practice clipping begins somewhere around 10 to 15 µF at 100 Hz on a typical meter, meaning anything above roughly 10 µF measures acceptably in circuit while smaller values become unreliable and then meaningless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Conveniently, large electrolytics are exactly the parts one usually wants to check in circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> The threshold depends on the meter's range resistors, so each instrument must be characterised individually — one meter starts clipping at about 1 µF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> A parallel pure resistance across the capacitor is largely harmless because it does not shift the phase angle, and even 100 Ω in parallel can leave the capacitance reading unchanged; low-impedance reactive paths such as transformer windings are a different matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup>

Three practices make in-circuit measurement work. Set the function manually to capacitance or inductance rather than relying on auto mode, since surrounding circuit elements readily confuse the identification algorithm; forcing the range tells the meter what it is looking at and lets it do its best through the range resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup> Measure ESR at 100 kHz and subtract the lead resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> And if the meter offers a selectable drive level, use the lower setting — some LCR meters include a dedicated low-voltage mode specifically to avoid turning on active elements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup>

Diagnosis is not always a matter of reading a number. A thoroughly failed electrolytic may not be identified as a capacitor at all in auto mode, which is itself the verdict.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> The converse trap also exists: a capacitor that measures correctly on an LCR meter can still be leaky, harmless in most circuits but fatal in combination with, for example, a 1 MΩ resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Measurements taken at the meter's low test voltage do not necessarily describe behaviour at working voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-379" title="EEVBlog #379 - Yamaha RX-V557 Receiver Fix">379</a></sup> Class II ceramics make this explicit — a part measuring 8.65 µF on an LCR meter behaves as 7.8 µF under a scope-based charge measurement, and a Y5V dielectric can collapse toward a small fraction of its marked value under DC bias, so a nominal 10 µF reading on the bench predicts very little about the working circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

## Uses beyond L, C and R

Low-resistance capability is a large part of an LCR meter's bench value. Resolution down to 100 µΩ makes it the right tool for measuring cold DC resistance of fuses and for reading contact and conductor resistances in the single-digit milliohm region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup> The same low-ohms resolution allows short-circuit tracing across a board by following the resistance gradient to the offending part, a task that needs at least a 4.5- or 5.5-digit capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> Impedance mode serves for bulk-checking capacitors in a repair against expected 100 kHz values, and for characterising loudspeaker drivers whose nominal impedance ratings are approximate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - FREE Your Sonos Speaker! (HACK)">1519</a></sup> It answers empirical questions that datasheets do not, such as the actual inter-strip capacitance of a solderless [[breadboard]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> In test engineering it is the measuring core of automated fixtures, switched through [[relay]] matrices onto a common bus to test transmission lines, coils and insulation resistance in complex assemblies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1632" title="EEVblog 1632 - WHY sell 2CH Oscilloscopes? + Test Engineering">1632</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-38" title="EEVblog #38 - LCR Meters, Transmission Lines, and Moving goal posts">38</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-C_tXWwcyZlc" title="Dumpster HP3488A Switch Teardown + Silverbrook STORY">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-40" title="EEVblog #40 - Dilbert and the world of micro managed Engineering">40</a></sup> Driven from an LCR meter, piezoelectric elements audibly sing, which was used as a connectivity check in production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1400" title="EEVblog 1400 - Hard Drive Micro Actuators are AMAZING!">1400</a></sup>

## Form factors

Handheld probe-style meters are the general-purpose choice and are the recommended primary instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup> Tweezer-type meters put the measurement contacts directly on SMD parts and eliminate leads scattering components across the bench; the form factor is excellent for batch matching and rework, but as a general-purpose instrument a handheld with standard probes is more versatile, and tweezer attachments are available for handhelds anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup> The trade-off historically was price — a tweezer instrument at $320 street price — though inexpensive tweezer meters have since made having one hard to argue against.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

Very cheap tweezers and component testers occupy a different category. A $70 tweezer meter provides basic L, C and R plus [[diode]] testing and signal generation but no secondary parameters, has limited resolution and accuracy, and can misidentify a 1 MΩ resistance as a 9 pF capacitor in auto mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> The $7 [[transistor]]-tester-style component analyser is a capable component identifier and go/no-go tester giving ballpark figures, and should not be used as a substitute for a real LCR meter when quantitative measurements are needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1020" title="EEVblog #1020 - Is A $7 LCR / Component Tester Any Good?">1020</a></sup> Neither belongs as the only LCR instrument in a lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1020" title="EEVblog #1020 - Is A $7 LCR / Component Tester Any Good?">1020</a></sup>

## Construction

Internally these are modest, precision analogue designs rather than dense ones. A typical handheld comprises a high-resolution ADC — a substantial step up from the 4.5-digit dual-slope converters used in earlier generations — a handful of precision op amps, analogue muxes, an [[eeprom]] and extensive case shielding, which is common across the class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-234" title="EEVblog #234 - Agilent U1733C LCR Meter Teardown">234</a></sup> One design is notable for how little circuitry it contains, a minimalist approach with correspondingly little to go wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-229" title="EEVblog #229 - IET DE-5000 LCR Meter Teardown">229</a></sup> Much of the market shares heritage: many visually similar handhelds descend from a single original design house whose instruments were rebadged under multiple brands, and at least one manufacturer redesigned the internals from scratch while keeping the familiar exterior.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup>
