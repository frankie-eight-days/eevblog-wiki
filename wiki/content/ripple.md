Ripple is the residual AC component riding on a nominally DC supply rail, left over from rectification or from the switching action of a converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> It is distinct from, though usually measured alongside, high-[[frequency]] [[noise]]: on a captured [[waveform]] the low-frequency content is the ripple and the fast spikes on top are noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Because ripple is an AC quantity sitting on a DC average, a rail can measure correct on a meter's DC average and still be badly wrong — the DC number stays put while the AC component changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-379" title="EEVBlog #379 - Yamaha RX-V557 Receiver Fix">379</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

## Measurement

Ripple is measured AC-coupled, since the whole point is to strip the DC average and expand the small AC residue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> Triggering on it is awkward at low amplitudes; noise reject helps, and the mains AC line makes a usable trigger source for rectifier-derived ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Where the variation is small compared to the rail, window triggering set just outside the normal operating band will catch excursions that ordinary edge triggering cannot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> On high-[[voltage]] rails such as a bulk [[capacitor]] at several hundred volts, a differential probe at 100:1 keeps the measurement inside the scope's input range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup> Instrument power-analysis packages fold ripple measurement in alongside power modulation, safe operating area and switching loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup>

A dual-display meter showing AC and DC simultaneously is enough for a quick survey of many rails: a rail reading 5.3 V with 10 mV of AC, or 12.8 V with 26 mV, or 46 mV on another, is healthy and not worth pursuing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> Sub-volt logic core rails on a working board typically show no measurable ripple at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup>

Manufacturer specifications give the threshold that matters. The 5 V digital rail of a Tektronix 2465B is specified at 150 mV peak-to-peak; a measured 350 mV is out of spec and a legitimate suspect for misbehaviour elsewhere in the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup>

## Ripple as a diagnostic

Excess ripple on a rail points either to a degraded reservoir capacitor or to something drawing more [[current]] than the supply can deliver, and the two are distinguishable. A failed capacitor produces a large amount of ripple; if a rail has sagged badly — 4 V where 5 V is expected — but shows hardly any ripple, the capacitor is not the fault and the rail is being dragged down by excess load that the output winding cannot support.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The converse case, a rail carrying far more ripple than it should even after a much larger capacitor is fitted, indicates something loading it down rather than a filtering deficiency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup>

This is why blanket capacitor replacement on a board whose rails all measure clean is unwarranted: with no measurable ripple and correct voltages, the fault lies elsewhere, in a dry [[bga|BGA]] joint or the digital section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> Confirming a suspect capacitor directly is a matter of ESR, measured at 100 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

One important limitation: a board powered from a bench DC supply rather than from its own mains front end has no ripple to observe, so ripple-based reasoning about input bypass and reservoir capacitors does not apply in that configuration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup>

The consequence of ripple depends entirely on what the rail feeds. A rail feeding 5 V logic, or feeding a local 3.3 V regulator downstream, will generally tolerate tens of millivolts without trouble; failures traced to modest ripple usually involve some more subtle sensitivity in one particular part of the circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

## Sources of ripple

In a rectifier-and-capacitor supply the ripple frequency and shape carry information about the topology. A 4.[[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] showing 22 kHz ripple identifies the converter's [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] directly, and a second rise within each cycle betrays full-wave rectification from a second coil.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup> Where the ripple is at mains frequency, it originates in the rectified line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

In a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]], ripple amplitude tracks both load and operating mode: the ripple waveform visibly changes character as the converter shifts modes with load, and peak-to-peak amplitude varies with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> A converter measuring almost 200 mV peak-to-peak is high against a calculated target but not necessarily out of the ballpark; adding the intended output [[inductor]] and capacitor cleans the no-load ripple to the point where a 20 mV/div scale is needed to see it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

Charge pumps and voltage doublers ripple as a direct function of switching frequency, because the frequency sets how far the capacitors discharge between pumps. Raising the frequency makes the ripple disappear; lowering it brings back both ripple and droop, visible from around 4 kHz down, and 1 kHz is unusable. Around 10 kHz serves as a working rule of thumb for these circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup> Where the resulting DC feeds analog circuitry, a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] downstream is the usual remedy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

Inadequate [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] for the claimed output power leaves a supply unable to hold ripple down under load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-893" title="EEVblog #893 - Mailbag">893</a></sup> At the opposite extreme, more output capacitance is not simply better: the design target is the lowest output capacitance that still keeps ripple low, because stored charge in a large [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] is dumped into the load when the supply transitions into constant-current mode, before the control loop can respond.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Bulk capacitor voltage must also be chosen with the ripple trough in mind — a rail whose minimum sits at 46 V leaves plenty of margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

## Filtering and attenuation

Filtering ripple out is a chain, and the weakest link dominates. In a [[pwm|PWM]]-derived control voltage, removing output capacitance and reducing a filter capacitor allowed the full switching ripple to pass straight through to the output; adding a second RC stage of 10 kΩ and 100 nF dropped it very significantly even with almost no output filtering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> The cost is [[bandwidth]]: a 10 Hz filter cutoff means the [[output-voltage|output voltage]] cannot be changed quickly, which is acceptable for a manually adjusted knob but not for fast programming.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> As a design target for a control rail, 1 mV of ripple transferred to the output may be acceptable, but better than that is the sensible goal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

A [[capacitance]] multiplier — a pass [[transistor]] with an RC on its base — multiplies the effective filter capacitance by the transistor's current gain. With a 470 mV peak-to-peak input ripple at 1.59 kHz, the output came out around 310 mV, close to the −3 dB value of roughly 330 mV, so the achieved cutoff sat far above the 15.9 Hz predicted from the multiplied capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> At 100 Hz the residual is small but still visible at 2 mV/div.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The governing trade-off is the [[series-resistor|series resistor]]: raising it from 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ starves the transistor of base current and increases the [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] across the pass device, but improves ripple attenuation by the RC ratio, to the point where the ripple disappears into the [[noise-floor|noise floor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Replacing the single transistor with a Darlington pair raises the gain, permitting a larger [[resistor]] for a given capacitance and better low-frequency attenuation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

## Ripple and light output

In [[led|LED]] lighting, supply ripple appears directly as flicker, because [[led-current|LED current]] follows the rail. A driver using secondary-side constant-current regulation with substantial filter capacitance is not entirely ripple free but has little enough that it will not visibly flicker.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup> Linear LED drivers with no ripple on their output cannot produce strobing at all; where flicker is present in such an installation, PWM dimming rather than supply ripple is the mechanism.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup>

## Simulation

Ripple is visible in simulation as the steady-state condition that follows the initial ramp: the differential voltage across a pass element is higher during ramp-up than after settling, and what remains at steady state is the ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup>
