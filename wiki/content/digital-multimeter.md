A digital multimeter (DMM) is a single instrument containing several instruments at once, designed to measure multiple electrical parameters — it has the functions of a voltmeter measuring both AC and DC, an ohmmeter measuring [[resistance]], and an ammeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> It is the default measuring tool of electronics work, made in every form from pocket-sized units through the sort an electrician carries to the large benchtop instruments found in a higher-end lab, at prices spanning roughly $2 to $700 or $800 for a large advanced brand-name model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Regardless of type they are fundamentally similar in construction and operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Beyond hand-held and bench instruments, the DMM function is routinely embedded in other equipment: oscilloscopes carry a DVM alongside cursors, counters and mask testing,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> portable scopes are sold with a meter built in,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> and integrated benches include a 5.5-digit [[multimeter]] as one of several instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup>

## Display, counts and resolution

A typical meter has an [[lcd-display|LCD display]], usually four digits on lower-end units and possibly five on higher-end ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> The figure quoted is the count, which is simply the maximum value the reading can go up to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> A 6,000-count meter reads to 6,000, or 6.000 on a lower range; a 3,200-count meter measuring the same 3.1 V or 3.2 V shows the same number of digits, but at 3.3 V it ranges up and a digit of resolution is lost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> More counts is therefore better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Most meters carry a rotary selection dial to choose between functions — the origin of the name multimeter — although some substitute front-panel push buttons; a real [[range-switch|range switch]] has the practical advantage that its off position is unambiguous.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

Resolution scales upward through the bench classes. Five-and-a-half digits is about all that is wanted in a mid-range integrated instrument;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> on a dedicated bench DMM, five and a half digits is regarded as modest and six and a half the expected step up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1686" title="EEVblog 1686 - Electronex 2025 Melbourne - Walkaround">1686</a></sup> At the top, a 7.5-digit instrument such as the Keithley DMM7510 combines an 18-bit converter, a million samples per second update rate and 14 ppm accuracy for around $4,000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Resolution of that order is what makes fourth-decimal-place work possible: total changes of barely 100 microvolts in a cell's terminal [[voltage]] are visible on a 6.5-digit meter, and more than 6.5 digits are available in its trend chart mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup> Designing a 6.5- or even an 8.5-digit DMM requires some of the best engineering in the world.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 5 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Input impedance and circuit loading

A digital multimeter does not have infinite [[input-impedance|input impedance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> The standard figure is a nominal 10 megohms on most voltage ranges, AC or DC,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> and this is what nearly every meter presents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup> Connecting the meter across a circuit node therefore places that resistance in parallel with whatever is under test, exactly as an analog movement does, only with far less effect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

The consequence is quantifiable. Ten megohms across a 1 megohm [[resistor]] causes a measurement error whose size depends on the rest of the network; with a 100 k source resistance the error is roughly 1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> Measuring the midpoint of a matched 200 k divider fed with precisely 10 V gives a reading below the expected 5 V, though for that source [[impedance]] the error remains small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> The error grows with source resistance, so high-impedance circuits are where loading genuinely ruins a measurement and produces false readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> The same arithmetic governs circuits designed to drive a meter: 100 ohms in series with a 10 megohm input gives negligible error, and the meter impedance has to fall to about 100 k before that 100 ohms contributes even about 0.1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

Better meters, both bench units and some hand-helds, offer a second input impedance: a high-Z mode alongside the standard 10 megohms, which visibly reduces the load — the voltage on a 9 V [[alkaline-battery|alkaline cell]] rises when the meter switches out of 10 megohm mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup> In one uncommon direction the low impedance of an analog meter is the useful property and the DMM's high impedance is the trap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## Accuracy and its limits

Basic DC volts accuracy is the headline specification. An ordinary 3.5-digit meter should be at least 0.5% accurate, which is sufficient for tasks such as setting a lithium-ion charge voltage of 4.2 V to within the ±1% the chemistry demands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-919" title="EEVblog #919 - How To Charge Li-Ion/LiPo Batteries With A Power Supply">919</a></sup> Mid-range hand-helds do better: a compact 6,000-count meter specified at 0.2% basic DC volts plus three counts sits in that class,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-432" title="EEVblog #432 - Brymen Multimeters">432</a></sup> a vintage Gossen Metrawatt claims 0.25%,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> and a 1980s bench instrument such as the HP 3478A offers 5.5 digits with high stability, to the point that used examples can generally be expected to still meet their specifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup> Because the millivolt range is usually the most accurate range on a digital multimeter as well as the easiest to use, instrumentation designed to be read out on a meter is often scaled to land on it — for example targeting the 200 mV range of a 2,000-count meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

Specified accuracy is not the whole error budget. Temperature affects the meter more than it affects a good resistance standard: across a 4 °C change, a hand-selected aged standard shifted by about 0.0002 while the meter reading shifted by roughly 0.0008, four times greater, with the excess attributable to the meter or the interconnects rather than the resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-834" title="EEVblog #834 - Wekomm Resistance Standard">834</a></sup> Meters also drift over decades; an old manual-ranging unit can be found 2.6% out on one voltage range and 1.4% out on [[current]] while its resistance function remains respectable, sometimes correctable with an internal trim pot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup>

[[current-measurement|Current measurement]] introduces a thermal error of its own. A DMM measures current by using its voltmeter to read the drop across an internal shunt; after carrying high current for a period the shunt warms, its resistance increases, the voltage across it rises, and the displayed current creeps upward even though the actual current has not changed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gqzZHbEfWDU" title="KoradFollowup Charles">•</a></sup>

There are measurements a DMM simply cannot make. Its resistance and [[diode]] functions apply only two or three volts to the circuit, so an insulation punch-through that only occurs at high voltage — such as a fault putting mains onto the [[heat-sink|heat sink]] of an [[led|LED]] tube — cannot be found with a multimeter and requires a megohmmeter instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup>

## Displacement of the analog meter

Digital meters beat analog ones in almost every aspect, and the honest way to structure the comparison is to enumerate where the digital is better first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> The digital instrument states the voltage, resistance or current directly, whereas an analog meter requires interpreting the needle position and first zeroing the movement mechanically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

Resolution is the starkest gap: even a 3.5-digit 2,000-count display beats any analog meter, and even a $2 digital meter beats the resolution of a movement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> The needle itself has a width, and a full sweep contains perhaps a couple of hundred needle widths at best, so the last figure is a guess.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Analog scales are also not provided for every switch position, so a 25 V range must be read off the 250 V scale with the last digit dropped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

Accuracy follows. A classic analog meter specifies about 2% for DC volts, worse than practically the worst cheap digital meter on the market, and a better example such as the Triplett 630-NA manages only 1.5% on DC, degrading to around 3% on AC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Analog input impedance is not a fixed 10 megohms but varies with range as an ohms-per-volt figure — 20,000 ohms per volt DC means 20 k on the 1 V range and 200 k on the 10 V range — which is a trap and one of the major reasons digital meters took over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Only at the 500 V and 1,000 V ranges does such a meter reach the 10 to 20 megohms a digital meter offers everywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

The traditional defence of the analog meter is needle response to fast-changing signals, and modern fast-updating bar graphs answer it: Fluke were among the first with a fast-responding bar graph designed precisely to replicate and beat the analog movement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> A digital meter's bar graph tracks a 10 Hz and even a 20 Hz signal visibly, and at 5 Hz with a 1 V RMS signal the excursions are plainly readable — behaviour not obtainable on the analog meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> The other analog party trick, gauging [[capacitance]] from the decay of the needle, is superseded by a dedicated capacitance mode that is easier and more accurate and requires no calculation from a decay factor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Combined with ohms measurement, which analog meters are poor at, the digital meter wins on accuracy, price, resolution and usability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

The high input impedance that distinguishes the DMM was historically achieved by other means. A VTVM used its tubes to obtain a high input impedance comparable to today's 10 megohm digital meters; in the transition away from tubes in the 1970s and 1980s the FET VOM substituted field-effect transistors for the valves to get the same active high-impedance front end, at the cost of needing a [[battery]] or mains supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup> FET analog meters accordingly do reach 10 megohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

## Historical development

The first commercial DMM was made in 1953 by Non-Linear Systems, using grain-of-wheat bulbs and edge-lit lucite digits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup> Early instruments were coarse by later standards: one of the first digital multimeters, advertised at the end of 1970, offered 17 ranges at 0.1% with no LED display at its price point,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup> and a 1978 advertisement touted 1% accuracy, 12 separate ranges and a three-digit 0-to-999 display, not even three and a half digits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-142" title="EEVblog #142 - Electronics Magazine Memories">142</a></sup> A period construction-project digital voltmeter used state-of-the-art LED displays and the Fairchild 3814 LSI, equivalent to 3,000 transistors, for a 4.5-digit readout with only three DC voltage ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup>

Analog meters remained predominant in retail advertising in 1985, when the cheapest digital alternative was $59.50 against $10 for a technician's 10 k ohms-per-volt analog cheapy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup> By 1990 digital multimeters had come to dominate, and analog meters were relegated to legacy sales.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup> [[true-rms|True RMS]] measurement was a notable capability well before it was commonplace, and its absence was what construction projects for true RMS AC voltmeters existed to remedy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-142" title="EEVblog #142 - Electronics Magazine Memories">142</a></sup> Fully manual-ranging meters were common into the 1980s;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup> most digital multimeters are auto-ranging today, while manual-ranging digital meters remain available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

## Functions and interfaces

Beyond the core voltage, resistance and current functions, mid-range hand-helds add temperature, a 60 mV DC range, microamps, a crest or min-max capture mode, and in some cases a built-in [[electric-field|electric field]] detector for locating live wires and power points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-432" title="EEVblog #432 - Brymen Multimeters">432</a></sup> Min-max capture has become common enough that other instruments describe their own V min and V max readings by analogy with a multimeter's min-max mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Continuity with a fast, latching beeper is a basic expectation,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> and dedicated jigs exist to exercise it, feeding pulses of adjustable mark-space ratio into a meter to see whether it beeps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1681" title="EEVblog 1681 - Mailbag: Nice DIY CC Load,  Amazing Proto Boards, Ian Johnston">1681</a></sup> Almost all meters include an automatic power-off timer of a few minutes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

True RMS versus average-responding is a standing product distinction. The Fluke 70 series were average-responding meters, and the line has effectively been discontinued in favour of the 170 series — the 175, 177 and 179 — leaving the 70 series to legacy customers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup> The difference can be small in hardware terms: an average-responding Fluke 77-IV can be converted to a true RMS 177 for around ten cents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup>

Digital interfaces exist but are not always convenient. Brymen's 6,000-count meters publish an [[rs-232|RS232 protocol]] that, rather than emitting the reading as a numeric string, outputs the [[lcd|LCD]] digit segments and maps them, requiring the host to decode the display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup> The meter form factor has also been extended into other measurement types, including LCR tweezers built as an all-in-one instrument with a [[usb|USB]] port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1375" title="EEVblog #1375 - Mailbag: IEEE">1375</a></sup>

## Notable instruments

- **Keithley DMM7510** — 7.5-digit bench multimeter, 18-bit converter, one million samples per second, 14 ppm accuracy, around $4,000; described as one of the best obtainable, and capable of resolving down through nanoamps into picoamps on its lowest current range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup>
- **HP 3478A** — 1980s 5.5-digit bench multimeter, notably stable and still likely to meet specification decades later.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup>
- **Prema 6047** — unusual late-1980s integrating digital multimeter with 7.5 digits, using an LM399-class reference (an LM299 in the unit examined), the same family of reference found in the HP 3458A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup>
- **Fluke 17B** — a very basic meter, sold only in China and India, but well built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup>
- **Brymen [[bm235|BM235]]** — a true RMS DMM produced as an EEVblog-branded variant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AzIuayXzCvs" title="Brymen / EEVblog BM235 Multimeter Unboxing">•</a></sup>
