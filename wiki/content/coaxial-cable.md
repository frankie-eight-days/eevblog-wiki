Coaxial cable is a shielded [[transmission-line|transmission line]] with a center conductor surrounded by an outer braid or solid shield, used to carry RF and high-[[frequency]] signals while containing their electromagnetic fields.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup> It matters because, above low frequency, it cannot be treated as an ideal wire: length, characteristic [[impedance]], termination, dielectric, and shield integrity determine amplitude, phase, delay, [[noise]] pickup, and emissions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup>

## Transmission-line behaviour and termination

A coaxial cable has a characteristic impedance rather than merely a lumped impedance, and this should not be confused with the impedance of a circuit element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> In common 50 ohm systems, both source and load are expected to be 50 ohms; spectrum-analyser tracking-generator output and RF input are typical examples.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

When a 50 ohm coax is left unterminated at either end, the open end reflects energy back along the cable, producing standing waves, phase rotation, and frequency-dependent addition or cancellation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> A pure [[sine-wave|sine wave]] can still be reflected from an open coax end and return at a phase that depends on frequency, so the measured amplitude can collapse, double, or vary strongly across frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> Adding even a short open coax stub to a correctly terminated measurement can make a signal nearly vanish at one frequency while leaving nearby frequencies almost unaffected; demonstrations showed cancellation effects around 47 MHz and 57 MHz and large amplitude/phase anomalies near 35 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>

Different frequency components can experience different phase and group delays in air, coax, or other media, so a [[square-wave|square wave]]’s harmonics can return with different delays and phases after reflection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> Coax [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]] is on the order of nanoseconds per metre; one worked example used 5.05 ns/m, predicting about 227 ps for a 45 mm length mismatch, while the [[oscilloscope]] skew correction measured about 262 ps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>

A series termination [[resistor]] at the source end can remove the [[ringing]] seen with direct coax connection, which is why a plain BNC-to-banana or direct coax hookup can be misleading at high frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> The correct ways to measure a function-generator output amplitude over frequency are a properly terminated 50 ohm coax path or a high-frequency 10× [[oscilloscope-probe|oscilloscope probe]], not an arbitrary length of unterminated coax.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope probing

Direct coax can be a very-high-[[bandwidth]], unity-gain probing method, but it presents low impedance at high frequency and loads the circuit with the cable [[capacitance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> A metre of coax was approximated as about 100 pF for loading calculations, with the exact value depending on cable type.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup>

Ordinary coax is not interchangeable with a ×1 oscilloscope-probe cable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> Regular coax measures essentially 0 ohms end-to-end because the center conductor is a direct copper path, whereas scope-probe coax is deliberately built as a lossy transmission line and can measure a few hundred ohms, with examples around 230 ohms and 330 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup> The lossy construction uses a resistive, nichrome-like center conductor with a deliberate wiggle, matched to the [[probe-compensation|probe compensation]] network so reflections are damped without requiring [[50-ohm-termination|50 ohm termination]] at the scope input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> Lossy coaxials are credited to John Kobbe at Tektronix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

A simple resistive, Z0, or transmission-line probe can be made from coax such as RG174, a BNC, and a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] at the tip, commonly 1 kΩ, with the braid going to the [[ground]] tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup> The series resistor isolates the cable capacitance from the node under test, giving very low [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]]; correctly constructed and terminated versions are claimed to rival the signal-integrity performance of far more expensive probes into the gigahertz range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

## Noise pickup, resonance, and EMC

Coax [[inductance]] together with oscilloscope input capacitance forms an LC tank; back-of-envelope values for typical coax and scope input capacitance place resonance around roughly 100–120 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14" title="EEVblog #14 - An unusual oscilloscope phenomenon!">14</a></sup> Broadband static energy, such as that generated by standing up from a chair, can excite this tank through the coax and produce a sinusoidal response even when the probe tip is shorted or a 50 ohm terminator is fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14" title="EEVblog #14 - An unusual oscilloscope phenomenon!">14</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-20" title="EEVblog #20 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon Part 2">20</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21" title="EEVblog #21 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon - Part 3">21</a></sup> The effect is therefore not primarily a ground-loop artifact, because it persists with shorted probe tips, plain coax, terminated coax, and 50 ohm input termination enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-20" title="EEVblog #20 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon Part 2">20</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21" title="EEVblog #21 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon - Part 3">21</a></sup>

Any breach in [[shielding]] can turn internal noise into conducted emissions: a BNC through an otherwise sealed metal chassis lets [[switching-noise|switching noise]] couple onto the signal and onto the coax, carrying interference out along the copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> Coax quality and routing matter for common-mode noise pickup, and a noisy switching supply coupled into a coax run can dominate a measurement until the source is removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup>

Triaxial cable is used where ordinary coax shielding is insufficient, especially low-leakage, low-[[current-measurement|current measurement]] systems; it looks similar to coax externally but adds another shielding/guard layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> In guarded electrometer-style circuitry, driving the guard at essentially the same potential as the high-impedance node removes the [[voltage]] differential that would otherwise cause [[leakage-current|leakage current]], while the outer coax earth goes to system earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

Cable vibration and shock can legitimately inject charge through triboelectric effects, so sensitive [[current]] measurements may need to check whether cable movement is contributing signal; in one photon-counting setup no significant triboelectric contribution was observed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup> Mechanical twisting of a coax has also been observed to produce impulse-like artifacts on a scope in at least one bench anomaly, although the supplied context does not establish the mechanism.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2FlQnPsZCdE" title="Scope WTF!">•</a></sup>

## Construction variants and practical uses

Rigid and semi-rigid coax are used where controlled impedance, shielding, or thermal/mechanical stability is needed. RG402 is a 50 ohm rigid coax with a solid outer shield, available pre-terminated with SMA connectors, and a short length can form the loop of a low-cost H-field EMC probe when paired with a small wideband preamplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1178" title="EEVblog #1178 - Build a $10 DIY EMC Probe">1178</a></sup> Semi-rigid coax is used for high-frequency lines in cryogenic quantum hardware, but the inner conductor is poorly thermally anchored compared with the outer body, so loops are used because the PTFE dielectric contracts and pulls during cooldown; the cable body is bulkhead-mounted and thermally anchored to gold-plated plates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup> At megahertz nuclear-spin excitation frequencies, coaxial-line losses are much lower than at about 40 GHz electron-spin frequencies, allowing substantially more power to be delivered despite a much smaller gyromagnetic ratio.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup>

DC power can be superimposed on an RF coax line to feed remote amplifiers, as in TV masthead amplifiers and counter-surveillance front ends: DC is tapped through an [[inductor]]/resistor network while the RF path is AC-coupled, and measurable [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] can occur along the coax feed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-956" title="EEVblog #956 - Countersurveillance Monitor Teardown">956</a></sup>

Inside instruments, coax is used where [[pcb-routing|PCB routing]] would compromise shielding, impedance, leakage, or layout. High-bandwidth oscilloscopes route front-panel BNCs into high-quality coax and then directly into hybrid front-end and ADC assemblies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-342" title="EEVblog #342 - Agilent 90000 Oscilloscope Teardown">342</a></sup> RF signal generators may run coax inside a full metal tube rather than relying on the braid alone for shielding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> A low-leakage [[transformer]] can use heavily shielded coaxial windings on the primary side with ordinary enamelled wire on the secondary, relying on magnetic coupling through ferrite toroids.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Analog TV transmission facilities use large rigid coax and motor-driven RF switches, with waveguide sections where hollow guide replaces an inner conductor and transitions back to coax at splitters and antennas.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-569" title="EEVblog #569 - Tour of an Analog TV Transmission Facility">569</a></sup>

Coax capacitance is useful diagnostically: a capacitance meter can estimate cable length or locate a break, with one 10 m coax measuring 2.66 nF before an introduced break.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Conversely, vintage live-chassis televisions present a safety trap because the [[antenna]] coax shield and [[connector]] shell can be at mains potential despite appearing to be an ordinary RF connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup>
