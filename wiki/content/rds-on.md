RDS(on) is the [[resistance]] measured between the drain and source terminals of a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] when the gate is driven hard enough to turn the device fully on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The name comes from the two terminals it is measured across, with the gate acting as the control element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> It is the parameter that decides how much [[voltage]] a switching MOSFET drops and how much power it burns, and alongside drain-source voltage rating and [[current]] rating it is one of the three specifications that matter for a device used purely as a switch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup>

A MOSFET switched on behaves as a [[resistor]] rather than as a saturated junction, and RDS(on) is the MOSFET counterpart of VCE(sat) in a [[bipolar-transistor|bipolar transistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> The much lower on-resistance available from MOSFETs is a principal reason they displaced BJTs in switching roles: lower on-resistance means lower [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]], and therefore more current and more power handling from a given package size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

## Dependence on gate drive

RDS(on) is not a single number; it is always quoted at a specified gate-source voltage, and data sheets conventionally give two operating points, commonly 10 V and 4.5 V or 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> A part specified at 9 mΩ maximum with 10 V of gate drive typically degrades to something in the region of 15 mΩ when driven from 5 V logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Manufacturers also print an RDS(on) versus VGS curve, which shows the resistance rising steeply as drive is reduced; by the time gate drive falls to around 2 V, a typical jellybean part has little left to give.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

Data sheets list both typical and maximum values, and the distinction becomes important when a candidate part is marginal against the requirement rather than comfortably inside it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Logic-level parts that hold low resistance at reduced drive are the ones worth stocking for [[microcontroller]]-driven applications, since a 3.3 V output must be able to turn the gate on by itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

## Temperature behaviour

RDS(on) rises as the device heats.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> Live measurement of a MOSFET in operation shows the effect directly: a device sitting at roughly 3.1 mΩ drifts upward to 3.2 mΩ as it warms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1753" title="EEVblog 1753 - Designing a 2000V Isolated Oscilloscope (Cleverscope)">1753</a></sup> Any burden-voltage or dissipation figure calculated from a cold data-sheet value therefore describes the best case, valid only at low currents before self-heating sets in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

## Package, dissipation and typical values

For a given silicon technology, moving up in package size buys both lower RDS(on) and greater [[power-dissipation|power dissipation]] capability, because there is more surface area to get the heat out; the step from SOT-23 to SO-8 is the routine example.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Dissipation follows directly from the on-resistance and the drain current as an I²R loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

The spread across ordinary parts is very wide:

- Small-signal jellybean N-channel devices in SOT-23 sit around 2.4 Ω at VGS of 10 V, advertised as 2.5 Ω, which is high for a MOSFET and rises further at lower gate voltages. Such a part is adequate for a couple of hundred milliamps and no more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>
- The traditional P-channel counterpart is rated at 50 V with a comparatively wimpy 10 Ω at ±5 V of gate drive and around 130 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>
- Better modern SOT-23 parts reach tens of milliohms rather than ohms — around 26 mΩ at 10 V drive and still roughly 48 mΩ at only 2.5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> One such device carries 3 A at 2.5 V drive with 24 mΩ typical and 48 mΩ maximum, which works out to only about 0.2 W of dissipation at that current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>
- Larger SMD parts reach roughly 18 mΩ at both 10 V and 4 V of gate drive, with double-digit amp ratings that are impractical to exploit in a SOT-23 outline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>
- A TO-220 part specified at 35 mΩ at 4 V drive and 21 A handles 46 A continuous and 160 A pulsed, dissipating 3.8 W in free air and considerably more once bolted to a [[heat-sink|heatsink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

On-resistance is also a headline marketing parameter for high-voltage power devices, where best-in-class RDS(on) and ultra-low gate charge are the claims made for 650 V parts in TO-247 packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-U_QoWa6qXeo" title="EEVsmoke #1 - Magic Component Smoke">•</a></sup>

RDS(on) is specified for integrated switches as well as discrete devices. Open-drain shift-register drivers, for example, are characterised at around 7 Ω typical with 250 mA of current capability,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> and the on-resistance of the output MOSFETs inside a display driver forms part of the series-resistor calculation for the segments it drives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup>

## Contribution to measurement error

In current-measurement instruments the on-resistance of a range-switching MOSFET adds directly to the shunt and therefore to [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> A big N-channel MOSFET switching a 10 mΩ shunt must have an on-resistance well below that shunt value, otherwise the effective shunt resistance is more than doubled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> Because a MOSFET that is on behaves as a plain resistor, it conducts in both directions, which is what allows the same arrangement to measure AC current or to tolerate probes inserted the wrong way round.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

The arithmetic is straightforward. On a 5 A range with a 100 mΩ shunt giving 50 mV, a MOSFET of 5 mΩ adds a further 25 mV, and an [[hrc-fuse|HRC fuse]] of perhaps 10 to 20 mΩ cold contributes another 50 mV, for a true terminal burden voltage in the region of 125 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The MOSFET's contribution scales with current, so on the 500 mA range the same device drops only 2.5 mV and ceases to matter, while the fixed [[fuse]] resistance dominates less and less as the ranges go up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

The same effect appears in [[battery]]-profiling instruments where a MOSFET shorts out a high-value sense resistor: the MOSFET's RDS(on) ends up in series with the remaining 100 mΩ sense element and must be calibrated out in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> A separate sense line would avoid the problem but requires a multiplexer to switch it, which is why the simpler arrangement is common.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Choosing a replacement part

For a device used as a switch, essentially every other specification on the [[datasheet|data sheet]] can be ignored apart from voltage, current and RDS(on).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Among parts of comparable voltage and current rating, on-resistance figures almost always land within a fraction of an order of magnitude of one another, and 10 to 20% is near enough for a repair; gate charge and drive [[capacitance]] need attention only if a part is unusually bad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup> For a 500 V, 18 A power-factor-correction device, matching the voltage rating, the current rating and an approximately similar RDS(on) is sufficient, though a [[ground]]-up design would warrant more care.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup> Voltage rating is the one specification that should never be skimped on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup>

Where the original is heavily over-specified for its application, a substitute with somewhat higher on-resistance is acceptable: a MOSFET rated at 12 A but never seeing anything close to that will run slightly warmer without the difference being detectable by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Replacing a 17 A part with a 30 V, 12 A device pulled from scrap is on this basis unremarkable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>

The difficulty in parametric searching is rarely RDS(on) alone but the conjunction of constraints. Finding another MOSFET with integral ESD protection is easy; finding one in the same package, at the same voltage and current, and with comparable RDS(on) as well, can prove impossible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Parametric tables compound the problem, since an on-resistance column expressed in milliohms is easily misread as a current rating at a glance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>
