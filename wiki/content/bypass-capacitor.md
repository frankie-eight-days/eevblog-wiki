A bypass capacitor is a [[capacitor]] placed between the power and ground pins of an integrated circuit, as close to those pins as the layout allows, to act as a local reservoir of charge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> When a digital circuit switches, it takes a sudden gulp of [[current]], and that current has to come from somewhere; the purpose of the bypass capacitor is to supply it locally rather than letting it be drawn from the far side of the board through all the series [[inductance]] and [[resistance]] of the power distribution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The industry rule of thumb is a 100 nanofarad — 0.1 microfarad — capacitor next to each IC, and it is one of the few conventions in electronics applied almost universally without further analysis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Why the current gulp exists

Bypass capacitors matter most for circuits that take large amounts of current when they transition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> The demand is not only from resistive loads. Every trace has [[capacitance]] to ground, and every logic input has capacitance — even a couple of picofarads — so when a signal transitions the load momentarily appears as a [[short-circuit]] and draws a gulp of current from the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> This happens on every edge, in a circuit with no resistive loads at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup>

Without local bypassing, the supply rail at the chip sags and rings. With the bypass capacitor removed entirely and a 50-ohm load being driven, a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] was seen swinging roughly two and a half volts either side of nominal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Depending on the trace impedances, the dip can drop below the operating [[voltage]] of the chip and produce strange behaviour, and the ringing on an output signal can push a receiving gate through its threshold and corrupt data, or drive a flip-flop into a metastable state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The resistance of the traces is not the main problem; the inductance is, and it becomes dominant as switching speeds rise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

## Impedance, inductance and resonance

A bypass capacitor is only as good as its [[impedance]] at the [[frequency]] of interest, and what is wanted is the lowest possible impedance over the widest possible frequency range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup> Real capacitors have series inductance — inside the part, in the [[pcb]] traces, and in the bonding wires inside the chip — so impedance falls to a minimum at the self-resonant frequency and rises again above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> Above resonance the part may as well not be there: the capacitance is still present and the energy is still stored, but the series impedance prevents it being delivered when the IC calls for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

Measured impedance curves make the effect concrete. A 47 microfarad electrolytic resonates somewhere around 8 or 9 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> A typical 100 nanofarad 0805 part, the one found in practically every product, resonates around 5 or 6 MHz, and at 100 kHz its impedance is already over 15 ohms — no good at low frequencies at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> Electrolytics in general have low impedance over only a narrow range, which is why they are poor choices for bypassing an IC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> Package inductance figures reach down into the hundreds of picohenries, and parts built specifically for low equivalent series inductance — reverse geometry capacitors, with the conductive end caps on the sides rather than the ends — are used where bypassing performance is genuinely critical, such as high-frequency switch mode supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> The same reasoning explains the preference for wide capacitor bodies over narrow ones on high-current processor modules.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-882" title="EEVblog #882 - Dumpster Dive Apple Xserve Computers">882</a></sup>

Modern logic switches at hundreds of megahertz with edges producing frequency components into the gigahertz range, which is precisely the region where an ordinary bypass capacitor has stopped behaving like one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

## Multiple values in parallel

Because each capacitor covers a limited band, it is common to see two, three or four capacitors in parallel on one [[power-rail]] — a 1 microfarad, a 100 nanofarad, a 10 nanofarad, a 1 nanofarad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> Processor data sheets frequently specify exactly this: a typical bypass configuration of three different values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup> Lattice recommends 100 nanofarad and 10 nanofarad capacitors per device power pin as a rule of thumb for their programmable logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> On dense [[fpga]] layouts the three sizes are visible as three physical package sizes — 1206, 0805 and 0603 — each covering a different part of the spectrum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

The mechanism is demonstrable. In a switching demonstration, a 100 nanofarad capacitor and a 2.2 nanofarad capacitor together outperformed either alone, the smaller part taking care of the higher-frequency components while the whole arrangement interacted with lead inductance and circuit parasitics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> A 0.1 microfarad part alone removed the high-frequency ripple but not the lower-frequency droop, so both capacitors were required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> Bulk capacitance — a 330 microfarad electrolytic, for instance — serves the low-frequency events such as mains ripple and general charge storage for the whole board, while the small ceramics next to each IC handle the switching transitions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup>

The multiple-value convention is not absolute. In some cases the multiple values are a detriment and a single larger bulk value is the better choice, particularly for undemanding applications such as bypassing the input of a DC-to-DC converter, where an additional 47 nanofarad part alongside the bulk ceramics contributes nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup> Where the resonances of the capacitors combine with trace inductance at just the wrong point, the bypassing itself can cause a problem at a specific clock frequency — uncommon, but it does happen, and it is a reason not to go overboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Placement, loop area and layout

Placement is at least as important as value. The capacitor should sit hard against the power pin it serves, because the inductance of the traces from an outlying capacitor to the power pins erodes its effectiveness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Bypass capacitors are properly placed early in a layout, immediately after component placement, before signal routing begins, because they are important and because leaving them until last forces compromises.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

Loop area is the governing quantity. The loop includes the signal path, its return through the ground system, and the power system with the bypass capacitors in it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Splitting a [[ground-plane]] so that the return current from a bypass capacitor has to detour around the split enlarges the loop dramatically and turns the board into a radiator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The fix at the capacitor is direct: capacitor pad to [[via]], via straight down to the plane, with the shortest possible path from pad to via.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

On BGA devices the caps go on the bottom side of the board directly under the chip, on a double-sided load, with vias straight up to the balls, which is the only arrangement that bypasses individual pins with acceptable inductance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Placing them around the outside of a BGA instead gives terrible loop areas and diminished effectiveness, and the resulting star arrangement of capacitors under an FPGA is a recognisable layout signature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> Where the board must remain single-sided load for cost or assembly reasons, the bypassing strategy has to be rethought rather than simply pushed outward into neat rows on the top side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup> A grid of many small capacitors laid out in tidy rows around a chip, all in line for the sake of layout aesthetics, is often the visible result of a [[schematic]] that specified a capacitor per pin combined with a layout constraint that made proper placement impossible — and no pushback from the PCB designer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup>

Bypass capacitor placement also drives via budget and routing order. On a design where the bypass vias were dropped straight to the power and ground layers early, the remaining space could be used to route the critical data, clock and control lines first, with low-priority lines such as I²C routed last.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Routing around densely placed bypass capacitors becomes ugly quickly, particularly when via sizes are constrained; a 0.3 mm via is about the practical minimum on an inexpensive board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

Capacitors are also added, removed and repositioned during layout rather than being fixed at schematic time — one eliminated because two chips sit close enough to share, another added because power arrives on both sides of a chip — with the changes back-annotated to the schematic afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-245" title="EEVblog #245 - PSU Design Part 10 - PCB Layout Editing">245</a></sup>

## Power planes are not a substitute

The copper planes of a multilayer board do form a capacitor, and on a two-layer board the top and bottom layers act as one large bypass capacitor in effect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Ground and VCC are effectively the same node at high frequency precisely because the bypass capacitors tie them together, which is one reason the planes are stacked close to each other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup>

But plane capacitance cannot replace a discrete part. Measured plane impedance is not particularly low, even though the capacitive response is proper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> The capacitance of a large plane is spread across its entire surface, while a chip needs to bypass between power and ground at one specific point, and the local capacitance available at that point is very small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> The inductance spreads out across the plane as well — a phenomenon called inductance spreading — and that inductance dominates over the small amount of capacitance available locally, which is what makes planes ineffective as bypass capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> A plane totalling tens of nanofarads cannot stand in for an actual 10 nanofarad capacitor at the pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup>

## Are they necessary

The question of whether bypass capacitors are actually required has been tested directly by removing every one from a working board. On a 6.25 MHz, 5 V retro computer, every bypass capacitor on the board was snipped out — one per chip — along with the bulk input electrolytic, and the machine continued to run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The eye diagram on the data lines remained good with no bypassing at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> Some individual signals did show glitching that resoldering the local capacitors improved, but other artefacts traced to a fundamental ground-impedance problem on the double-sided board that no amount of bypassing could fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

The conclusion drawn is not that bypassing is unnecessary. Shipping a product with no bypass capacitors would require qualification across the supply tolerance range and over temperature, and is not recommended; bypass capacitors can be absolutely critical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The narrower point is that not every capacitor a designer specifies by reflex is earning its place, and that a full analysis of the power delivery network requires sophisticated and expensive simulation tools that most projects do not use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Slow boards genuinely need very little: 1970s and 1980s equipment running at hundreds of kilohertz to a few megahertz frequently carries only bulk decoupling per card rather than a capacitor per chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> Conversely, fast logic families demand more — 74F series parts draw enough current that each one needs its own bypass capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup>

At the other extreme, large FPGAs take enormous gulps of current at power-up, which is why vendors publish application notes running to dozens or a hundred pages on power delivery and bypassing alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> A small FPGA with a single rail and no significant inrush is a different problem entirely, and two bypass capacitors may more than cover it — one for the core voltage and one for the I/O voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

## Failure modes and diagnostics

Bypass capacitors are a standard suspect when a power rail is shorted, because ceramic capacitors are fairly notorious for shorting out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> When a rail measures a dead short, removing bypass capacitors is a reasonable place to begin the brute-force search, since desoldering a capacitor risks far less than desoldering an SO-package chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> The technique does not always succeed: on a 3.3 V rail measuring around 0.1 ohm, every bypass capacitor on the rail was removed and the short remained, leaving the semiconductors as the culprits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Physical failure is also visible — a bypass capacitor on an [[asic]] rail that has shorted and burned itself out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup> Excessive ripple on a rail can point to a failed bypass capacitor for that rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup>

Charge stored in the bypass capacitors keeps a circuit alive after power is disconnected — on a low-power [[microcontroller]] board, for several seconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> This has a direct design consequence in protection circuits: a latch-up detector that kills power must be placed ahead of the bypassing, because bypass capacitors downstream will continue dumping their stored energy into the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HBLKxC4JzTA" title="Space Electronics Circuit Protection">•</a></sup>

Bypassing interacts with measurement, too. The capacitance of an [[oscilloscope-probe]] is itself enough to make a marginally bypassed circuit start or stop working, in either direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> For measuring supply ripple, the measurement point of choice is directly across the bypass capacitor of the supply under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

Missing bypassing produces characteristic signatures on a rail: on a keyboard controller, the 6 MHz crystal frequency was visible as a wiggle riding on the power rail purely from lack of bypassing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> An unrelated but instructive case is the 555 timer control pin, where omitting the recommended 10 nanofarad capacitor to ground produced a bizarre modulation confined to a narrow band around 55.5 kHz that vanished the moment the capacitor was fitted and returned when it was removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup>

## Bypass capacitors as a diagnostic cue

The pattern of bypass capacitors on a board is itself readable information. A cluster of capacitors on the underside of a board is a reliable indicator of a BGA on the top side; their absence suggests there is no large BGA there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-342" title="EEVblog #342 - Agilent 90000 Oscilloscope Teardown">342</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> Two capacitors flanking a small package with heavy traces in and out — an input bypass and an output bypass — identify a [[voltage-regulator]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> The converse holds: a three-pin device with no bypass capacitors near it is not a regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup> A capacitor next to every chip in a row of small packages suggests digital logic rather than analog circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup> A capacitor tied from a power pin to ground on an unidentified part confirms which pin is the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-V0RWwSw96Sw" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL - Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> Even sealed [[crystal-oscillator]] modules were found to contain an internal bypass capacitor between the power and ground pins, in two of two devices examined, without the data sheets mentioning it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup>

Densely packed bypass capacitors also force layout compromises elsewhere: on a densely bypassed socket area, [[silkscreen]] designators cannot be placed next to each part and must be grouped or relocated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-720" title="EEVblog #720 - Mailbag">720</a></sup>

## Related uses of the term

The word bypassing also covers capacitors placed across components other than supply pins. A large bias [[resistor]] can be bypassed with a capacitor to suppress its thermal noise, and precision voltage references often provide a dedicated noise-reduction pin for exactly this purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup> [[series-resistor]] networks in a high-voltage differential probe carry small parallel capacitors to compensate the divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> A 100 nanofarad value happens to be both the standard bypass value and the value required for a one-second time constant with a 10 megohm resistor, so the part is often already in the [[bill-of-materials]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup>

In op-amp work the bypass configuration itself can measurably change performance in ways that are not fully explicable from the [[datasheet|data sheet]]. On a chopper-stabilised amplifier, changing between two 100 nanofarad rail capacitors and a single capacitor across the supplies altered the measured [[offset-voltage]] and its behaviour over the supply range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-476" title="EEVblog #476 - Opamp Offset Voltage Measurement">476</a></sup> In another case, adding bypass capacitors from each rail to a virtual ground stopped an oscillation, at the cost of loading the amplifier output capacitively through a series resistor — a fix that works but is not the preferred solution, and bypassing the divider resistors that form the virtual reference was investigated as an alternative.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup>

Some parts need remarkably little. A GSM-hardened audio amplifier designed for RF immunity worked with nothing more than a single bypass capacitor, no RF filtering and no low-dropout regulator on its input, in an application where conventional parts had required RF traps at every rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-23" title="EEVblog #23 - GSM mobile phone audio design">23</a></sup> Low-dropout regulators, by contrast, depend on output capacitance for stability and can oscillate without the correct capacitance, value and ESR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> Some precision references need neither: no output capacitor is required and a capacitive load is tolerated, though bypass capacitors elsewhere on the supply rail do no harm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>
