A trimmer potentiometer, or trim pot, is a small adjustable [[resistor]] mounted on a circuit board and set with a screwdriver rather than a knob. It exists to absorb the tolerance of the parts around it — to pull a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]], a gain, a bias level or an [[oscillator]] [[frequency]] onto its nominal value once, at manufacture or service, and then be left alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> It is the physical embodiment of analogue [[calibration]], and its steady disappearance from new designs in favour of digital adjustment is one of the clearest markers separating older instruments from modern ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup>

## What trimmers adjust

The most common job is setting a reference. A Fluke 27 uses a trim pot alongside its 1.23 V reference to set the value that feeds the ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup> A Krohn-Hite DC [[voltage]] standard labels its first adjustment as pot number one on the board itself; it sets 6.5 mA through a zener, and the resulting measured drop is the source of the instrument's stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> A rubidium frequency standard, for all its 0.1 ppm tolerance and 0.01 ppm aging, still carries an adjustment trimmer, because the physics package must be trimmed to the correct frequency before its stability means anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup>

Gain and offset are the next most frequent targets. Two resistor trim pots on an [[oscilloscope]] [[current]] probe set the gain of each channel, one per sensor, because the spread between individual Hall-effect sensors is too wide to be handled by fixed resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> A 741-derived op-amp such as the OP07 has offset pins that accept a trimmer, taking the part to better specifications than its untrimmed figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1436" title="EEVBlog 1436 - The TOP 5 Jellybean OPAMP&#x27;s">1436</a></sup> A 500 Ω trim pot installed as the bias resistor at an op-amp input allows the [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] to be nulled out entirely, although the null holds only at the particular supply voltage and common-mode range at which it was set.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup>

Bias, sensitivity and frequency compensation account for most of the rest. A metal detector uses a 10-turn trim pot to set the DC bias level shared by two [[transistor]] bases, with a separate front-panel pot for sensitivity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> Logic analyser probes carry a trimmer for compensating the probe against the input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> High-voltage differential probes use 10-turn pots for frequency compensation, with trimmer caps in the same role.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> A differential probe design adds a dedicated common-mode rejection ratio trimmer, which compensates for mismatch between the upper resistor string and the lower divider resistor — mismatch that would otherwise degrade [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> Older scope probes and dividers used a trimmer on each [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] step, and each step could need separate high- and low-frequency compensation, which becomes ugly quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup>

Character LCDs are a canonical application: a trim pot sets the contrast bias voltage, adjusted until the characters sit just below the point where they fade out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-313" title="EEVblog #313 - Bus Pirate LCD Debugging">313</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup> Modern controllers replace this with a register value written over the interface — a contrast register incremented and decremented by buttons rather than a shaft.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup>

## Construction and grades

Single-turn carbon trimmers are the cheap end. A single 270-degree rotation covering the full [[resistance]] range gives very little mechanical resolution, and on a calibration adjustment the practical consequence is that the lightest pressure on the screwdriver moves the reading by a couple of digits either way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup> The wiper also finds natural resting places along the carbon track, so the element can effectively snap to a position and refuse a value in between.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup> How much of this matters depends on how wide the designer made the adjustment window around the nominal value — a narrow window is what makes a coarse element usable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup>

Multi-turn trimmers, usually 10-turn and sometimes 20-turn, spread the same resistance range over many screwdriver revolutions and are the standard choice where resolution matters: ovenised oscillator adjustment,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> high-voltage supply primaries,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup> precision reference circuits around a REF02,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup> and current-shunt and 10 A range calibration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup> Where both coarse and fine adjustment are needed, instruments provide both, as in a Philips timer counter with a 10-turn fine trimmer and a separate coarse adjustment elsewhere in the chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> A gap between coarse and fine granularity is felt immediately: a poor 10-turn adjustment gives a much finer range than a coarse one, and a proper multi-turn element is recognisable by feel alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-459" title="EEVblog #459 - Counter Shenanigans">459</a></sup>

## Access, mounting and locking

Because a trimmer must be reachable after assembly, its position drives mechanical design. Good practice drills access holes through the board so pots obscured by an upper board can still be adjusted from the back with a screwdriver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> Instrument enclosures carry matching holes: LeCroy places front-end trimmer pots behind a row of holes in the bottom cover that are not ventilation, so the units are trimmed after the case is on;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> Siglent cuts holes in the metalwork to reach the pots that tweak AC performance;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> a Fluke 37's alloy case has the adjustment openings moulded in and aligned to the trimmers below.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup> Where the holes are absent, the trimming must be done on the bench before final assembly, since the pots become unreachable afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup> Sealing a shielded compartment without provision for access can leave three trim pots inside that cannot be reached once the instrument is closed up, which undermines the point of having them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup>

Mechanical stress is a second design concern. Mounting trimmers away from the sensitive circuitry and running fine wires to them, with the pots rigidly coupled to the board, decouples the screwdriver force from the [[solder]] joints — pressing too hard on a pot soldered directly at the critical node is damaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup>

Once set, trimmers are commonly locked. Manufacturers apply lacquer, sealant or coloured compound over the element both to prevent vibration from moving it and to show that the unit has been calibrated;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-940" title="EEVblog #940 - Mailbag">940</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup> some are sealed entirely so the setting is not user-serviceable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-481" title="EEVblog #481 - Mailbag">481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-769" title="EEVblog #769 - Mailbag">769</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup> Adjustment itself requires care in the choice of tool: a metal screwdriver can upset the circuit it is adjusting, so plastic trimming tools are needed for trim pots and trimmer capacitors alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup>

## Manufacturing cost

Every trimmer on a board is a person and a step in the factory. A retro Canon still camera carries 40 trim pots on a single side of one board, each of which had to be adjusted individually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-937" title="EEVblog #937 - Retro Canon Still Camera Teardown!">937</a></sup> An HD DVD player has three trimmers next to the triple laser [[diode]] assembly, set at the factory to align the optics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1011" title="EEVblog #1011 - Retro Teardown - World&#x27;s First HD DVD Player">1011</a></sup> A Philips counter's expense is directly attributable to each unit having been hand assembled and hand tweaked, its coarse and fine trimmers set and the result soaked in and characterised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> A BWD MiniLab exposes a full bank of them, labelled for maximum frequency, minimum frequency, symmetry, gain, [[waveform]], peak rounding adjust and [[sine-wave|sine wave]] only.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup>

The same logic applies at the other extreme of volume. For a one-off piece of test equipment such as a [[pcb|PCB]] panel test jig, putting trim pots on the board and calibrating it by hand is entirely reasonable, because only one board will ever be built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## The move to digital calibration

The engineering judgment expressed consistently across the corpus is that external trim pots have no place in a modern instrument. Where a chipset lacks closed-case calibration — no [[eeprom|EEPROM]] to store calibration settings and compensate — the design is forced back onto external trim pots, and that is poor form given long-term drift characteristics and what modern processes can do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> A meter of any quality should not need trim pots today, although decent instruments carrying them still exist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Cheap designs betray themselves by the opposite: trimmer pots scattered everywhere, six or more visible in a low-cost meter, four adjustable from the bottom of the case, five on a single board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup>

The transition is visible within single product lines. The Uni-T UT61E used a 10-turn trimmer marked for DC volts calibration, another pot for AC volts, a third adjustment pot, and trimmer capacitors for frequency and [[capacitance]]; the UT61E+ that replaced it has no adjustment pots at all, relying instead on a better reference and better divider resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup> Similarly, an [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] may retain a single trim pot as its only physical adjustment alongside a jumper that selects calibration mode,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> or reduce to one tiny single-turn PCB-mount trimmer with everything else done in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-229" title="EEVblog #229 - IET DE-5000 LCR Meter Teardown">229</a></sup> A power analyser with one lone trim pot in the voltage path and none in the current path can be calibrated in software regardless, following a procedure in the manual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup>

Even an eight-and-a-half digit DMM may carry a crusty single-turn trim pot on the board without that reflecting on its class, because the trimmer is not touching the parts that determine performance — the precision relays, precision resistors and hand-selected, hand-aged voltage reference elsewhere in the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup>

Calibration in the metrological sense is separate from trimming. Sending a [[multimeter]] away for calibration does not mean someone tweaks the trim pots inside; calibration is the science of measurement, and adjustment is a distinct activity that may or may not follow it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-420" title="EEVblog #420 - What Is Calibration?">420</a></sup>

## Diagnostic value

The presence, absence or ambiguity of trimmers is itself information when working on unfamiliar hardware. Two shorted pins is a common trimmer footprint, so an unpopulated pad pattern with two pins tied together identifies a place where a trimmer was intended.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup> Conversely, four trim pots inside a clamp meter whose manufacturer releases no schematics and no calibration procedures leave a repairer with no way to know what they do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Yk_T-uCbg10" title="Returned BM036 Clamp Meter Investigation">•</a></sup> Adjustment ranges are frequently insufficient for a repair use they were never designed for: running a contrast trimmer to both extremes with no effect at all indicates the wrong topology rather than a faulty pot, and the fix in that case was to load down the V0 pin through a 5K trimmer instead of trimming the original series element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup>

Trimmers also fail and drift. A counter whose trim does nothing points to a fault in the trimmer inside the ovenised oscillator rather than in the oscillator itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup> An unlacquered trimmer moved by hand upsets a calibration immediately and irrecoverably without the original procedure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-940" title="EEVblog #940 - Mailbag">940</a></sup> And trim pots that were not fitted at the factory are a reliable sign of prior modification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1418" title="EEVblog 1418 - The Most EMBARRASSING Repair!">1418</a></sup>
