A temperature sensor is the element that converts temperature into an electrical quantity a circuit can act on, and in most equipment it exists not to display a number but to close a control loop or trip a protection threshold. Its accuracy is dominated less by the sensing element than by where it is placed and how well it is thermally coupled to the thing whose temperature actually matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> The recurring engineering problem is thermal path, not transduction: a well-chosen sensor bolted to the wrong piece of metal, or separated from the heat source by plastic and air, reports a temperature that is real but irrelevant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup>

## Sensing elements

The cheapest practical element is a negative [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] thermistor, used wherever a rough absolute reading with a strong slope is sufficient — heater controllers, air conditioner panels, EVSE chargers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup> A thermistor is also easy to triage in a repair: an in-circuit reading of the right order, such as 11 k, rules out an open or shorted element even without a curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup>

A plain silicon PN junction works as a sensor with a temperature coefficient of roughly 10 mV per degree C, and this is what appears in fast [[battery]] chargers, where cell temperature feedback right at the contacts is not optional — without it a fast charger will destroy the cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> The same crude junction sensor is integrated on-die in microcontrollers and processors to report [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]], and although the technique is unrefined it is adequate for [[thermal-management|thermal management]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Laptops and similar hardware carry several such sensors and use them to drive fan speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1345" title="EEVblog #1345 - HP DV7 Laptop Repair">1345</a></sup>

Thermocouples are used where the sensed point is remote, hot, or numerous. A precision instrument may distribute them very widely: one design places roughly two dozen thermal sensors across the whole chassis, with a sensor per channel on the low-power and high-power output amplifiers, one per board, one per main chip, and several across different regions of the [[fpga|FPGA]], all feeding an algorithm that sets fan speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1624" title="EEVblog 1624 - Electronex: Liquid Instruments Moku Oscilloscope">1624</a></sup>

Platinum [[resistance]] wire occupies the high-accuracy end. A meteorological radiosonde sensor of this type achieves a resolution of 0.01 degrees C with a response time of half a second, with a combined sounding uncertainty of about 0.3 degrees C below 16 km.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup>

Integrated digital sensors combine the element, conversion and an interface. The SHT21 class of [[i2c|I2C]] parts measures relative humidity and air temperature together and turns up in everything from IO interface boards to network appliances.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-377" title="EEVblog #377 - Mailbag Monday">377</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup> Some precision references also expose a temperature output: the REF01 brings the bandgap core out on a temp pin whose [[voltage]] varies linearly with temperature, so it can serve both as a system temperature sensor and as the input to compensation of the reference itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

## Cold junction compensation

Any [[multimeter]] that measures temperature with an external [[thermocouple]] must contain an internal temperature sensor, because the thermocouple only produces a voltage proportional to the difference between its measuring junction and the junction formed at the instrument's input terminals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Without knowing the temperature of those dissimilar-metal junctions, the reading carries an offset error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

The compensation can be done in software from the internal sensor's reading, commonly with a low-order polynomial, or in hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Dedicated cold junction compensation chips exist: a conventional three-op-amp [[differential-amplifier|differential amplifier]] with a calibrated, trimmed internal temperature sensor and a compensation network tailored to one thermocouple type, such as type K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup>

Placement is the whole game. In a properly executed thermocouple meter the compensation sensor is physically coupled through to the metal of the input jacks, and the [[schematic]] carries an instruction to the [[pcb|PCB]] designer to locate it near the inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Cheaper implementations simply place the sensor close to the jacks without coupling it, which is better than putting it halfway up the meter but is not the correct method.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> High-accuracy thermometry goes further still, with a calibrated precision thermometer and a large isothermal block at the terminals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> The same technique appears in unrelated places: a true-RMS converter section can use a device near the V terminal purely as a temperature sensor for thermal compensation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup>

## Reading the internal sensor deliberately

Because the cold junction sensor exists whether or not a probe is attached, it can be read directly. Selecting temperature mode and shorting the input with a shorting link or the probes makes the meter display its own internal sensor, giving an ambient reading with no thermocouple at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup> Dave Jones treats this as a general bench trick and has verified it against a thermal chamber, where the displayed value tracks chamber temperature closely, and against meters left for hours in a refrigerator, where several instruments independently converged on the same low ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-48J0m3dvpgA" title="Multimeter Fridge Test">•</a></sup> The same access path serves diagnostics: a sealed unit with no external probe point can be monitored during a thermal-shock repair by reading its internal sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup>

The important caveat is thermal lag. The sensor sits inside a plastic case and often inside a rubber holster, so ambient air takes a significant time to reach it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup> Instrumented in a thermal oven, a meter's internal sensor reads 35 degrees when the box is at 38, and just over 40 when the box is at 45.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Self-heating is a second error source: a meter with a built-in temperature display shows a visible rise after its internal battery has been charged, which is a design issue rather than a measurement one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup> Built-in ambient sensors of this kind are common enough to be unremarkable, sometimes buried far enough in the menus that their existence is not obvious.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1420" title="EEVblog 1420 - Mailbag">1420</a></sup>

## Over-temperature and thermal protection

The most common single use of a discrete temperature sensor is [[heat-sink|heat sink]] monitoring for over-temperature protection, and the quality of its mechanical attachment is a reliable indicator of design care. Good practice bolts or screws the sensor directly into the side of the heat sink so it measures that heat sink's own temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1698" title="EEVblog 1698 - Awesome Ebay AC Power Source: GW Instek APS-7050">1698</a></sup> One receiver design uses two dedicated PCBs solely to hold a TO-92 sensor against the metal, thermally coupling it to the heat sink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> Sensors mounted directly on the heat sink appear in bench supplies, instrument switch-mode supplies, high-power PSUs and inverters alike, on both primary-side switching transistors and secondary-side output devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> Where the heat sink is fan-cooled, the sensors also decide when the fan runs and how hard, so unobstructed radiant paths can raise the power available before the fan is commanded on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1173" title="EEVblog #1173 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz Power Supply Bonanza">1173</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup>

Amateur and one-off designs solve the coupling problem differently — surface-mount sensors soldered to the board and press-fit under the heat sink when it is bolted down, which works but risks mechanical stress on the packages since the compression acts on a single axis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup> Another approach cable-ties a power [[resistor]] and a sensor onto a chip to hold it at a stable temperature, cheap and possibly effective, though hot spots in the die and long-term aging remain unknown.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup>

Protection only works if the loop is closed properly. A hot air rework station whose fan seized destroyed its heating element even though a temperature sensor was fitted, because loss of [[airflow]] was never detected and shutdown was never commanded — a failure that could have been handled in [[firmware]] given a working thermocouple feedback loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1171" title="EEVblog #1171 - Yihau WEP SMD Rework Station Meltdown!">1171</a></sup> A related heater failure has the same root: with no air moving over the element, the thermistor sat in the wrong thermal environment and read low, so the controller kept delivering power while radiant heat continued to build.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup> Sensor placement in cheap heaters is frequently poor, with the sensing element sited too close to the heating element to represent inlet or [[ambient-temperature|ambient temperature]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup>

## Soldering irons and rework

[[soldering-iron|Soldering iron]] performance is governed largely by where the temperature sensor sits relative to the tip. In old-style construction the tip is a separate part that slides over a ceramic heater element, with the sensor embedded somewhere in that element, so the tip is physically decoupled from both heater and sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> Cartridge designs integrate tip, element and thermocouple into one part, putting the sensor right at the tip next to the element; the control loop is faster and power is delivered sooner when the tip is loaded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> The three contacts on such a cartridge are the visible evidence of the integrated sensor, and they allow real-time tip temperature to be displayed on the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Compared side by side, recovery on the older decoupled construction is markedly slower, and the difference is attributable to tip design, coupling and sensor integration rather than to raw wattage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup>

Verifying any of this is harder than it looks. Tip thermometers disagree substantially with the station's own display, and a displayed reading that barely moves when the tip is fully loaded indicates a problem with sensor location or the display path rather than genuinely tight regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup> Rework station handpieces combine the temperature sensor with stand detection for sleep mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup>

## Thermal imaging

Thermal cameras need a reference temperature to calibrate against. A shutter used for [[calibration]] is only effective if it carries a temperature sensor directly on it, so its temperature is known precisely; proper instruments use a large matte-black-painted aluminium plate with an embedded sensor, giving a uniform known temperature across the field.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> A clip-on micro shutter with no sensor on it cannot achieve the same performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> Microbolometer modules also contain their own internal temperature sensor and can signal the host [[microcontroller]] when compensation is required, with the micro deciding whether to command it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> Separately, a [[thermal-camera|thermal camera]]'s imaging sensor and its visible camera are physically offset, producing parallax error at close range that requires a software distance setting to correct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

## Environmental and atmospheric measurement

Sensors exposed to the environment face error sources that bench sensors do not. A radiosonde temperature sensor is made thin and tiny and held away from the body on a large air gap specifically to limit solar radiation error, since at altitude solar heating would otherwise raise the sensor above true air temperature; the small surface area also improves response rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup> The same geometry limits evaporative cooling error after the sonde passes through cloud and picks up moisture, and the companion humidity sensor may carry a small resistive heater acting as a de-icer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup> Radiosondes report temperature, pressure and position and transmit the data to [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup>

Multi-parameter environmental monitors bundle temperature with humidity, pressure, particulates, air quality and radiation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1079" title="EEVblog #1079 - Mailbag">1079</a></sup> Low-power wide-area nodes exist largely to get exactly this kind of reading onto a network, since LoRaWAN's long range and multi-year [[battery-life|battery life]] suit low data rate sensor traffic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1062" title="EEVblog1062 - The Things Network - Progress Edit">1062</a></sup> Even precision instruments now include temperature, humidity and pressure sensing internally, because at femtoamp [[current]] levels humidity genuinely affects the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

## Temperature-stabilised systems

Where an [[oscillator]]'s stability matters, a heater and a temperature sensor form a closed loop around the crystal. An ovenised oscillator carries the heater element and sensor on the outside of the can, regulating to a fixed point such as 79 degrees C, with foam insulation to make it immune to external thermal shock; the assemblies draw several watts to reach and hold temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> The sensor is sometimes deliberately placed on the outside of the can rather than inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> A cheaper approach compensates rather than controls: crystal temperature characteristics corrected by two thermosensors driving a varactor, producing a poor man's TCXO that is not temperature controlled at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup>

Peltier thermal chambers work the same way, with a sensor poking into the chamber and an impeller circulating air so the internal temperature stays consistent throughout the volume rather than only at the sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup> Consistency of the medium around the sensor is a general requirement; a heated bath places its sensor in the path of the agitator's flow so a continuous stream passes over it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hVIo7vmIExw" title="Mystery Dumpster Teardown - 2020-06-12">•</a></sup>

## Multi-point and system-level sensing

Systems with distributed thermal risk need distributed sensing. Competition electric vehicle rules require at least 30 percent of cells to be monitored with no sensor further than 10 mm from a cell's negative pole, which for a typical traction pack of around 100 modules in series and 80 cells in parallel implies hundreds of measurement points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup> Battery packs of all sizes carry a temperature sensor alongside the cells, often visible as an extra unlabelled contact or a two-pin lead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-923" title="EEVblog #923 - World&#x27;s Fastest Solar Electric Car">923</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Cn3DVQGmF9A" title="Dyson Battery Failure">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1593" title="EEVblog 1593 - 16kV ESD Tester REPAIR - Part 2">1593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> Planetary rovers likewise carry many temperature sensors alongside an inertial measurement unit, because knowing temperature is essential to operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup>

At the other end of the scale, an ambient sensor's usefulness depends on isolating it from local heat sources. A temperature sensor in a wristwatch is difficult to isolate from the heat of the wearer's wrist, which limits what such a reading means.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1391" title="EEVblog 1391 - Mailbag">1391</a></sup> A finger touching a small environmental board changes its reading immediately, which is both a fault-finding clue and a demonstration of how little [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] such sensors have.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup>

## Practical consequences

Where the sensor is placed defines what is being controlled. In a stratified hot water tank the sensor location sets the effective control point — one installation places it at the 30-degree mark, which is where heat input stops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1517" title="EEVblog 1517 - Heat Pump Hot Water Install &amp; Analysis">1517</a></sup> Meaningful thermal characterisation of such a system is not possible without proper water flow meters and proper temperature sensors in the right places.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1517" title="EEVblog 1517 - Heat Pump Hot Water Install &amp; Analysis">1517</a></sup> Multiple sensors plus an ambient reference allow the useful quantity to be extracted by subtraction rather than read directly: plotting the difference between each of three sensors and ambient, rather than the raw temperatures, reveals rises of up to about 13 degrees that the raw traces obscure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup>

Cheap dedicated data loggers make the same measurement portable. A coin-sized sealed logger uses its own case as the sensor pad, runs for about ten years on an internal lithium cell, holds 2 K of [[sample-memory|sample memory]] and can be set to log anywhere from once per minute to once per year.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9" title="EEVblog #9 - Maxim/Dallas ThermoChron iButton">9</a></sup> For bench climate control, a bench-mounted temperature sensor driving a hacked air conditioner over its Wi-Fi module can hold a small lab to roughly 0.2 to 0.3 degrees of hysteresis, tighter than the unit's own control system achieved — a result Ian Johnston attributes partly to the small volume being regulated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a4Xpsenpd6E" title="The Amp Hour #643 - Calibration &amp; Repair with Ian Johnston">•</a></sup>
