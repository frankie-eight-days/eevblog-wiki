A signal generator is a bench instrument that produces a [[waveform]] of known shape, amplitude and [[frequency]] for injection into a circuit or another instrument. It is one of the four or five items that define a general-purpose electronics bench, alongside a [[power-supply|power supply]], an [[oscilloscope]], an SMU and a computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1565" title="EEVblog 1565 - UNSW Electronics Lab &amp; Makerspace Tour">1565</a></sup> Its value lies in being the known quantity in a measurement: when the stimulus is trusted, any discrepancy at the other end belongs to the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1492" title="EEVblog 1492 - $5 Oscilloscope Repaired! + Oz GIVEAWAY">1492</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup>

## Output termination

A generator output driven into anything other than its design [[impedance]] misbehaves in ways that are easy to mistake for a fault in the circuit. The standard practice is to feed the output into a 50-ohm terminated load so that there are no transmission-line issues and no reflections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) Explained &amp; Measured">1521</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> An in-line 50-ohm terminator on the cable serves this purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> A scope's internal 50-ohm termination will do the same job, but external terminators are generally rated for higher power, which matters when the point of the test is to drive as high a [[voltage]] as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Where a scope has no internal 50-ohm termination and no in-line terminator is fitted, the mismatch shows up directly in the pulse response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup> An unterminated stub of [[coaxial-cable|coax]] hanging off the load produces reflections back down the line, and a [[square-wave|square wave]] — which contains many frequencies — makes them visible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>

The consequence of ignoring termination is a simple amplitude error. An RF signal generator assumes a 50-ohm load; presented instead with a 1-megohm input, a setting of 100 mV RMS reads as 192 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup>

## Measuring common-mode rejection

Common-mode rejection ratio measurement is one of the tasks where generator amplitude is the limiting factor. The signal being measured — the residual common-mode output — is very small, so the input signal should be as large as the generator can produce; a maximum of 5 V peak-to-peak at 10 MHz is a typical working point, 10 MHz being the figure quoted in the [[datasheet]] under verification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) Explained &amp; Measured">1521</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> The generator output is shorted onto both inputs of the differential probe through a banana breakout, so that both wires carry the same signal relative to [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Repeating the measurement at 1 MHz should improve the result by roughly 10 dB, and the scope's input [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]] is dropped in step with the test frequency — to 20 MHz for a 1 MHz measurement — so that the [[noise-floor|noise floor]] does not swamp the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) Explained &amp; Measured">1521</a></sup> A generator reaching 100 MHz allows the probe to be characterised across its full rated [[bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) Explained &amp; Measured">1521</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup>

## Verifying an oscilloscope

A quick functional test of an unknown scope is to confirm that both channels display a waveform, that they trigger, and that a high volts-per-division range and a low one both spot-check correctly. If the displayed amplitude also matches the generator, the scope is very nearly certain to be fully functional, because a generator failing by 50 per cent and a channel gain failing by the same amount in the opposite direction is not a plausible coincidence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1492" title="EEVblog 1492 - $5 Oscilloscope Repaired! + Oz GIVEAWAY">1492</a></sup> Feeding in 500 mV peak-to-peak and reading 508 mV is a reasonable [[calibration]] result for a scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1418" title="EEVblog 1418 - The Most EMBARRASSING Repair!">1418</a></sup>

The same method exposes what a scope actually does rather than what it claims. Feeding in a signal of known amplitude and sweeping frequency establishes the real [[analog-bandwidth|analog bandwidth]] regardless of what the configuration menu reports.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-978" title="EEVblog #978 - Keysight 1000X Oscilloscope Hacked!">978</a></sup> Winding up the frequency until the displayed amplitude falls reveals bandwidth that varies with the volts-per-division setting — a limitation of the scope, confirmed as such because the stimulus came from a known-good Marconi generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> A 10 MHz generator output, watched on a trigger-output line, gives a [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]] figure of 27,000 to 28,000 waveforms per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> A 200 MHz sine from an RF signal generator fed into a 200 MHz scope sampling at 1 GSa/s renders as a jagged shape, which is what motivates sine-x-on-x interpolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> Probe behaviour is characterised the same way, with a 1 V peak-to-peak sine at 1 MHz as the reference against which attenuation is judged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup>

## Accuracy and drive limits

Generator amplitude accuracy sets a floor on what can be concluded from a measurement. A 4 V peak-to-peak setting can read 4.12 V while the frequency is exactly right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> At the other extreme, a nominal 100 microvolt signal measured as 71 microvolts may say more about the generator's accuracy at that level than about the scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup> Low-amplitude generation is generally where cheap hardware falls down: producing a 10 mV signal accurately is difficult, and the deficiency matters when the device under test has a gain of 100 and needs a small input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #858 - Red Pitaya Extended Cut">2</a></sup>

[[current|Current]] drive is the other limit. A generator channel set to a 5 V DC level can power a small board directly, but the rail may dip under load, and a generator that cannot supply enough current produces amplitude errors downstream that look like a fault in the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup> A two-channel instrument is useful here precisely because one channel can supply the DC rail while the other supplies the test signal — 500 mV peak-to-peak into 50 ohms through an in-line terminator, up to the instrument's 100 MHz limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup>

## Injecting into a live circuit

Connecting a generator to a node that carries DC pushes that DC back onto the coax and into the generator's output stage. Where a front end is AC-coupled and the injection point sits at 6 volts, the options are to AC-couple the generator with a series [[capacitor]] or to lift one end of the offending component so the voltage cannot be fed back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup> Shorting the output is likewise not something a generator will tolerate happily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup> Manufacturers acknowledge the risk from the other direction: a scope's built-in generator output may carry a [[fuse]], placed there less against a short than against something being fed back into it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> A generator's ground reference can also be the problem rather than the solution — an audio source with no galvanic connection to the bench avoids ground issues that a mains-referenced generator would introduce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1072" title="EEVblog #1072 - KRK Rokit 6 Studio Monitor Speaker Repair">1072</a></sup>

## Applications beyond displaying a waveform

Square-wave stimulus is a standard technique for compensating any closed-loop system: connect a generator, look at the output step response, and adjust until it shows only a small amount of [[overshoot]] before rounding off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup> Current probes are checked by shorting the generator's 50-ohm output through the probe at 1 kHz, giving about 25 mA — below the probe's nominal 50 mA measurement floor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> [[multimeter|Multimeter]] [[input-impedance|input impedance]] effects are demonstrated by driving the meter from a generator, whose low source impedance makes 10 megohm and high-impedance modes indistinguishable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> In audio repair, a 1 kHz tone injected at the input and switched on and off shows whether the signal is reaching the ADC and whether the ADC output data changes in response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup>

A generator also substitutes for a misbehaving clock source: where an open-collector [[optocoupler]] and pull-up produce an edge too slow for a counter input, feeding the same node from a generator confirms the diagnosis immediately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> Half-bridge gate-drive boards accept a generator into a 50-ohm input, with the board inverting the signal and adding dead time so both transistors cannot conduct together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> Driven through a power amplifier, a generator running at 8.5 Hz can shake a vibration motor to hunt for mechanical resonances.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1607" title="EEVblog 1607 - PSU Switching Noise Reduction via Vibration">1607</a></sup> At the high-frequency end, calculating the sensitivity of an RF crystal detector requires a generator capable of delivering −20 dBm at 10 GHz, with a power meter to verify the level at the measurement point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EVcxrYnY1ok" title="Guest Video: TGSoapbox - RF Crystal Detectors">•</a></sup> EMC radiated-immunity testing to IEC 61000-4-3 is built around a computer-controlled signal generator and amplifier outside the chamber driving a transmitting [[antenna]] at the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> Filter response can be measured with a generator and an oscilloscope, though it is considerably harder than using a spectrum analyser's tracking generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup>

## Generators built into other instruments

Many oscilloscopes carry a generator as a paid option, and the hardware distinction is real rather than a licence key: the base model of one 1000-series scope does not have the signal generator, and is understood not to have it physically populated even though the board layout is otherwise identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> Similarly, a 1054Z lacks the source outputs of higher models in its family and cannot be ordered with the source option at all — there is not even a [[connector]] where a source board would attach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> Option pricing is substantial relative to the instrument: on a 999-dollar 350 MHz scope, a dual-channel 100 MHz generator is a 700-dollar option and a 50 MHz version 419.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> That generator warrants its own dedicated board, identifiable by its relays, carrying dual 100 MHz arbitrary generation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> Option-slot scarcity can force a choice — some scopes cannot take both the generator and the logic analyser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup>

Two-channel implementations raise the question of phase relationship between the outputs, which is set through a start-phase parameter rather than a direct alignment control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> Feature sets on higher-end scope generators extend to arbitrary, sine, square, ramp, pulse, DC, [[noise]], sinc and exponential-rise waveforms with AM and FM modulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> Modulation options elsewhere include AM, FM and FSK with sine, square and ramp modulating waveforms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> One scope offers to cut out part or all of a captured waveform and save it as an output on the signal generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup>

The quality of these built-in generators varies enough that some are not worth using. A generator whose output is a bare pin, with no way to adjust the output signal level, is best treated as though the instrument did not have one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup> Another handheld combination tops out at 1 MHz with sine, square, triangle and sawtooth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Where the generator and scope share reconfigurable hardware, switching applications can silently stop the generator — running a generator in one app and then moving to a separate oscilloscope app loses the signal, which forces the use of a combined app instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup>

Generators appear inside instruments other than scopes as well. Dynamic signal analysers covering DC to about 100 kHz include a source that generates sine and noise alongside their dual wide-dynamic-range inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup> A [[frequency-response|frequency response]] analyser provides a source spanning 0 to 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> An isolated oscilloscope intended for gain-phase work carries a fully isolated signal generator to match its isolated channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> An optical-fibre probe contains a test generator used during its calibration routine, generating a signal the probe then measures to null out offsets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> LCR tweezers may include noise generation at 0.1 MHz, a capability of marginal usefulness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> A scopemeter could be fitted with a generator output as an option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup>

## Standalone instruments

Dedicated generators span an enormous range of capability and price. A two-channel [[arbitrary-waveform-generator|arbitrary waveform generator]] offering 160 MHz and 500 MSa/s with independent channels and separate sync outputs sits at the value end of professional gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-323" title="EEVblog #323 - Rigol Scratch&#x27;n&#x27;Sniff">323</a></sup> A DG1022-class instrument at around 400 dollars is a reasonable first generator, in single- or dual-channel form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> At the other extreme are portable 20 GHz signal generators<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1468" title="EEVblog 1468 - Electronex Show Tour 2022">1468</a></sup> and 6 GHz synthesisers; the latter, when fitted with a small [[lcd-display|LCD screen]], get reached for far more often than equivalent screenless units that require a computer and a GUI to drive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MecbPOPeovs" title="Whatever Happened to the uSupply Project?">•</a></sup> Rohde & Schwarz built its reputation on RF signal generators and related high-frequency test gear long before entering the oscilloscope market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup> Marconi Instruments occupied the high-end signal generator space in the same way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup>

Older field instruments combined generation and measurement in one portable box: a 20 Hz to 64 or 110 kHz unit designed as both generator and receiver, supplied with a padded case and shoulder strap, was carried out to check telecommunication lines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1258" title="EEVblog #1258 - MORE Dumpster Wandel And Goltermann!">1258</a></sup>

Signal generators sit at the expensive end of test equipment, and this shapes how labs provision them. Where a spare handheld multimeter or general-purpose scope can simply be bought and shelved, that logic does not extend upward: "You're not going to have a spare signal generator."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-424" title="EEVblog #424 - $3M Agilent Portable Calibration Lab Tour">424</a></sup> [[time-base|Timebase]] options — the same high-stability references offered on other instruments — are available in most signal generators, either as standard or as an option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-424" title="EEVblog #424 - $3M Agilent Portable Calibration Lab Tour">424</a></sup> Against that, second-hand and low-cost new test gear has made generators more available than at any earlier point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> Failures do occur: a dead generator that will not come up at all is a total loss of the instrument,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup> and in a repair context a source output that produces nothing regardless of waveform selection, while the analog overload and half-rail detection circuitry flags faults, points at damage well beyond the source section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup>
