Memory depth, also called record length or storage depth, is the number of samples a digital [[oscilloscope]]'s acquisition memory holds for a single capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> Together with the [[sample-rate]] it fixes how much time a capture spans, so it decides whether a signal is recorded with enough resolution to be believed, and whether there is any data left to examine when the capture is zoomed into.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> It is one of the key figures of merit for an oscilloscope, alongside [[bandwidth]] and sample rate, and it is one of no fewer than six independent settings a user must understand before a modern digital scope can be driven properly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

## Memory depth, sample rate, and time base

The three quantities are locked together by simple arithmetic: the acquired time window equals the memory depth divided by the sample rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> At 5 GSa/s the interval between samples is 200 ps, so 4 Mpts of memory buys 800 µs of record, and 2 Mpts buys 400 µs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> At 2.5 GSa/s the sample interval is 400 ps and 10 Mpts corresponds to 4 ms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> The consequence is that as the [[time-base]] is slowed, a scope must either consume more memory or drop its sample rate; a scope with 4 Mpts cannot hold 5 GSa/s at 1 ms per division, whereas 400 Mpts would allow it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

Because measurement and analysis functions read the acquisition record rather than the screen, memory depth also governs what those functions can see. Some instruments let measurements be gated to the screen, to the full record, or to the region between cursors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Serial decoding may run over the entire record — one implementation decodes across the full 14 Mpts supplied as standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11-AQ_E1fz8" title="Siglent SDS1000X-E Serial Decoding">•</a></sup> Decoding at 12 Mpts across a long capture reveals occasional glitches within a packet stream that a screen-length record would not contain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup>

## The interpolation and aliasing trap

The most common failure mode is running out of samples at a long time base and mistaking the interpolated result for the signal. All modern scopes apply sin(x)/x interpolation by default, and with too few points across the record it will draw a smooth, plausible, entirely fictitious [[waveform]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> This has nothing to do with bandwidth — reducing the analogue bandwidth to 20 MHz changes nothing, and the artefact appears on a low-[[frequency]] square wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> With 14 kpts a digital signal captured at 50 ms per division is unrecoverable; raising the memory depth, or choosing a faster horizontal setting such as 1 ms per division, restores the real data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> The same demonstration at 1.25 Mpts on an instrument capable of 256 Mpts is cured by moving to 125 Mpts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup>

The trap is insidious precisely because the wrong answer looks clean, and a user who is not mentally tracking how many points are in play can spend a long time chasing an imagined probing problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> The lower the memory depth, the sooner a given signal will alias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Scopes with a genuine automatic memory depth mode do not permit this: the auto mode picks enough memory that aliasing does not occur, and on the Keysight InfiniiVision series sin(x)/x interpolation cannot be enabled or disabled by the user at all — the instrument decides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup>

## The trade-off against waveform update rate

Deeper memory means more data to process per acquisition, and the [[waveform-update-rate]] falls accordingly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup> On one instrument the same infrequent event visible at 140 kpts becomes noticeably less frequent at 1.4 Mpts and hard to catch at all at 14 Mpts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> The best update rate obtainable from a Tektronix 2 Series was 18.8 k waveforms per second, at 10 ns per division, 250 points of memory, and normal trigger mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Every scope has an optimum determined jointly by sample rate and memory depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> A thousand-point record can produce fast-acquisition-like behaviour on an ordinary scope, and increasing the memory immediately costs update rate — the waveforms-per-second figure is not magic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup>

The practical rule Dave Jones applies at the bench is to choose the lowest amount of memory that still resolves the signal, because catching an infrequent event is a matter of statistics and depends on how many acquisitions per second the scope achieves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Stated directly: "you should choose the lowest amount of memory for the fastest update rate possible for a given time base".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> The corollary is equally firm — "Don't always think it's the best thing to actually set your scope to the maximum memory depth."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> A minimum selectable depth of 4 kpts is a reasonable compromise between update speed and record length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup>

There are exceptions in both directions. An instrument whose acquisition architecture is slow enough may show the same update rate regardless of memory depth, in which case there is no reason not to run maximum memory permanently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Conversely, an [[fft]] wants the memory set to maximum unless fast updating matters more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup> Intensity-graded and persistence displays only reveal modulation artefacts when there is enough data behind them; at 50 kpts the artefacts simply are not there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup>

## Automatic versus manual memory depth

Two design philosophies exist. One exposes memory depth as a user setting; the other manages it automatically and, in the strongest form, removes the control entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1612" title="EEVblog 1612 - Siglent SDS1000X HD 12 bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1612</a></sup> The automatic approach optimises the acquisition and displays the best waveform it can without the user having to know what mode the instrument is in, which makes for a markedly better day-to-day driving experience.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> Against that, an instrument with a manual setting can be left in a state that misleads: with its memory at 1000 samples a Tektronix produces artefact-ridden garbage on a complex modulated waveform, is nearly right at 10 kpts, begins to resolve at 100 kpts, and needs a megapoint before it approaches what a competing scope shows automatically — and 5 or 10 Mpts for a genuinely equivalent trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> Deep memory of that order is not how such a scope would normally be operated, because it is too slow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup>

Lacking an auto mode is a real deficiency. The Siglent SDS1000X offers 14 kpts, 140 kpts, 1.4 Mpts and 14 Mpts but no automatic selection, so aliasing that a competing auto mode simply prevents must be avoided by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Manually forcing the competing scope to 14 kpts reproduces the identical failure, which confirms that the difference lies in the selection policy rather than the hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Some instruments remove the fixed-memory option and allow only a fixed sample rate, leaving automatic memory management as the only behaviour — the likely motive being to eliminate a control that confuses users, though the currently selected depth is still clearly displayed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1612" title="EEVblog 1612 - Siglent SDS1000X HD 12 bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1612</a></sup>

Manual control has one clear advantage. Where a scope will not extend a stopped record beyond the screen in auto record length mode, fixing the memory size explicitly can restore that ability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

Implementation quality varies. Some instruments refuse to change memory depth outside run mode, returning "Function is disabled" when the change is attempted, which is discovered only by trial.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> Others cannot change it in stop mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1320" title="EEVblog #1320 - Premature Oscilloscope Triggering">1320</a></sup> One scope reported that one megapoint memory depth was unsupported in the mode it happened to be in, then produced the setting later without explanation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup>

## Memory the scope will not use

Nominal memory depth and usable memory depth are not the same number. Some architectures allocate points according to the time base regardless of the maximum set: with 200 Mpts manually selected, one scope uses only 20 kpts at a fast time base, and at 500 ps per division delivers just 10 points across the screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> Reaching the full 200 Mpts requires winding all the way out to 10 ms per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> By contrast a Keysight with a nominally modest 4 Mpts uses the whole of it at those same time bases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1312" title="EEVblog #1312 - Siglent Oscilloscopes CRIPPLING History Mode!">1312</a></sup> Four megapoints used fully is worth more than 200 Mpts used at 400 points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1312" title="EEVblog #1312 - Siglent Oscilloscopes CRIPPLING History Mode!">1312</a></sup>

One cause of this is an always-on history mode that reserves the entire acquisition memory for stored frames — the full 200 Mpts is genuinely in use, but as roughly two thousand frames of 100 kpts each rather than as one deep record.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1312" title="EEVblog #1312 - Siglent Oscilloscopes CRIPPLING History Mode!">1312</a></sup> The allocation is not clearly documented, and the trade-off it imposes is not obvious from the specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1312" title="EEVblog #1312 - Siglent Oscilloscopes CRIPPLING History Mode!">1312</a></sup>

Another is a ping-pong buffer, in which the memory is split in two so that acquisition and readout can overlap. This produces different memory depths in run/stop mode versus single-shot mode: in single shot the instrument knows no further acquisition is coming, disables the ping-pong split, and uses the entire memory with 50% pre-trigger and 50% post-trigger data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> Even so, at 10 ms per division the whole 4 Mpts is consumed by the visible screen and nothing remains outside it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

A scope quoting 128 kpts of storage depth that cannot be zoomed into after stopping is offering a number with nothing behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup>

## Channel sharing

On instruments that interleave ADCs and acquisition memory between channel pairs, turning on a second channel halves both the sample rate and the memory depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> The mechanism is visible in the hardware: two MegaZoom ASICs and their ADC packages each serve two channels, so enabling channels 1 and 2 halves both figures, while enabling 1 and 3 leaves each at full memory and full sample rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup> The effect is standard across the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> On a four-channel Rigol, 12 Mpts standard (24 Mpts optional) drops to 6 Mpts with three or four channels active.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> The underlying part is a single 512 Mbit DDR2 device — 64 MB, or 16 M samples per channel at 8 bits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup>

## Apparent noise

Memory depth is the principal reason digital scopes are accused of being noisier than analogue ones, an accusation that is not true.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> The more memory depth in use, the more of the signal's high-frequency content is captured and displayed, so the trace thickens.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> Bandwidth acts the same way, and always did on analogue instruments too.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> The other contributors are the boxcar average or high-resolution mode, the intensity-graded display, the update rate and the sample rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Changing memory depth on a running scope visibly changes the apparent noise and brings out the variable-intensity shading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Comparisons between instruments are therefore only meaningful with memory depth held equal — a noise shootout run at 1 MHz, 20 MHz bandwidth limit and 1 Mpts on every scope, with no averaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1000" title="EEVblog #1000 - Fundamental Mailbag Retro Teardown Shootouts are Bullshit">1000</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup> Combining a reduced memory depth with boxcar averaging visibly thins the trace by filtering out that high-frequency content.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup>

## Costs of deep memory

Processing a deep record is slow. Statistical measurement on a full 10 Mpts record can crawl to the point of being unusable, while the same measurement at 1 Mpts updates visibly and 5 Mpts is intermediate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup> Saving a full record to file takes several minutes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup> Serial decoding across 14 Mpts takes on the order of a second per update, which rules out real-time decoding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11-AQ_E1fz8" title="Siglent SDS1000X-E Serial Decoding">•</a></sup> Front-panel interaction can stall entirely at long record lengths.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup>

Segmented memory and zone triggering offer a way around a modest memory budget: capturing ten segments of interest at full resolution and navigating between them gives packet-level detail that a single deep record would otherwise be needed for, which is why 4 Mpts is not necessarily a serious limitation in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Segmented memory implementations differ in flexibility between vendors and between model ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Where memory is genuinely short on an older instrument, peak detect mode should be used so that absolute peaks are not missed between samples — a sensible default when measuring supply ripple and noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

## Displaying the setting

Continuous on-screen indication of the [[current]] sample rate and memory depth is a usability requirement, not a luxury, because it is the only way to confirm the scope is being used properly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Instruments that omit it — showing date, time and a logo instead, or burying the figures in a system status page in the utility menu — force the user to guess.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> The omission recurs across vendors and price points, including instruments with ample unused screen area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l-fuyHCs2Sw" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2004 Oscilloscope Acquisition Bug">•</a></sup> Where it is done well, sample rate and memory depth are always visible and the user always knows where they stand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup>
