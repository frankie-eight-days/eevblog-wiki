A digital-to-analog converter (DAC) takes a digital number and produces a proportional analog output — usually a [[voltage]], sometimes a [[current]] — without any further work by the circuit that fed it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> It is the counterpart to the analog-to-digital converter, and the two are almost always found together: a modern instrument digitises what it measures and synthesises what it outputs, so a DAC sits behind almost every generated [[waveform]], control voltage, offset and threshold in the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> Its two headline parameters are resolution, which fixes the size of the smallest output step, and update rate, which fixes how fast those steps can be walked through.

## Resolution and step size

Resolution is stated in bits, and the arithmetic that follows is the whole of it: a 12-bit converter has 4,096 steps, giving just over 1 mV per step across a 0 to 5 V span.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A 10-bit device driving a 0 to 20.48 V output through a gain of ten gives 20 mV per bit, which is adequate for a general-purpose bench supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> At the other extreme, an 18-bit part such as the DAC9881 works out at 38.146 µV per bit,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup> and a 20-bit unbuffered voltage-output part like the AD5791 reaches 1 ppm resolution and 1 ppm integral non-linearity while operating from bipolar supplies of up to 33 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Because step size depends on both bit count and output span, the two must always be quoted together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> Eight bits is coarse enough to be visible: a generated waveform driven from an 8-bit DAC shows obvious staircase structure on a scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1008" title="EEVblog #1008 - Mystery Teardown">1008</a></sup> The quantisation steps of a source DAC can be resolved directly by a sufficiently low-noise 12-bit [[oscilloscope]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup> and even an analog scope will show the step response of the DAC inside a digital [[function-generator]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup>

## Against PWM

For the common job of letting a [[microcontroller]] set a voltage, the DAC competes with pulse-width modulation. A multi-turn [[potentiometer]] costs five to ten dollars, a dedicated DAC around two dollars and up, while a [[pwm]] module is effectively free because most microcontrollers already contain one and only a [[resistor]] and [[capacitor]] are needed to filter the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Historically the deciding factor was that most microcontrollers had no DAC on board at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> For a given number of bits the two approaches give identical voltage resolution — a PWM-based DAC is a digital-to-analog converter that happens to use the PWM technique.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A rotary encoder or a microcontroller port can drive either equally well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

The case for an external converter is resolution and simplicity: where an on-chip PWM peripheral offers only 10 bits, an external 12-bit DAC paired with an external 12-bit ADC buys more resolution than the microcontroller can manage on its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> The situation has since changed, with 12-bit dual-channel DACs appearing in mainstream microcontrollers,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> including parts whose internal DAC is judged good enough for a non-precision programmable supply,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> a 4 Msample/s update-rate DAC adequate for a handheld function generator,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> and 10-bit DACs alongside ADCs and PWM in ARM microcontrollers of modest cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup>

## Interfacing

Discrete DACs are fed over a serial bus, typically [[spi]] or I²C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> The choice has board-level consequences: moving from an SPI DAC to an I²C DAC in one power-supply design freed three microcontroller pins and cost slightly less.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Where an SPI DAC and SPI ADC coexist, the clock and data lines can be shared and only the chip-select lines duplicated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Converter families are often deliberately software-compatible across resolutions. A 16-bit-wide command word is sent regardless of whether the part is 8, 10 or 12 bits, with the device simply ignoring the surplus least significant bits — four on the 8-bit part, two on the 10-bit part — so the same [[firmware]] drives any member of the family.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> The output is likewise always referenced to full scale, 4,096, whatever the device's actual resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> The corollary is that the word length is not optional: a converter fed only 14 of the required 16 bits does not recognise the transfer as a valid command and produces no output at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> Other failure modes at this interface are mundane and worth checking before the converter is blamed — a dead clock line,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> or a chip-select driven through an I²C I/O expander that has itself stopped working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>

For waveform output, feeding the DAC from software in a busy loop makes a good [[sample-rate]] hard to achieve. The standard technique is to hold the waveform in a lookup table and let a DMA controller traverse it, optionally triggered by a timer for a defined sample interval, which frees the CPU entirely and runs the converter at its maximum data rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eUA2TRX1NTc" title="STM32 ARM Development - DMA &amp; ADC Discussion">•</a></sup> Where DMA is shared with less critical traffic such as an [[lcd]] refresh, the DAC transfer is given the higher priority, since a momentary pause in the screen update is harmless while a gap in the waveform is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eUA2TRX1NTc" title="STM32 ARM Development - DMA &amp; ADC Discussion">•</a></sup>

## Resolution is not accuracy

The distinction between resolution and accuracy applies to DACs exactly as it does to multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A 12-bit converter genuinely steps its output in 1.22 mV increments,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> but the absolute value of those steps is set by the reference, the gain error and the offset error. In one measured case a nominal 0.5003 V output came out at 0.495 V, with a second channel of the same device reading 0.4973 V from identical data; the [[datasheet|data sheet]]'s typical gain error of −0.1 % of full scale plus an offset error of up to 20 mV at the low end accounts for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> The blunt summary of that result: "So much for our precision DAC."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> Probing directly on the converter pins and on the reference input pins rules out [[voltage-drop]] elsewhere on the board as the cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>

The reference is therefore part of the converter. A single 2.048 V reference commonly feeds both the DAC and the ADC in a design,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> and its own tolerance bounds what the converter can do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> Some DACs carry a programmable internal reference with no provision for an external one, in which case the internal zener sets the ceiling on performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> Layout matters too: keeping the ADCs and DACs on a signal-ground region carrying no load current puts them all at the same potential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Where a DAC sets a precision offset, a low-drift reference is required to support it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

The analog circuitry after the converter must also suit it. A DAC swinging from 0 V to 2.048 V into an op-amp demands an input stage that works down to the negative rail, which not every dual-supply op-amp provides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> Current-output DACs need an I-to-V stage — a resistor network followed by a current-to-voltage amplifier in an arbitrary waveform generator,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup> or a pair of op-amps behind a dual current-output part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup>

## Waveform generation

Almost all modern function generators are direct digital synthesis instruments: the waveform is a digital construct converted by a DAC, as distinct from a true analog RF signal generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup> Sixteen bits is the usual resolution,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> and the converters used are among the most expensive parts in the instrument. A dual-channel 16-bit part running at 1.2 Gsample/s costs around $60 in 2,000-piece quantity and dissipates about 1.5 W on its own at full rate;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup> a four-channel arbitrary generator uses 2.5 Gsample/s 14-to-16-bit converters, with the higher-[[bandwidth]] channels each given a dedicated DAC and the lower-specification channels sharing one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Even so, the output drivers can cost more than the converters: twelve 900 MHz current-feedback amplifiers at $7 each exceed the DAC spend in one such instrument, because a DAC cannot drive a 50 Ω output directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>

Supporting circuitry follows from the data rate. Traces from the [[fpga]] to the DAC are length-matched,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> the clock source is a dedicated low-noise VCO with its own low-dropout regulator because analog performance depends on it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> and anti-aliasing filters follow the converter output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup>

The same converters appear in oscilloscopes with built-in generators — a 14-bit DAC904E is a typical choice<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup> — though in some designs the DAC is absorbed into the instrument's main [[asic]] and has no visible presence on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> [[usb]] and modular instruments follow the pattern: 14-bit ADC and DAC pairs at 100 to 125 Msample/s,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup> with an output amplifier giving roughly ±5 V,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup> and the end-to-end bandwidth of about 10 MHz set by the whole chain including both converters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup>

An incidental consequence of DDS architecture is that any generator with a DC or DC-offset mode is a high-resolution programmable [[voltage-source]]. With 16 bits over a ±10 V span, adjustment in 0.1 mV steps is possible, which is finer than the 1 mV granularity often assumed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> Absolute accuracy is the limitation, since DC output is not the instrument's purpose and the internal voltage standards are not exceptional; the technique suits fine adjustment rather than absolute setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup>

## Housekeeping and control roles

Much of the DAC population in test equipment does unglamorous work. Offset control is the most common: quad 12-bit voltage-output parts for the offsets of a handheld scope,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> a dense octal part with a 5 ppm/°C on-chip reference doing offset duty in another,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup> small DACs scattered around a high-speed scope's front end,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> and an offset-nulling DAC inside a high-voltage differential probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> Logic analysers of mid to high grade use a DAC to make the input threshold adjustable, typically over a range of about −5 V to +5 V, in place of fixed logic-family presets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 - Part 2 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup>

Programmable sources are the other large category. A bench supply's control loop needs only a reference for its error amplifier, and that reference can equally come from a DAC or a potentiometer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> A multi-output supply may give each channel its own processor, reference, ADC and DAC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> Electronic loads pair a DAC to set the demanded value with an ADC to read it back, alongside the MOSFETs and sense resistors;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup> a fully digital load implements constant-current, constant-[[resistance]] and constant-power modes in software and therefore needs a fast DAC and ADC,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> often 16-bit parts matched to each other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> The same approach makes a home-built load practical from little more than a DAC, a FET and an op-amp,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup> and feeding the measured voltage back to a microcontroller that recomputes the DAC setting on the fly turns a constant-current load into a constant-resistance or constant-power one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Source-measure units and 6½-digit-class instruments use 16-bit multiplying DACs in the same role.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup>

Where the output voltages need not be precise, 8 bits is enough: a universal device programmer uses an 8-bit quad DAC to set the various programming voltages up to 12 V or 25 V,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-371" title="EEVblog #371 - Universal Programmer Teardown">371</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-371" title="EEVblog #371 - Universal Programmer Teardown">371</a></sup> and a high-end programmer with per-pin clamp and current-source control uses 12-bit parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> Other appearances include the [[calibration]] DAC in an oscillator-oven upgrade, whose absence leaves only a trimpot adjustment,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> a DAC driving the inverting input of a femtoammeter's front end for burden-voltage calibration offset,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> a 12-bit DAC in a 4-20 mA process-control transmitter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DX_f0Cg6pHo" title="How a 4-20mA Process Control Current Loop Rotational Sensor Works">•</a></sup> and an R-2R ladder of only three bits inside an uncommitted logic array, used to switch the gain of an op-amp in a current- and temperature-sense loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-501" title="EEVblog #501 - Sinclair C5 Electric Car Teardown &amp; Test Drive">501</a></sup>

## Video and audio

Video output is a classic DAC application: a three-channel part such as the ADV7125 driving a VGA display is what an FPGA-based display controller uses,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-145" title="EEVblog #145 - Agilent LAN/VGA Module Teardown">145</a></sup> and a simple FPGA VGA demonstration can be built either with a DAC or bodged from resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup>

Audio DACs are specified by dynamic range rather than by bit count alone — a stereo audio DAC with 102 dB signal-to-noise ratio and sample rates to 96 kHz is representative of portable recorder hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup> Integrated codecs bundle DACs and ADCs with a digital mixer, an I²S interface and on-chip DSP,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ag-MjKAfATw" title="Rode AI-1 USB Audio Interface Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup> and digital mixing consoles replace analog pots and op-amps with converter pairs feeding a DSP engine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup> An unusual historical variant is a floating-point audio DAC with a 10-digit mantissa and a 3-digit exponent, giving 16-bit dynamic range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup>

One implication of a digital audio chain is often ignored in marketing: between a memory card and the DAC the signal is nothing but ones and zeros, so a product whose output is affected by the card's radiated emissions is a product that has been designed incorrectly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-719" title="EEVblog #719 - Sony Low Noise Audiophile SDXC Memory Card">719</a></sup>

## Selection and cost

[[parametric-search]] on distributor catalogues is the practical way to choose a converter, filtering on resolution, channel count, package, interface and internal reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> In one design the cheapest 12-bit four-channel part in an eight-pin package came out at $2.40 in 100-off, with the nearest alternative from another vendor unable to meet the channel-count and package constraints; the matching DAC from the same vendor was likewise the cheapest option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Microchip's analog line is a recurring choice for this kind of part on grounds of low power, low price and availability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5" title="EEVblog #5 - Maxim product marketing, Function Generator Review">5</a></sup>

At the top of the market the picture inverts. The high-speed, high-resolution converters in mixed-signal instruments are genuinely expensive chips that make a self-built equivalent uneconomic in low or medium volume,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1596" title="EEVblog 1596 - NEW Digilent Analog Discovery Pro ADP2230 TEARDOWN">1596</a></sup> and the most costly catalogue converters run into thousands of dollars each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup> Between those extremes, the cheapness of a converter is often the point: bare 8-bit DACs in bank-note acceptors<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup> and 12-bit DACs on Raspberry Pi expansion boards<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-979" title="EEVblog #979 - Mailbag">979</a></sup> exist because the function has become nearly free.
