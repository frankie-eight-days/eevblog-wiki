Trigger holdoff is an [[oscilloscope]] trigger control that disables the trigger circuitry for a fixed period after each acquisition, preventing the scope from re-arming until that time has elapsed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> Its purpose is to force a repeating but internally complex [[waveform]] — a burst, a packet, a modulated pattern — to trigger at the same place every time, producing a stable live display where ordinary edge triggering produces a jumbled one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> It exists as a dedicated knob on most higher-end older analog scopes and as a menu setting on essentially every modern digital scope, and it is widely present but poorly understood.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

## Principle of operation

On an [[analog-oscilloscope|analog oscilloscope]] the beam sweeps across the screen, retraces to the start, and the trigger circuitry re-arms ready for the next sweep.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> The holdoff control inserts an additional delay after the retrace: the beam still returns, but instead of re-arming immediately the trigger is held off for a set time, which is where the name comes from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> That time must inherently be at least as long as the retrace period plus a margin for the sweep to settle before the trigger circuit resets, and the control extends it beyond that minimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> Some analog implementations are calibrated in real time units; others are a bare relative control that only lets the time be increased.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

A digital scope has no retrace as such, only processing time, and it typically places the trigger in the middle of the record so that pre-trigger and post-trigger data are both available — but the holdoff concept is unchanged: after the capture finishes, triggering is inhibited for a predetermined period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> The practical advantage of digital implementations is high-resolution numeric control, allowing a precise holdoff value to be entered rather than dialled in blind.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

## The problem it solves

Consider a burst of data followed by a long dead period, then another burst.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> With plain positive-edge triggering the scope has no way to distinguish an edge in the middle of the burst from the first edge of the burst — every one of them satisfies the trigger condition equally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> The result on screen is garbage: the levels are visible and edges can be seen when zoomed in, but the display jumps around and the packet structure cannot be made out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> The resulting trigger jitter is characteristically the same width as the packet itself, because the trigger point wanders anywhere within it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup> Auto-setup does not fix this; a scope can autoscale the signal correctly and still fail to trigger on the packets, because the feature is not smart enough to recognise the structure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

Holdoff resolves it by exploiting the dead time. If the burst is followed by a quiet gap, setting the holdoff so that re-arming falls inside that gap guarantees the next trigger is the first edge of the following burst, whatever random edge the very first trigger happened to land on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> After the initial acquisition the scope waits out the holdoff, re-arms during the dead period, and then captures from the leading edge of the next packet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup>

## Choosing a value

The holdoff time that works is bounded by the period of the entire repetitive cycle being captured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> Two approaches are practical. If the dead time is known — say 100 µs — the holdoff is set slightly below it, around 90 to 95 µs, so that the trigger will only fire on an edge preceded by at least that much quiet time and all other candidate edges are ignored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> If it is not known, the control can simply be increased until the display locks up, since no measurement is required to find the working value by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

The working setting is a window rather than a single number. In one worked case with cursors placed on the packet, the usable range ran from roughly 40 µs to just under 47 µs; 39.6 µs was close enough to hold, and beyond the upper edge the display broke up again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> For a 20 µs sine-wave burst, holdoff values below 20 µs still allow triggering on interior cycles, and stability appears exactly when the setting exceeds the burst length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup> Values in practice span a wide range: 2.2 µs to steady a complex test waveform during update-rate comparisons,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> and 400 ms to lock onto slow serial packets from an air conditioner control panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup>

Minimum holdoff values differ by instrument and represent the scope's own rearm overhead — 500 ns on a Rigol DS1052E, 40 ns on another digital scope, 100 ns on a Tektronix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> The adjustment mechanism is often awkward: rotary controls tend to jump if turned quickly, and velocity-sensitive encoders can [[overshoot]] badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Some instruments also offer a random holdoff mode as an alternative to a fixed one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup>

## Side effects

Increasing holdoff necessarily costs acquisition throughput, since the instrument spends the holdoff interval doing nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> On a digital scope the [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]] falls to approximately the reciprocal of the holdoff value: 100 µs of holdoff yields roughly 10 kHz of updates, against about 24 kHz with holdoff at its minimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> On an analog scope the same effect shows up as brightness, because fewer retraces per second mean less beam time on the phosphor — the trace visibly dims as holdoff is turned up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> This is useful diagnostically: setting holdoff to minimum, noting the trace brightness, then turning the control up and confirming the trace dims is a quick functional test that the holdoff circuitry works, as applied to the Tektronix 2225.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup>

Holdoff has no value for simple repetitive waveforms such as a plain [[sine-wave|sine wave]], where turning it up merely delays the sweep without changing what is displayed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> It is likewise not involved in straightforward edge triggering on a single transition, where trigger coupling is left at DC and [[noise]] reject and holdoff are both off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1678" title="EEVblog 1678 - Oscilloscope Trigger Warning">1678</a></sup>

## Implementation across instruments

Access varies. On an analog–digital combi scope the front-panel control marked delay position doubles as the holdoff control, acting as delay only when the delayed [[time-base|time base]] is engaged and as holdoff in normal operation; the fact that it gets a dedicated button reflects its importance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> On digital scopes it sits in the trigger mode or trigger setup menu.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup> The Tekway DST1102B assigns adjustment to the V0 knob.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> The Siglent SDS5000X exposes the effect on its trigger output, where the output pulse width visibly changes as holdoff is adjusted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Absence of the control is a genuine capability gap: the Digilent Open Scope MZ provides no trigger holdoff or trigger delay at all, leaving no way to trigger reliably on a packet following a dead space.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup>

Capturing once and zooming into stored memory is an alternative for a scope with sufficient record length, but a stable live display remains preferable because glitches and intermittent behaviour can be seen as they happen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> Segmented memory addresses a related class of problem for bursty signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup>
