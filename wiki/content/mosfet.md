The MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect [[transistor]]) is a [[voltage]]-driven transistor in which a gate voltage, rather than a continuous base [[current]] as in a BJT, switches the device on and off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> MOSFETs dominate switching applications — [[relay]] and [[led]] loads, [[pwm]] dimming, and the switching elements of DC-to-DC converters — because their on-[[resistance]] (RDS on) is far lower than the equivalent VCE saturation behaviour of a BJT, giving lower [[voltage-drop]] and more current and power handling in a given package size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>

## History

The MOSFET was invented in 1959 by Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs and demonstrated in 1960.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Z_c4wjBpSzE" title="Grokipedia vs Wikipedia">•</a></sup> The practical power MOSFET followed from work by Alex Lidow, whose key idea was to inject extra charge into a too-narrow region of the device so that it appeared wider to electrons; the device worked on the second attempt and immediately dropped in resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> Lidow's team launched the HEXFET in 1979, roughly eight months after the first product, using a cell structure about three times denser than the earlier racetrack layout; the first part was the IRF100, followed by devices such as the IRF130, and the density advantage made the power MOSFET commercially compelling against the bipolar transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

## MOSFET versus BJT

A MOSFET is voltage-driven: applying a gate voltage turns the transistor on, with no constant drive current required, whereas a BJT needs continuous base current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> MOSFETs are faster and better suited to high-[[frequency]] switching, including DC-to-DC converters operating into the megahertz region, which is why resonant-mode converter topologies such as LLC effectively cannot be built with bipolar transistors — their drive requirements are too high.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup> BJTs retain advantages: they are more robust, are not easily damaged by [[electrostatic-discharge|static discharge]], may have better overcurrent capability, can work at ultra-low voltages, and are simpler to drive and bias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup> With a BJT there is no gate oxide to blow [[via]] ESD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Consequently, when a transistor has failed in circuit and the choice is between a BJT and a MOSFET, the MOSFET is the more likely suspect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

## Key parameters

The headline specifications are drain-source voltage rating (VDSS), continuous and pulsed drain current, and RDS on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> For a pure switching application, voltage, current, and RDS on are essentially the only parameters that matter; other [[datasheet]] content can largely be ignored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Banner RDS on figures can mislead: a headline value may be specified at, for example, 9 A and VGS 10 V rather than at the full 18 A rating, so the conditions of the specification must be checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup>

Gate threshold voltage (VGS) matters greatly for drive. Characteristic output curves show drain current for different gate voltages — for one small SOT-23 device, 100–150 mA at 3.3–3.5 V gate drive versus over 400 mA at 5 V — and the transfer characteristic (drain current against VGS) is the other standard graph, plotted at several temperatures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

Input (gate) [[capacitance]] rises with die size: a small-signal part may be around 20 pF while a power device in a large package can be 630 pF, because higher-current MOSFETs need greater silicon area and capacitance scales with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

Every MOSFET contains an intrinsic body [[diode]] (substrate diode) across drain and source, an unavoidable consequence of the silicon construction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup> This diode is often adequate as a catch diode but must be checked for suitability; it can also be deliberately exploited, for example as free input-protection clamping in a [[multimeter]] front end, or it can feed energy back through an H-bridge into the supply capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup>

## Gate drive practice

A 3.3 V [[microcontroller]] cannot directly drive an ordinary 12 V MOSFET; the recommended solution is a logic-level MOSFET, otherwise a second transistor with a pull-up [[resistor]] is needed, taking care that the pull-up against gate capacitance does not produce an excessive rise time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> Distributors parametrically list logic-level parts, and a device specified to work at around 2.5 V is generally safe for logic-level use; "logic level" now typically means 3.3 V compatibility rather than 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Where a higher gate-source voltage is needed for low RDS on — for instance where 3.3 V drive is inadequate — the gate can be driven from a higher rail rather than directly from the microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Gates must never be left floating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup>

## Applications

Switching is the dominant use. Synchronous rectifiers in DC-to-DC converters use external N-channel MOSFET pairs driven by multiphase controllers, with [[switching-frequency]] sometimes locked to a sampling clock to avoid interference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup> A synchronous rectifier can replace a [[bridge-rectifier|diode bridge]] entirely: four MOSFETs under smart control eliminate the roughly 0.6 V per-diode drop of a conventional bridge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> H-bridges use four MOSFETs (with multiple paralleled devices per leg for high current) arranged around the load in an H shape for motor drive or [[inverter]] output, typically with P-channel devices on the high side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup> H-bridge stages feeding a [[transformer]] need DC blocking so that a shorted MOSFET does not place a dead short across the [[battery]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>

Battery systems make heavy use of MOSFETs. Lithium cell protection boards place MOSFETs (commonly a back-to-back N-channel/P-channel pair or dual devices) in series with the pack to disconnect on over-voltage or under-voltage, monitoring cell voltage through the same pin that powers the protection IC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-412" title="EEVblog #412 - Jawbone UP Pedometer Teardown">412</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-331" title="EEVblog #331 - Mailbag">331</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup> Multi-cell chargers use a pair of MOSFETs per cell to switch each battery between charge and bypass paths, with charge and bypass never on simultaneously since that would short the cell; body-diode orientation determines which paths conduct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> A battery emulator is essentially a [[power-supply]] with a series MOSFET or bank of MOSFETs driven to simulate a battery's [[equivalent-series-resistance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup>

In instrumentation, MOSFETs switch current-shunt resistors for range selection. Because a MOSFET's gate draws no current, the switch can sit in the low side or in series with a shunt, and devices in series can be cascaded across decade-spaced shunts (10 mΩ, 100 mΩ, 1 Ω, 10 Ω, 100 Ω) so that [[burden-voltage]] stays constant per range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> High-current ranges demand genuinely beefy parts — keeping error to one 12-bit ADC count (2.5 mV at 2 A) requires RDS on below 1.25 mΩ — while milliamp-range switches can be any jellybean N-channel device whose VGS requirement is met.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> High-end multimeters MOSFET-switch the 10 mΩ shunt of the amps range for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Where a switch's on-resistance would corrupt a low-[[impedance]] feedback network, the MOSFET is moved into the high-impedance op-amp input path instead, where no current flows and RDS on is irrelevant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

Other uses include resetting a peak-detector [[capacitor]] by shorting it with a MOSFET gate signal<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>, soft-latching power switches built around a P-channel pass MOSFET<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup>, active ripple filters in which the absence of gate current permits very large gate resistors and therefore very small filter capacitors<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>, and analog multiplexers such as the 4066, which are simply four independent MOSFET pass switches with some on-resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> A microcontroller's open-drain output is itself a MOSFET pulling a load low, and probing such a pin shows nothing switching unless a current path exists through the load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-890" title="EEVblog #890 - ArmourCard Active RFID Jamming Teardown">890</a></sup>

## Paralleling

MOSFETs may be paralleled for current, but current sharing cannot be left to the spread of internal resistances: a low-value ballast resistor in series with each device is required to even out the current — for example 0.3 Ω, 5 W resistors on each transistor in one parallel bank — because even same-wafer parts are not identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1698" title="EEVblog 1698 - Awesome Ebay AC Power Source: GW Instek APS-7050">1698</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>

## CMOS and digital inputs

A CMOS inverter is a complementary N-channel/P-channel MOSFET pair with gates tied together; with a slow-slewing input there is an interval where both transistors are partially on simultaneously, causing shoot-through current, which is why standard CMOS inputs demand fast edges and Schmitt-trigger inputs exist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Schmitt action inside a logic gate is implemented with stacked MOSFETs — four on the input plus source-follower feedback devices — rather than resistors, which cannot be fabricated economically in the process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

## Identification and repair

On a board, a MOSFET in an SO-8-style package is recognisable by its pinout: multiple pins shorted together form the drain on one side and the source on the other, with a single separate gate pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> The reference designator Q marks a transistor generically — BJT, MOSFET, or JFET.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Part-number conventions often encode the ratings directly: an 18N50 is an 18 A, 500 V device, and a 20N60 a 20 A, 600 V device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup> Typical markings such as "4303" or "4407" on small parts correspond to common MOSFET families and can be found by searching the number with "MOSFET" and "PDF" — though identical numbers from different makers are not equivalents: one 4303 carried 8 kV gate ESD protection while another had none, a difference that could cause field failures if substituted blindly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup>

Common failure modes are a drain-source short — detectable as near-zero or one-ohm resistance in-circuit — and a shorted gate, which should measure open; a shorted gate is not normal for a MOSFET.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup> Battery-protection MOSFETs are somewhat notorious for failing open, leaving good cells disconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-V0RWwSw96Sw" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL - Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RasOXxxEhCk" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL Investigation - Part 1">•</a></sup> Replacements for simple switching duty are forgiving: virtually any MOSFET with adequate maximum voltage and current ratings will work even if its RDS on and package differ, and serviceable parts are routinely salvaged from scrapped laptop power sections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> [[parametric-search]] by voltage, current, and RDS on is the reliable way to find equivalents; distributor "equivalent part" advertisements are not to be trusted, as they may point to a different package, polarity, or voltage class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Metal-tab versus insulated-tab packages are interchangeable if an insulating washer and sleeve are added where the tab would otherwise be live on the [[heat-sink|heatsink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup>

## Related devices and beyond silicon

The IGBT (insulated-gate bipolar transistor) combines desirable aspects of the MOSFET with the ruggedness of the bipolar transistor, and high-voltage parts (for example 650 V field-stop devices) have historically been easier to obtain as IGBTs than as MOSFETs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> Silicon-carbide FETs extend blocking voltage to around 1200 V, beyond the roughly 500–600 V ceiling of ordinary silicon MOSFET solutions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> Gallium-nitride devices exploit a two-dimensional electron gas to achieve efficiencies theoretically thousands of times beyond what a silicon MOSFET can reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> At the other extreme of scale, modified MOSFETs with channels of order 50 by 100 nm serve as charge readout devices in silicon quantum computers, sensing currents of about half a nanoamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1316" title="EEVblog #1316 - Quantum Computing for Electrical Engineers">1316</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup>
