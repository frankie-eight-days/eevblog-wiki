A ribbon cable is a flat, side-by-side bundle of conductors used to carry many parallel signals between two boards inside a single enclosure. In its classic form it terminates in insulation-displacement connectors on 0.1-inch headers, an arrangement old enough to appear in early-1980s portable computers and still common in modern instruments<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup>. It is the default answer whenever a design is split across more than one [[pcb|PCB]] — front panel to motherboard, [[processor]] board to display, [[power-supply|power supply]] to everything else — and much of what can be learned about a product's internal architecture can be read off where its ribbons go<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-880" title="EEVblog #880 - Mailbag">880</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup>.

## Role in multi-board architecture

Splitting a product across boards is usually forced by mechanics or manufacturing rather than by electronics, and the ribbon cable is what stitches the pieces back together. A keyboard instrument may be divided in two simply because nobody wants a board that long going through a pick-and-place machine, with a ribbon at each end of the split<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1108" title="EEVblog #1108 - Casio CT-X700 Keyboard Teardown">1108</a></sup>. A processor board that cannot fit everything gets a secondary board connected by a ribbon underneath, a board that disappears entirely once the design is consolidated with surface-mount parts<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup>. Where a [[connector]] has to sit flush with a front panel, the connector goes on a front panel board and its signals come back over a ribbon<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1612" title="EEVblog 1612 - Siglent SDS1000X HD 12 bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1612</a></sup>.

The same cable is routinely used as a distribution bus. Power supply outputs are brought over as a ribbon rather than discrete wiring because it is simply a neater way to do it<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-591" title="EEVblog #591 - Agilent 54622D Retro Mixed Signal Osciloscope Review &amp; Teardown">591</a></sup>. Instruments daisy-chain ribbons from board to board, and an omitted link in the chain is itself a clue — a supply built with a spare connector deliberately left unpopulated is a partly populated version of a larger design<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup>. Because a ribbon presents every rail on an accessible row of pins, it also makes a convenient [[troubleshooting]] point: pulling the cable from a power supply board exposes all the output voltages for measurement in a way that probing the assembled motherboard does not<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup>.

The choice of what to route over a ribbon defines the boundary of a design's fast circuitry. High-speed analog paths are carried on purpose-designed, expensive board-to-board interconnects; the ribbon is reserved for the lower-[[bandwidth]] run from the acquisition engine to the display<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup>. In a high-security electronic lock the reasoning is different but equally deliberate: only a few three- to five-pin ribbons cross to the keypad assembly, because putting no active electronics on the exposed outside face minimises the attack surface<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1484" title="EEVblog 1484 - Kaba Mas X-09 High Security Electronic Lock Teardown">1484</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup>.

## Current, decoupling and grounding

Ribbon conductors are thin, and treating them as a power bus has consequences. A design that omits local bulk decoupling and instead draws large [[current]] pulses through a long ribbon from another board forces those pulses down thin wire shared with low-power digital lines, and the power and [[ground]] rails bounce accordingly at currents of a couple of hundred milliamps<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>. The remedy is local bulk [[capacitance]] — hundreds of microfarads where the current is actually consumed<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>.

Where a ribbon is the whole power path, pin count is the capacity. A standard 0.1-inch ribbon carrying an entire instrument's supply is questionable practice; multiple pins are paralleled, but the working rule of thumb is roughly an amp per pin<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup>. Instruments that do this properly use a wide cable dedicated to the job, such as the 10- or 12-way ribbon that feeds an analog section from its rectified secondary-side capacitors<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>.

Return paths through ribbons are easy to misread. Reversed wire colouring on a supply harness, combined with a ribbon carrying the return current, produced a measured current of only tens of milliamps and an incorrect picture of where the current was actually flowing<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>. Mechanically, a power-carrying ribbon connector that allows insertion at an offset angle can end up misaligned on its pins, which is enough to blow a supply [[fuse]] on reassembly<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup>.

## Emissions, shielding and isolation

A long unshielded ribbon carrying a parallel memory or expansion bus off to an external box, with no metallised [[shielding]] in the case and no chokes on the I/O lines, is an emissions problem by construction<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup>. Where designers take the issue seriously, the ribbons are shielded, earthed, and the earth connection properly terminated rather than left loose — a decision that has to be taken up front because it drives assembly labour<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-969" title="EEVblog #969 - Vintage $80k NLE Video Editor Teardown">969</a></sup>. Implementations range from foil shielding over the runs to the touch panel and [[lcd|LCD]]<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup>, to a ferrite around the cable to the front panel<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup>, to shielded ribbons in a gold-foil pouch<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-969" title="EEVblog #969 - Vintage $80k NLE Video Editor Teardown">969</a></sup>. In an [[oscilloscope]], an entire shield plate can be present over a ribbon with no board beneath it at all<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup>.

Creepage and clearance apply to the cable as well as the board. The distance between two pins across the surface of a ribbon connector is creepage; clearance is the air-to-air distance, and only applies where the boards or cable fold to create a physical air gap<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-388" title="EEVblog #388 - Fake Apple USB Charger Teardown">388</a></sup>. A charger that runs primary and secondary across the same ribbon cable with an inadequate gap fails on that basis<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-388" title="EEVblog #388 - Fake Apple USB Charger Teardown">388</a></sup>.

## Failure modes

Ribbon cables fail mechanically far more often than electrically, and the failures cluster in a few patterns. Conductors break, most commonly at the ends where flexing and stress concentrate, and a break in the middle of an undamaged cable is unusual enough to be suspicious<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>. A cable can shear off entirely where the assembly it is bonded to comes unglued<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sqB-PnvQfO4" title="Samsung Galaxy Tab A 10.1&quot; - REPAIR (Part 2 of 2)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o4S-TkTuHZs" title="Samsung Galaxy Tab A Troubleshooting">•</a></sup>. Micro-cracks produce intermittents that resist wiggle testing<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-afErPINq8qc" title="Mini Arcade Game Console Repair OOPSIE!">•</a></sup>, and a repeatedly reseated or dislodged cable can be the entire fault<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uDzj3xdqi7Y" title="JBL Wave Flex Earbuds REPAIR">•</a></sup>. Repeated flexing across a hinge or removable assembly is a known long-term reliability risk; one early portable computer's removable keyboard ribbon was replaced in later revisions by a curly cable with a right-angle attachment<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup>.

The first diagnostic step is therefore to reseat the cable at both ends, since the fault may be mechanical rather than electrical at all<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup>. When continuity does fail, the cable is not automatically the culprit: a break traced through two ribbons and at least six jumper links turned out to be in a [[pcb-trace|PCB trace]], not in any of the cables<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1673" title="EEVblog 1673 - JBL Partybox 310 Repair - Part 2 (SPOILER)">1673</a></sup>. Cables are also easy to damage during repair — excess heat will melt flat flex<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup> — and the reassembly hazard is real enough that designers add plastic retainer clips to stop a cable flapping loose and being crushed as the case slides on<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup>, or tape it down so it does not get pinched under the metalwork<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup>.

## Orientation errors

Many 0.1-inch ribbon headers are unshrouded, unkeyed and carry no pin 1 marker, which makes reversed insertion a recurring and destructive mistake — in one case releasing the magic smoke from a chip almost instantly<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-513" title="EEVblog #513 - Mailbag">513</a></sup>. The same error appears as a non-working prototype that looks electrically dead<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-313" title="EEVblog #313 - Bus Pirate LCD Debugging">313</a></sup>, as a display fault chased at length before pin 1 was verified against the far-end pin<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup>, and as damage to a driver board plugged into an [[arduino|Arduino]]<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>. Cables fitted backwards during a previous repair leave a diagnostic signature: a header pin with a trace going to it but no connection at the other end<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup>.

## Practical judgement

Ribbon cables are cheap, flexible and fast to design in, and that is precisely why they proliferate past the point of good sense. Small four-way cables are fiddly to handle<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1291" title="EEVblog #1291 - Mailbag">1291</a></sup>. Mounting a panel LCD on standoffs and running a ribbon to it wastes enclosure space and requires extra hardware, where a PCB-mount display can be fixed rigidly with no mounting screws at all<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-644" title="EEVblog #644 - How To Design Front Panels On Extruded Enclosures - µSupply Part 14">644</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-258" title="EEVblog #258 - PSU Housing Design - Part 11">258</a></sup>. A ribbon crossing to a front panel to serve a single switch is effort spent that a differently placed switch would not have required<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1278" title="EEVblog #1278 - Mailbag">1278</a></sup>.

Against that, the ribbon frequently is the right answer. Running a [[flat-flex-cable|flat cable]] at right angles to reach a rear-panel connector is easier than designing a right-angle connector and getting it out the back<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup>. Where board-to-board interconnects are used instead, contact reliability depends on the boards being rigidly held; if they are only located by rear-panel screws, moving one board can disturb the others<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1395" title="EEVblog 1395 - Onkyo Repair SUCCESS">1395</a></sup>. And a ribbon is what makes a second-source design possible: a spare unpopulated footprint paralleled with the existing display connector lets the same board accept whichever TFT panel is available, by moving the cable across<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup>.
