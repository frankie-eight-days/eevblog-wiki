Contact resistance is the unwanted [[resistance]] appearing at any mechanical junction in a circuit — a switch contact, a [[relay]] contact, a connector, a banana plug, a [[battery]] spring terminal, a probe tip pressed against a pad. It is normally small, in the milliohm to sub-ohm range, but it is never zero, it is rarely stable, and it sits in series with whatever is being measured or powered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> It matters wherever the quantity of interest is itself small: a low-value [[current-shunt]], a precision resistance standard, a [[voltage]] measured across a high-[[current]] path, or a switch read as an analogue value rather than a logic level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup>

## Typical magnitudes

A good mechanical switch contact is on the order of milliohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> A fresh tactile dome measures down in the tens of milliohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup> A well-made flanged banana jack repeats to within a couple of hundred microohms as the plug is rotated, pulled part way out, and reinserted — around 500 microohms of movement in total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1052" title="EEVblog #1052 - BEWARE Crap Banana Plugs!">1052</a></sup> Gold pogo pin probe tips measure a gold pad at 0.238 ohms and shift by only a couple of milliohms as the pins are worked up and down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-407" title="EEVblog #407 - Mailbag">407</a></sup> A pair of ordinary test leads contributes roughly 55 to 56 milliohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup>

At the other extreme, cheap banana plugs have been measured at 0.243 ohms, rising to 0.7 ohms and beyond 2 ohms under nothing more than a light wiggle, with the [[solder-joint]] itself demonstrably sound — a resistance far worse than would normally be expected of such a plug.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1052" title="EEVblog #1052 - BEWARE Crap Banana Plugs!">1052</a></sup> A degraded tactile switch dome can vary from a couple of ohms under hard pressure up to many hundreds of ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup>

## Instability, not magnitude, is the real problem

A contact resistance that is high but constant can be calibrated or nulled out. One that moves cannot. The diagnostic technique is mechanical: wiggle the probes while watching the reading. If the display holds steady, the contact is sound; if it climbs or jumps, the measurement is not trustworthy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1052" title="EEVblog #1052 - BEWARE Crap Banana Plugs!">1052</a></sup> Firm, consistent pressure on the probe tips is essential, and very sharp high-quality tips are needed to pierce the oxidation on solder joints and reach metal underneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>

This matters most when tracing a small differential. Locating a short on a [[power-rail]] by measuring voltage-drop-style resistance across a board means chasing a delta — starting from 0.11 ohms at the connector and hunting for the point where it falls toward 0.06 ohms — so repeatability of the contact dominates the whole exercise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Measuring through a plug-in memory module rather than directly on the board raises the reading to 0.17 ohms purely through the connector contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>

Contact instability also masquerades as bad data. A single 500-ohm reading among values that should all be near 100 ohms is far more likely to be a contact artefact than a real result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1658" title="EEVblog 1658 - TUTORIAL: Mean vs Median">1658</a></sup> Even in a precision bench setup, brushing a 1 kΩ [[resistor]] under test with a hand adds enough contact resistance to shift a reading from 999.991 ohms to 1.000008 kΩ and push the result into a different histogram bin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

## Cancelling it out

Four-wire (Kelvin) measurement removes the resistance of the leads and the contacts from the result entirely: the same [[pcb-trace]] reads 212 milliohms in two-wire mode and 51 milliohms in four-wire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> Where four-wire is unavailable, the constant part can be nulled — subtracting a measured 0.15 ohms of combined lead and contact resistance from every reading in a batch to recover the true resistor values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> Neither trick removes the varying part.

The same principle applies to sensing voltage on a live circuit: the sense connection must be taken at the point of interest, not upstream of the contacts. Moving a voltmeter terminal from a battery's end cap back to the instrument's input jack introduces enough drop through a short lead and a spring terminal contact to change the reading measurably at 250 mA; with the current turned down, the discrepancy vanishes because there is no current to develop a drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> For the same reason, battery cutoff testing should measure at the battery terminal rather than trusting the supply's own display, though at low power the contact contribution is small enough to ignore.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Contacts and wiring in a discharge rig also mean a nominal cutoff voltage is reached earlier than the cell's true state warrants, leaving real capacity — potentially a couple of hundred milliamp-hours — unextracted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup>

## Swamping low-value shunts

In [[current-measurement]] the [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] is deliberately made tiny, which puts contact resistance directly in competition with it. A 10 milliohm milliamp-range shunt is the same order of magnitude as the contact resistance of a typical switch, plus the connectors and other joints in the path, so the parasitic resistance swamps the intended value and destroys the accuracy of the range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> The remedy is to tap the sense connection at the shunt itself, so the amplifier sees only the shunt and not the switch in series with it; the switch still adds to total [[burden-voltage]], but burden voltage is a far more forgiving budget.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> On higher ranges using 10 ohm and 10 kΩ shunts, switch contact resistance is negligible and can be ignored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

[[breadboard]] contacts are poor enough to matter in precision analogue work: unexplained residual error in a 1 A current source built from 0.02 percent resistors was attributable to breadboard contact resistance rather than the components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

## Heat and high current

Contact resistance dissipates power, and at high current that shows up as heat. Alligator clips carrying 11 amps get noticeably warm for exactly this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> The effect is also self-reinforcing and time-dependent: pitted contacts in a mains switch can run slightly resistive, heat up, and degrade further until the switch can no longer deliver enough power to a [[transformer]], causing every downstream rail to sag — a fault that appears only after the instrument has been running for a while.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> In a twelve-panel solar string, a single joint developing higher resistance and heating under load is a plausible cause of a significant output drop, since a long string contains many connections and any one of them can be the culprit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup>

Where a low-resistance bond genuinely matters at high current, it is made explicitly rather than assumed. A 10 kW laser supply routes a dedicated bus bar between all the [[transistor]] cases rather than relying on the metal of the heat transfer block to carry the connection, purely to obtain lower contact resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup>

## Precision resistance standards and decade boxes

In decade resistance boxes and resistance calibrators, contact resistance is the dominant design problem, not the resistors. The resistors themselves are stable and do not age much; the reliability and repeatability of the switching is what the price buys.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup> Every mechanical switch in the chain has dirty contacts, bounce, and a certain minimum contact resistance, which sets a floor on how low the bottom decade can usefully go.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

Several countermeasures recur. High-quality thumbwheel switches are custom made with extra-thick gold plating specifically for low contact resistance maintained over age.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup> Precision calibrators use top-quality relays on the low ranges, sometimes with multiple contacts paralleled to drive the accumulated contact resistance and long-term error inside the target spec.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-545" title="EEVblog #545 - Vintage Design Rant">545</a></sup> The requirement is range-dependent: on the kilohm ranges physically smaller relays with somewhat higher contact resistance are acceptable, and by the megohm ranges contact resistance ceases to matter at all — it is unmeasurably small relative to the value — at which point the critical parameter flips to the insulation resistance of the relay, which is why ultra-high-insulation reed relays appear at the top end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-545" title="EEVblog #545 - Vintage Design Rant">545</a></sup>

Switching topology also multiplies the problem. A decade built from normally-closed switches puts four contact resistances in the path per decade instead of one, which is why a home-built box of that design should start its lowest decade at 10 ohms rather than attempting a 1 ohm or 0.1 ohm decade — unless high-quality switches are used throughout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Contact resistance is also the first suspect when a decade box gives nonsensical readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1586" title="EEVblog 1586 -  Stranger Things Decade Resistor Box + Repair">1586</a></sup> Boxes that have sat in storage need their switches operated a number of times before they will meet their minimum contact resistance spec again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup>

At the level of a dedicated resistance standard, contacts join temperature and lead resistance as one of the error terms that must be controlled, using low-noise materials such as tellurium copper for the contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-834" title="EEVblog #834 - Wekomm Resistance Standard">834</a></sup>

## Switch matrices read as analogue values

A switch matrix decoded as pure logic is largely immune to contact resistance: whether a closed contact reads zero ohms, one ohm, five ohms, or a couple of hundred ohms, it either registers as pressed or it does not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> The tolerance disappears when multiple switches are multiplexed onto a single [[microcontroller]] pin by having each switch insert a different resistance into a divider read by an ADC. In that arrangement a dodgy contact varying from a couple of ohms to many hundreds of ohms shifts the divider output and causes the wrong key to be decoded, or inconsistent behaviour with no clean failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> The technique itself is a long-established way to get many switches onto one line; the vulnerability is that it converts a contact-resistance problem into a functional one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup>

The measured resistance of a tactile dome is not a property of the dome alone — it depends on the contact, the surface, and the materials it is mated to, which is why dome test equipment measures it in situ alongside trip force, return force, free height, displacement, and tactile slope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1291" title="EEVblog #1291 - Mailbag">1291</a></sup>

## Plating and probe choice

Gold plating is the standard answer for a contact that must stay low over time, both in precision switchgear and at the probe tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uV9mW0rpRxg" title="eevBLAB #33 - Why Gold Plated Probes Matter">•</a></sup> Gold-plated [[multimeter]] probes make better contact than plain silver-type ones, giving faster continuity response and lower contact resistance, and the price premium is usually small; general purpose silver probes are better regarded as electrical-work probes, made to be shoved hard into contacts, and are a poor choice for electronics use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uV9mW0rpRxg" title="eevBLAB #33 - Why Gold Plated Probes Matter">•</a></sup> Where sharpness is prioritised for fine-pitch work, there is a trade-off — fine pogo-pin probes can carry noticeably high contact resistance despite being mechanically ideal for the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup>

## Wear and life testing

Because contact resistance degrades with mechanical cycling, it is the natural pass/fail metric for switch endurance testing. A rotary [[range-switch]] can be instrumented by wiring out its off position and reading it through an ADC in a divider, with a parallel resistor included so an open contact still produces a finite, calculable value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup> Such a switch measured a nominal 0.9 ohms rock steady at the start of life, still read 0.85 ohms after 11,000 cycles, and remained around a couple of ohms while visible wear accumulated — plating marks on the gold pads and fiberglass dust pushed in from an unplated hole edge — proving that visible wear and electrical degradation are not the same thing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup> Failure at approximately 25,000 cycles came not as a resistance number out of spec but as intermittent misbehaviour in one mode, with the contact resistance still nominally acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup> Fixturing is itself a source of error in such tests: poor positioning of the unit in the jig corrupted a run's contact resistance data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup>
