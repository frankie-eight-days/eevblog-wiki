An analog oscilloscope, or [[cathode]] ray [[oscilloscope]], displays [[voltage]] against time by sweeping an electron beam across a phosphor-coated CRT in real time, with no digitisation and no memory anywhere in the signal path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> It remains a genuinely useful bench instrument decades after digital storage scopes displaced it, both because a second-hand one costs almost nothing and because the way it works is the way every oscilloscope works underneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> Its one structural limitation is that it cannot store a [[waveform]], which is precisely the capability a digital storage oscilloscope adds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup>

## How the display works

The instrument measures voltage with respect to time: voltage on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal, with the vertical scale set by a volts-per-division control and a division being one square of the graticule.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> The sweep always begins at the left-hand edge of the screen, travels to the right, and then retraces back to the start; the image exists only because that sweep is repeated continuously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-153" title="EEVblog #153 - YouScope Demo on a Digital Scope">153</a></sup>

Brightness is not a display setting so much as a physical consequence: the intensity of any point on the screen is determined by how long the beam spends in that position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> A waveform that repeats identically every sweep is written over the same path each time and appears bright and fine; a runt pulse that occurs one sweep in a million is written once and is effectively invisible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> The phosphor supplies a natural persistence, holding the illuminated point for a period before it decays, which is what gives the analog display its intensity-graded appearance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> Turning the intensity up increases the effective persistence and thickens the trace, but there is a hard limit to how far that can be pushed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup>

Traditional analog front ends have a Gaussian [[frequency-response]], for which the classic relationship holds: rise time equals 0.35 divided by the [[bandwidth]] of the scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Modern digital scopes do not necessarily follow it, since their analog input channels often use a maximally flat response instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup>

The CRT itself is an electromagnetic device with large deflection coils that radiate a substantial [[magnetic-field]], commonly at a [[switching-frequency]] around 25 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1329" title="EEVblog #1329 - Magnetic Field Shielding DEMONSTRATED">1329</a></sup>

## Triggering

Because the concept originates with the sweep of a CRT beam, analog scopes are the natural place to understand triggering, and the behaviour carries directly over to digital scopes, which work the same way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> Trigger holdoff is a dedicated knob on most higher-end older analog instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> Adding holdoff has a visible side effect unique to the analog display: the screen is no longer re-traced as often, so the trace dims noticeably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

AC trigger coupling sits at the top of the trigger coupling selector on essentially all analog scopes, positioned as the de facto default, because it makes probing an unknown circuit easier by removing the DC content and giving an easier trigger point to work from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-685" title="EEVblog #685 - What Is Oscilloscope AC Trigger Coupling?">685</a></sup> Trigger coupling is independent of input channel coupling; the input coupling setting makes no difference to triggering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-685" title="EEVblog #685 - What Is Oscilloscope AC Trigger Coupling?">685</a></sup> A digital scope offering AC and DC trigger coupling should behave identically to an old-school analog scope regardless of whether the selection is implemented with a real [[capacitor]] and [[relay]] or entirely in the digital domain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-685" title="EEVblog #685 - What Is Oscilloscope AC Trigger Coupling?">685</a></sup>

Alternate trigger, which jumps between two channels fast enough that both appear on screen simultaneously, was an analog feature that modern digital scopes have largely dropped, replaced by pattern or OR triggering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup> Some analog scopes also offered trigger view, which displayed the trigger signal itself as an extra trace; a four-channel Kikusui COS 6200 could be made to show twelve or fourteen waveforms this way, counting trigger views and displayed sweep waveforms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1418" title="EEVblog 1418 - The Most EMBARRASSING Repair!">1418</a></sup>

## The noise myth

A persistent claim holds that analog oscilloscopes are lower noise than digital ones. This is not true.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup> Digital scopes are not noisier; their sampling behaviour and update rate simply make them capable of displaying more of the noise that is genuinely present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> An analog scope averages the signal out across its screen, so uncorrelated high-[[frequency]] content is written too dimly and too infrequently to be seen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

This can be demonstrated directly. A 30% noise component added to a [[sine-wave]] is barely visible on an analog scope because the noise is uncorrelated to the sweep and each excursion is written only briefly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> Photographing the screen with a long exposure reveals the dim phosphor the eye cannot resolve, and the noise appears superimposed on the waveform exactly as the digital scope showed it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> The ridges around the bezel of analog scopes exist for this purpose: they accepted a hood so a film camera could be mounted for long exposures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> A direct amplitude comparison at 5 mV per division shows common-mode switching noise of exactly the same amplitude on analog and digital.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup> Turning the analog intensity all the way down produces an apparently clean flat line while that common-mode noise is still present on the signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup>

There are at least six mechanisms besides the [[noise-floor]] of the ADC that affect how noisy a digital scope appears: [[memory-depth]], analog bandwidth, boxcar averaging, the intensity-graded display, the update rate, and the [[sample-rate]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Boxcar averaging in high-resolution mode thins the trace in almost exactly the way an analog scope's phosphor persistence does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

Two genuine advantages do remain on the analog side. A digital scope can radiate its own switching noise into a low-level measurement, which an analog scope does not do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup> And at high dynamic range with small vertical settings, a digital scope is limited by its 8-bit converter — at 500 µV per division the individual quantisation steps become visible in the trace, where the analog display stays continuous.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1716" title="EEVblog 1716 - University Dumpster Diving: Kikusui Oscilloscope">1716</a></sup>

## What an analog scope is still good for

The absence of storage is the whole of the deficit; apart from that the instrument is highly useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> A good lab is still worth equipping with one for its higher update rate, its lower apparent noise, and its ability to show signals a low-end digital scope cannot capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-51" title="EEVblog #51 - A tour of the EEVblog Electronics Lab">51</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup> It is a strong choice for measuring [[power-supply]] ripple and noise, where high-frequency detail matters and the vertical range is small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Analog subtraction for differential measurements is immediate, where a digital scope's math function may be sluggish because the processing is done in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

It is also good at slow signals: wound out to seconds per division, the beam crawls visibly across the screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> Bode plotting works on an analog scope with nothing more than a sweep generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> Oscillator [[calibration]] by watching a waveform drift against a reference trigger is fundamentally an analog technique that works identically on either instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-457" title="EEVblog #457 - Oscillator Calibration Followup">457</a></sup> Entry-level 20 MHz analog scopes frequently included a built-in curve tracer, which measured component parameters using the XY mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> A few analog scopes offered measurement functions, but these were cursor-based rather than the automatic parametric measurements a digital scope provides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup>

The intensity-graded displays on modern digital scopes exist specifically to replicate the analog look.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> The value of that emulation is judged against a real CRT: 64 shading levels with fast update is close, 256 levels closer still, and colour grading of the waveform is a separate matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-591" title="EEVblog #591 - Agilent 54622D Retro Mixed Signal Osciloscope Review &amp; Teardown">591</a></sup> A display advertised as fast but showing hard vertical lines and no true persistence does not achieve it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup>

## Where an analog scope loses

Single-shot capture is impossible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 2 of 2 - Comparison of PC Based Oscilloscopes">13</a></sup> An event that happens once cannot be held on a phosphor screen, and a rare glitch that appears one sweep in a thousand and lasts a millisecond will not build enough brightness to be seen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> A digital scope can be stopped and the captured waveform then zoomed, measured, and analysed at leisure with the probe disconnected, which is the single most valuable thing it does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Faster waveform update rates improve the statistical probability of catching an infrequent event — 100,000 waveforms per second gives far better odds than 100.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>

The engineering judgment that follows is unambiguous: a low-end digital scope capable of single-shot capture is more useful than a top-of-the-line analog one with dual time bases and high bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Someone with a limited budget and no scope at all should buy a cheap DSO first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

The complaint that analog scopes are harder to use does not hold up. A digital scope does the same things and differs only in storing the waveform rather than tracing it continuously on a CRT; used manually, there is no difference at all, and a well-laid-out analog front panel can be easier than a menu-driven digital one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> The counterargument is that a beginner who learns only to press the auto-set button has learned nothing about operating an oscilloscope, which is the basis for recommending an analog scope as a learning instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup>

## Buying second-hand

A working dual-channel 20 MHz analog scope can reliably be found for $50 or less, and they are given away free by universities, labs, and individuals on forums.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-498" title="EEVblog #498 - How to get a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay">498</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> Twenty megahertz dual channel is the baseline; 50 MHz is nicer and 100 MHz better still.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> Paying more than about $50 is poor value — the money is better saved toward a $300 to $400 modern digital storage scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup>

Practical search tactics matter more than the money. Many sellers do not put the word analog in the title or listing category, so filtering on it is a good first pass but misses bargains, and browsing categories catches items listed by people who do not know what they have.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-498" title="EEVblog #498 - How to get a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay">498</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Watch lists across several brands, familiarity with completed listings, and patience are what actually produce the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-498" title="EEVblog #498 - How to get a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay">498</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Surplus auction houses carry high-end analog scopes including 2465Bs, though often only in pallet lots rather than singles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1184" title="EEVblog #1184 - Ebay $200 6.5 Digit Meter Search">1184</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup>

When assessing a listing, prefer one whose photographs show waveforms on both channels — a trace on screen means the EHT section works, and roughly 95% of the time a scope showing a trace will be functional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup> A tilted trace in a photograph is only trace rotation and is trivially adjusted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Very old instruments are best avoided; 1980s models are preferable to 1970s ones for general use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup> Almost any analog oscilloscope will do the job, and there are essentially no bad ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup>

Cheap pocket DSOs, [[fpga]] scope kits, and AVR-based oscilloscope kits are not a substitute at the same price, lacking proper high-bandwidth vertical attenuators and proper triggering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup>

## Notable instruments

The Tektronix 2465 and 2465B, 400 MHz, are regarded as about the best analog scopes Tektronix produced, excepting the micro-channel-plate CRT models with their storage capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> They are long in the tooth, last manufactured around the mid-1990s, and the 2465 and 300 series both have known long-term failure modes with active repair communities around them — a 2465B restored to specification after thirty years may need nothing more than a full recap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup>

The Tektronix 2225 is a 50 MHz dual-channel scope of late-1980s vintage, notable for a 500 µV per division vertical range, one of only about three oscilloscopes offering it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1000" title="EEVblog #1000 - Fundamental Mailbag Retro Teardown Shootouts are Bullshit">1000</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> The Tektronix 475 is a 200 MHz instrument dating from 1972 and sold into the early 1980s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-jmb9ICnI8xI" title="Dumpster Tektronix 475 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 1">•</a></sup> The HP 1740A is a 100 MHz dual-channel scope with delayed [[time-base]], auto and sweep and intensified modes — about as advanced as an analog scope became in its day — and its vertical section includes a custom differential-output driver chip rather than being entirely discrete.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup> The HP 1700 series was available with a [[multimeter]] attachment on top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-756" title="EEVblog #756 - Mailbag">756</a></sup>

Hitachi and Goldstar, the latter being the original name of LG, both made good analog scopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup> Hameg produced high-quality German-made instruments, including combiscopes, at the cost of a confusing user interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-153" title="EEVblog #153 - YouScope Demo on a Digital Scope">153</a></sup> Hung Cheng 20 MHz dual-channel units turn up cheaply and are serviceable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Prices in period were substantial: a Hitachi V5500 50 MHz was $1795 in 1980 and even a 15 MHz unit was $595, while a Goldstar 20 MHz with 1 mV per division sensitivity was around $650.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup> Dave Jones's first oscilloscope was a Kikusui COS5020, a 20 MHz dual-channel single-time-base unit bought new for about $800.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-54" title="EEVblog #54 - Electronics - When I was a boy...">54</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ln_XJDPKJlc" title="eevBLAB 103 - Is an $800 Oscilloscope OVERKILL?">•</a></sup>

## Combiscopes

A number of instruments are true analog scopes with a digital storage board added, rather than digital scopes emulating analog. Switching storage mode off returns them to normal analog operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup> Their digital performance is usually poor by modern standards — a Hameg 20 MHz example sampled at 20 MS/s with 2K per channel and had no sin(x)/x interpolation, so no meaningful storage bandwidth should be expected from it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup> A 40 MHz Kikusui with a digital add-on managed 25 MS/s into 1,024 points of [[sample-memory]], enough for the display; switching from analog to storage mode makes the 8-bit ADC resolution steps visible in the waveform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1716" title="EEVblog 1716 - University Dumpster Diving: Kikusui Oscilloscope">1716</a></sup> On such a machine the storage board can be removed entirely and the instrument still works as a dual-channel analog scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup>

The Philips PM 3370B, later badged Fluke, is an analog scope with digitally controlled focus, intensity, and trace settings held in [[battery]]-backed memory; with the battery dead the settings must be reset at every power-up, so it is not purely analog in that respect even though it works as an analog scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup> The Tektronix 213 is a 1 MHz portable combining a classic analog scope architecture — a matched JFET input pair on the front end — with switching that routes the probe through to multimeter circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup>

Some late analog scopes added digital readout and cursors without becoming digital scopes, which can make a listing photograph look misleadingly like a DSO.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup>

## Supply

The population of surviving instruments was reduced deliberately. At least one major test equipment manufacturer ran a trade-in programme that accepted any scope, including old analog CROs and other brands, against a discount on a new instrument, and required its dealers to destroy the traded units and provide evidence they had done so — including perfectly functional and relatively high-end scopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a4Xpsenpd6E" title="The Amp Hour #643 - Calibration &amp; Repair with Ian Johnston">•</a></sup> Working instruments also continue to be dumped by shops and universities when they fail or when premises change hands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-jmb9ICnI8xI" title="Dumpster Tektronix 475 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 1">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1716" title="EEVblog 1716 - University Dumpster Diving: Kikusui Oscilloscope">1716</a></sup>
