A parallel circuit is one in which two or more elements are connected across the same pair of nodes, so that every element sees the same [[voltage]] and the total [[current]] divides among them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> It is one half of the pair of connection rules — series and parallel — that all elementary circuit analysis is built on, and which introductory electronics curricula treat as foundational material.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> The configuration matters far beyond textbook problems: it governs how resistances and impedances combine, how current and heat are shared between duplicated parts, and, most awkwardly in practice, why a component measured while still soldered into a board so often reads wrong.

## Combining elements

For resistors, the total is found from the reciprocal relation 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 and so on for as many branches as there are.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Conductance, defined simply as G = 1/R, exists partly to make this tractable: inverting every value at the outset turns a chain of nested reciprocals into a plain sum, which makes a real difference once a network contains more than two parallel elements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> The same reciprocal form carries over to AC once each element has been reduced to an [[impedance]] — 1/ZT = 1/Z1 + 1/Z2 + 1/Z3 is identical in structure to the DC case, with the caveat that the quantities are complex and carry a phase component, so the additions and divisions are complex-number operations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Parallel combinations are commonly written inline with a double-bar notation, so that a series-parallel network reduces to an expression such as R1 plus R2 in parallel with R3.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup>

Because the branches share nodes, they share voltage: two elements in parallel necessarily drop the same voltage, which is why a pair of parallel LEDs specified at a 2 V forward drop can be treated as a single 2 V element when working out a midpoint voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> Current, conversely, splits at the junction according to Kirchhoff's current law — current in equals current out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> The compact current divider formula that follows from this applies only to the two-[[resistor]] case and cannot be extended term by term to three or more branches the way the total-[[resistance]] formula can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

## Reducing networks by symmetry

Where a network is symmetric, points at the same potential can be shorted together without changing anything, and this is the standard route into otherwise intimidating three-dimensional networks such as the resistor cube. Physically shorting two equipotential corners places previously separate resistors directly in parallel, collapsing each pair to R/2.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> Branches carrying no current can likewise be shorted out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> Applied repeatedly, the cube reduces first to a simple parallel circuit of three resistors — each branch being a series chain of 2R, 2R and 3R — and eventually to two resistors in parallel with a further resistance in series, at which point it is an ordinary exam-level problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>

## Sharing current and dissipation

Paralleling identical parts to divide current or heat is routine, but the division is only ever as equal as the parts are matched. An [[led|LED]] tube built as four strings of twenty-four LEDs in series, with the four strings simply paralleled and no current-sharing resistors at all, relies entirely on the strings themselves matching closely enough to share roughly equally — imperfect, but adequate for the purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup> The same idea appears at die level, where a six-pin package containing three LED dies wired out to separate pins can be paralleled by shorting the pins together on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> Parts rated nominally at 60 mA and run at 20 mA are not being stressed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup>

Uneven sharing shows up as uneven heating. In a bank of paralleled wire-wound resistors, the units nearest the middle of the board fared worse than those at the edges, the outermost being the exception.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-SoeVNaz7KGc" title="EEVsmoke #2 - Magic Smoke Captured!">•</a></sup> On another board, burning concentrated down the middle near a group of 91 Ω resistors, with the failure attributable to a combination of the resistor and LED arrangements heating the same region rather than to any single part being run out of spec.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup>

Paralleling is also used deliberately to spread dissipation in protection circuits. Low-leakage [[diode]]-connected transistors used at [[multimeter]] inputs do not survive genuinely high-voltage faults in the ohms range even with a series PTC, because the PTC is too slow to stop the junctions frying; the common remedies are cascoding or paralleling the circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup> In the EEVblog [[121gw|121GW]], two such devices are placed in parallel so they share the dissipation, which in turn requires the transistors to be selected and individually tested to ensure they meet the safety standards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup>

## Parallel elements as a measurement trap

The single most common practical consequence of [[parallel-connection|parallel connection]] is that in-circuit measurements are unreliable. A 10 kΩ resistor measured in an unpowered board can read 3.5 kΩ, because other components in parallel with it offer alternative paths for the ohmmeter's test current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> The rule is to break the circuit before measuring resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> The same trap applies to semiconductors: a diode with a 68 Ω resistor and a coil forming a loop around it presents well under 100 Ω in parallel with the junction under test, so it reads tens of ohms in both directions and appears shorted when it is fine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> An in-circuit reading that produces a plausible-looking total — a string of resistors measuring 131 Ω, for instance — still cannot be trusted, since something in parallel may be responsible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup>

Some in-circuit measurements survive precisely because of the parallel arithmetic. A large [[electrolytic-capacitor|electrolytic capacitor]] sitting across an output rail is by a wide margin the lowest-impedance device in that parallel network, so a low-voltage ESR meter — low enough not to switch on any active devices — effectively ignores everything else and reads the [[capacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup> The result can be decisive: a reading of 0.24 Ω against a specification an order of magnitude lower condemns the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup> The exception is when several capacitors sit in parallel with one another, which confounds the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup> Conversely, two identical capacitors side by side that are not in parallel and that measure identically can reasonably be taken as good.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup>

## Instrument loading

A measuring instrument connected across a circuit is itself a parallel element. Adding a second 10 MΩ multimeter across a high-impedance divider visibly drops the indicated voltage, because the divider ratio has changed — which is why precision meters offer a high-impedance mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> The effect becomes dramatic when the added impedance is low: an analog meter of 120 kΩ [[input-impedance|input impedance]] placed in parallel with a 10 MΩ digital meter gives a combination that is essentially just 120 kΩ, and a reverse-biased Schottky diode that measured close to the full 5 V collapsed to 0.33 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Lower ranges on the analog meter, with correspondingly lower input impedance, drove the reading toward zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## Parallel connections in practice

Decoupling is a standard parallel arrangement: several bypass capacitors of different values — for example 10 µF, 1 µF and 0.1 µF — are placed in parallel near a chip's supply pin to cover different impedances across [[frequency]], and on a [[schematic]] they should be drawn grouped and physically close to the chip rather than tied back through a long line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> Elsewhere on a board, a capacitor placed in parallel with an input rail sits behind an MOV that protects it; putting two MOVs in parallel, by contrast, serves no purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup>

Parallel paths can also be an unwanted drain. A diode and two capacitors found in parallel with a [[battery]] remain connected even with the power switch off, providing a standing path for current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup> Deliberate parallel battery banks solve a different problem: two columns of series AAA cells wired in parallel let one column be replaced at a time without losing memory contents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup>

The arrangement scales to mains wiring, where the circuits of a house hang in parallel off separate breakers from a common main — power point circuits at perhaps 20 A, lighting at 8 A, with further circuits for oven, stove or hot water, and grid-tied solar arrays wired into the same switchboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> It also appears structurally: the rows of electrodes in an electrostatic whiteboard are all connected in parallel, forming what is effectively a single two-plate capacitor separated by a gap of a few millimetres.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1149" title="EEVblog #1149 - How An Electrostatic Whiteboard Works">1149</a></sup>

Precision resistance networks lean on parallel combinations to reach values and tolerances that single parts cannot. A resistance calibrator uses four paralleled 40 Ω resistors of 0.05% each to make a 10 Ω standard, that 10 Ω in series with another, and the result in series with a further paralleled arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup> A snap-off thick-film trimmer works the other way: ten elements of differing lengths, and therefore differing values, are laid out in parallel on a ceramic substrate, and breaking off one part at a time raises the total resistance by 20% per snap, reaching a five-fold increase once nine are gone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1391" title="EEVblog 1391 - Mailbag">1391</a></sup> Lumped-element microwave assemblies use the same freedom, with paralleled 10.8 µH inductors and paralleled spiral-cut resistors on some sections and single components on others, the configuration tweaked section by section to match a simulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-756" title="EEVblog #756 - Mailbag">756</a></sup>

## Energy accounting

Parallel connection does not create energy. A claimed free-energy demonstration powering 23 LEDs in parallel for 52 hours from a single AA cell was not fraudulent in the sense that the circuit failed to work — the fault lay in the explanation, which skipped the basic engineering step of accounting for where the energy actually came from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup>
