Reverse engineering is the recovery of a design — a [[schematic]], a pinout, a bus protocol, a [[firmware]] image — from a finished product, using only the physical article and whatever instruments are on the bench. It exists because the information usually is not published: manufacturers withhold schematics even from their own dealers,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> service manuals for a given model often simply cannot be found,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> and undocumented salvage hardware carries no documentation at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-641" title="EEVblog #641 - Dumpster Cash Register Teardown">641</a></sup> The practical payoff is repair without a schematic,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> reuse of a working subassembly instead of redesigning it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1489" title="EEVblog 1489 - Mystery Teardown!">1489</a></sup> and the ability to say what a design actually does rather than what its marketing claims.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>

## Scope: partial by design

A complete, verified schematic is almost never the goal. The normal working result is a partial trace-out of the one subsection that answers the question — an [[oscilloscope]]'s analog front end rather than its whole board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> a [[multimeter]]'s input path rather than its full measurement chain,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> a few subsections of an [[inverter]] rather than its entire topology.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> Such results are stated with their accuracy caveat attached: not 100 percent, but "good enough for Australia" for the purpose at hand,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> close enough for a specific experiment,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-530" title="EEVblog #530 - Electrophoresis Power Supply Teardown">530</a></sup> enough to find out the one thing worth finding out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup>

The decision to stop is itself an engineering judgement. Work is abandoned when the signal paths run somewhere convoluted and the effort would balloon out of proportion to the question,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup> when getting at the circuit would require ripping the whole guts out of the unit,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup> or when the answer simply is not needed to proceed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup>

## Method

For a simple single- or double-sided board the traces can be followed by eye and held in memory, checking off what has been done.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> For anything more complex, the standard aid is a photocopy of both sides, with the trace-out done on paper so that completed nets can be marked off as they are resolved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> A refinement is a printed transparent overlay registered against the board, or a fade between top-side and bottom-side images, with a handful of measurements taken to confirm the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> Under any method, high-resolution photographs of both sides are sufficient raw material for a competent third party to reconstruct a front end without touching the hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> A light box is standard bench equipment for the inspection work this requires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Lrhee7hz53U" title="Dumpster Light Box">•</a></sup>

Obstacles are physical. White and black solder masks make traces genuinely hard to see and slow the work down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup> Multilayer construction hides runs entirely, and where an X-ray machine is unavailable the buried connections must be inferred.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Traces that pass under components force desoldering of those parts before the net can be resolved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup> Vias that drop under a switch body end the visual trail outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup>

Where the unit still works, probing beats tracing. An operational device can be powered up and its interface probed live to capture the data format directly, which is far quicker than deriving it from copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-488" title="EEVblog #488 - Dumpster Dive Parts Salvage">488</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-641" title="EEVblog #641 - Dumpster Cash Register Teardown">641</a></sup> The same approach recovers bus traffic on a proprietary serial link.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-297" title="EEVblog #297 - Canon LANC Bus Reverse Engineering">297</a></sup> It can be pushed further by cutting away the parts that are in the way — tearing out a [[transformer]], wiring in bench power, and taking the output of the final stage amplifier directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>

## Cost and error

The effort is routinely underestimated. A single scope front end represents many hours rather than an hour or two, consumed by red herrings and dead-end traps, and the work itself is tedious.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Getting a mains [[power-supply]] schematic out of a board is not conceptually hard but is very time-consuming to do correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup>

Errors in the result are close to certain. The honest position is that a recovered schematic almost certainly contains a mistake or two; being sure would require checking it, having someone else check it independently, and then simulating it to confirm the configuration works and nothing was left out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> The characteristic failure is a single misread connection propagating into a wrong conclusion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> Familiarity with standard circuit building blocks is the main defence: when a recovered fragment does not make sense as a known topology, the goof is usually obvious, and without that knowledge it is much harder to spot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup>

Skipping the trace-out has a measurable cost. Identifying a blown [[resistor]] by colour band alone, without first establishing that it sat in a Zener dropper, produced a value wrong by at least an order of magnitude — basic reverse engineering would have placed the correct value in the tens of kilohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Similarly, a discharge path that was missed on the first pass through a charger board was found immediately on a second, more careful look.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

## Representative results

The Rigol DS1054Z front end was traced to establish how [[bandwidth]] limiting was implemented; the same work showed the channel has no variable-gain amplifier, with gain control instead performed digitally inside the ADC chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1228" title="EEVblog #1228 - Do Digital Scopes Have REAL Verniers?">1228</a></sup> The Brymen BM2257 low-[[impedance]] mode was resolved to a 2.3 kΩ path, in contrast to the roughly 10 MΩ presented at the IC in the earlier BM235.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> A metal detector's drive circuit came down to a 10 nF [[capacitor]] directly across the coil, forming the LC tank needed to resonate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> A fast NiMH charger proved to use two N-channel MOSFETs across each AA holder, fed from a DC-DC constant-[[current]] source into the centre tap — a minimalistic and clever arrangement for per-cell charge and discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> An aircon control panel was found to encode its keypad as a resistor-divider read by the [[microcontroller]]'s internal ADC, rather than the scanned matrix expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> An EPIRB's test-mode detection reduced to two transistors and an [[ac-coupling]] capacitor off the [[antenna]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>

Firmware is reverse engineered by the same logic at a different layer: a multimeter's binary was decompiled, understood, and modified to fix slow auto-ranging;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3MD88EYLdQQ" title="121GW Hacked firmware test">•</a></sup> an ARM board's firmware was rewritten from scratch after the original was analysed;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup> and an [[asic]] miner chip with no public [[datasheet]] was worked out well enough for an independent open-source operating system to be written for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0h7lKgIFFXA" title="BitAxe Ultra $80 Bitcoin ASIC Miner REVIEW">•</a></sup> Whole-product analyses of this kind run to hundreds of pages of detail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1581" title="EEVblog 1581 - Tesla Roadster now OPEN SOURCE?">1581</a></sup> Salvaged display modules are a standing target, since a bare vacuum fluorescent panel without its controller requires the high-[[voltage]] drivers, the multiplex, and the AC filament supply to be worked out before anything can be shown at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup>

## Countermeasures and their limits

Manufacturers obstruct the work by laser-marking part numbers off chips, rebranding standard parts under their own logo, potting assemblies, and designing enclosures to be effectively unopenable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-893" title="EEVblog #893 - Mailbag">893</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup> None of it holds long. A lasered-off part can usually be narrowed down in a few hours from its expected function in the block diagram, its package type, and its pin count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup> Sanding the markings off a wideband RF detector front end leaves a circuit that is probably not hard to recover anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-956" title="EEVblog #956 - Countersurveillance Monitor Teardown">956</a></sup> A design that does not obfuscate its parts at all is simply traced from photographs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup>

The measures that do work operate on cost rather than possibility. Firmware locked behind blown security fuses can still be extracted, but the difficulty is high enough that a copyist may as well take the idea and re-engineer it from scratch — which is the practical argument for firmware protection over patents as a defence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-106" title="EEVblog #106 - Top 5 Tips To Bring Your Product to Market (and why Patents are a BAD idea)">106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Dedicated cryptographic chips carry hardware protections built into the die specifically against penetration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup> Attacking those requires purpose-built equipment for voltage-spiking parts and probing security fuses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-720" title="EEVblog #720 - Mailbag">720</a></sup>

Secrecy in a communications protocol is a weaker defence still. Where a product relies on an undisclosed protocol for security, a hostile party will reverse engineer it regardless, so withholding the specification from legitimate users buys nothing — the same lesson the lock industry learned, that mechanisms become more secure once their vulnerabilities are openly discussed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1407" title="EEVblog 1407 - Right to Repair with iFixit Founder Kyle Wiens">1407</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k4zrmTsZj_s" title="Australia Right to Repair Summit - Session 1 of 4">•</a></sup>
