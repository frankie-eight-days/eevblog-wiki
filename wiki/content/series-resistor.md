A series resistor is a [[resistor]] placed directly in a [[current]] path so that the full circuit current flows through it, where it drops [[voltage]], limits current, or shapes the behaviour of whatever lies downstream. It is one of the most heavily used roles a resistor can take: protecting inputs, biasing LEDs, modelling the internal [[resistance]] of real sources, and extending the voltage rating of a circuit by stacking several resistors in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1397" title="EEVblog 1397 - DC Fundamentals Part 2: DC Voltage &amp; Current Sources (Thevenin &amp; Norton Theorems)">1397</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>

## Series combination: value, tolerance, and power

Resistors in series add directly: the total is R1 + R2 + R3, however many are in the chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> A common beginner mistake is assuming the tolerances add as well — that four ±1% resistors in series yield a ±4% part. They do not; the combination retains the same percentage tolerance as the individual parts, so ten 1% resistors in series still give a 1% result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> Parallel combinations behave differently: with a true Gaussian distribution of values around the nominal mean, paralleling (for example, ten 10k resistors to make 1k) can produce a tolerance better than that of the individual parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup>

Power handling also improves in series. In a decade resistance box built from 0.5W resistors, dialling in more sections raises the total dissipation capability to a maximum of 2.5W because the heat is spread across the series chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup>

For hand-tweaking values, series combinations are arguably safer than parallel ones: a poorly contacting resistor in a parallel pair can silently leave the lower value slightly out, whereas a failed resistor in a series chain produces a gross, obvious failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

## The series resistor inside every real source

A real [[voltage-source]] cannot be modelled as an ideal voltage source alone; in practice it must include a series resistor, which is the essence of the Thévenin equivalent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1397" title="EEVblog 1397 - DC Fundamentals Part 2: DC Voltage &amp; Current Sources (Thevenin &amp; Norton Theorems)">1397</a></sup> This shows up concretely on the bench:

- An arbitrary [[waveform]] generator used as a high-resolution DC source has a fixed 50Ω output [[impedance]] — equivalent to a precision adjustable supply with a 50Ω resistor in series — and this is unaffected by the high-impedance load setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup>
- [[battery]] ESR can be simulated with a series resistor: a vintage portable computer booted fine at 1Ω and 5Ω of simulated ESR but refused to boot at 10Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup>
- A [[microcontroller]] parasitically powered through a reset pin tolerates series resistance up to a point — still running with several kΩ dimming its LEDs, but dead by 10kΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

Series resistance also appears inside measurement setups, where it creates burden: a picoammeter front end with a 10k series resistor into its feedback amplifier exhibits an offset (burden) voltage of around 100µV that must be accounted for in the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup>

## Current limiting and LED droppers

The classic series-resistor job is setting [[led]] current. When LEDs are paralleled, they do not share current evenly unless matched at the semiconductor level, so a series ballast resistor per string is the general rule; even a single dropper resistor in series with a whole multi-segment display is better than none, though the current is then shared between however many segments are lit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> A worked dropper example: a 7.2V battery driving a 5.6V LED string at 250mA leaves 1.6V across the resistor, giving 6.4Ω by Ohm's law — 6.8Ω as the nearest E12 value — and I²R gives 0.25W dissipation, so a quarter-watt part suffices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> LED strips use the same scheme, e.g. a 360Ω drop resistor in series with each six-element section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> A series dropper also enables a bidirectional LED indicator across a logic [[inverter]]: the LED pair wired between input and output [[via]] one series resistor lights one way for a high input and the other way for a low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup>

A series resistor is equally effective for simply throttling a load: inserting 100Ω in series with a [[cooling-fan]] both quieted the acoustic whine and dropped the operating voltage substantially.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zoyaHOqp9gI" title="Rigol DHO800 Oscilloscope Fan Upgrade Experiment">•</a></sup>

## Protection and clamping

Series resistance is the standard first line of [[input-protection]] because it limits the current that can flow into a device under fault conditions. A logic analyser front end routes each input through a 510Ω series resistor and then through low-[[capacitance]] [[diode]] clamps to the supply rails; the resistor limits current so the clamps can absorb overloads without damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> The same principle protects a programmer/debugger when a line is pulled low externally — the series resistor ensures the tool cannot be harmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-liWWY5cSs4Q" title="Microchip - Atmel Collaboration">•</a></sup> At the design stage, a series resistor in a supply line to a switch can prevent a shorted external case from destroying the circuit, though it costs an extra part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GoKbPDADG0c" title="The Incredible Switch Replacement Repair">•</a></sup>

Zener-based protection is impossible without a series resistor: the resistor must limit the current into the Zener or the Zener itself blows on overvoltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup> The Zener additionally has its own internal dynamic resistance that varies and must be taken into account alongside the external series resistor in a practical circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> The [[transistor]]–Zener clamp (a BJT whose base-emitter junction acts as a roughly 6V Zener in reverse and a 0.6V diode in forward) clamps at about ±6.6V, and only works as a complete protector when a series resistor sits on the input to take up the clamped energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1000" title="EEVblog #1000 - Fundamental Mailbag Retro Teardown Shootouts are Bullshit">1000</a></sup>

A small series resistor also appears at op-amp outputs for stability and short-circuit protection. A 100Ω series output resistor keeps an op-amp stable into capacitive loads and protects it if the output is shorted; the error it introduces against a 10MΩ [[multimeter]] input is negligible, and only becomes a ~0.1% problem once the driven instrument's [[input-impedance]] falls to about 100kΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> The caveat is that such a resistor does not truly isolate a capacitive load — it shifts the pole rather than eliminating the stability problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup>

In switch debouncing, the classic RC circuit can generate a large discharge current spike when the switch shorts the [[capacitor]] directly; adding a series resistor between switch and capacitor forces the discharge through that resistor instead, typically with a value smaller than the pull-up so the cap still discharges quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-961" title="EEVblog #961 - Monkey Debouncing">961</a></sup> Series resistors can be omitted entirely where the current is already constrained — for instance inside ICs, where internal constant-current generators limit transistor base current and no series resistor is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## Stacking resistors for high voltage

A single surface-mount resistor has a limited voltage rating, so high-voltage circuits routinely place several resistors in series purely to divide the voltage stress across the chain:

- A grid-tied solar hybrid inverter uses four series surface-mount resistors — each good for a couple of hundred volts — where one through-hole part could have done the job, for grid-voltage sensing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>
- Multimeter high-voltage input strings use two or three series resistors (e.g. three 3MΩ parts) together with PTCs to stand off the input voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup>
- An [[oscilloscope]] front end with four large resistors in series indicates a high-voltage path; that is the only reason for the arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup>
- An EVSE charger uses a bunch of series resistors to achieve a high-voltage-rated drop for its auxiliary supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup>
- A mains appliance test circuit places three 1206 resistors in series across active and neutral with a [[relay]] coil, the series combination meeting the voltage requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup>
- A 1MΩ series resistor suffices for detecting the presence of mains AC on an alarm panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup>

Analog multimeters illustrate the flip side: the meter is essentially just series resistors plus the movement, so on the lowest ranges (e.g. 30µA) the movement is at the mercy of the input, saved only by back-to-back germanium diodes clamping the voltage across it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup>

## Instrumentation and measurement applications

Decade resistance substitution boxes are built entirely from series chains: each decade has a string of equal-value resistors tapped by a multi-way switch, the decades themselves wired in series, so a six-decade box spanning 10Ω to 1MΩ can dial any value in 10Ω steps up to 10.1MΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup> Lower-cost designs use a 1-2-3-4 arrangement where successive switch positions route through one, two, three, or four series resistors, with a further position shorting one out to reach five.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1586" title="EEVblog 1586 -  Stranger Things Decade Resistor Box + Repair">1586</a></sup> Deliberately desoldering a series resistor on a board and fitting a two-pin header is a quick way to break into a supply rail for current-consumption measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup>

Elsewhere in instruments:

- An [[lcr-meter]] drives the device under test from a [[function-generator]] through an output series resistor, tapping the resulting voltage for amplification and A/D conversion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup>
- Current shunts in a multimeter can be cascaded in series so all ranges are read from a single tap at the top of the stack, eliminating multiplexer taps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>
- A multimeter's low-impedance (LoZ) mode inserts a 1k series resistor plus a PTC in series, which will safely discharge a charged capacitor through the meter itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup>
- A diode-test function with only a couple of milliamps of drive — limited by roughly 2.2k of internal series resistance — may fail to light an LED at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup>
- A transmission-line (Z0) passive [[oscilloscope-probe]] is nothing more than [[coaxial-cable|coax]] with a 1k series resistor at the tip; the resistor isolates the cable and scope capacitance, which at 1GHz would otherwise present a reactance of only about 1.6Ω for 100pF. The 1k value is the conventional compromise — not so high as to limit [[bandwidth]], not so low as to load the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>
- Series termination resistors on digital lines should be placed physically close to the driving device they belong with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup>

## Signal-line and bus roles

On digital buses the series resistor defines whether a line can be driven at all: a CAN bus terminated in 120Ω cannot be driven through two 10k series resistors, which simply lack the drive capability to assert the bus.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1181" title="EEVblog #1181 - Car ECO OBD2 OptiFuel Fuel Saver SCAM!">1181</a></sup> Series resistors on I²S clock and data lines provide convenient probing points — the signals can be identified by touching one side of each resistor without knowing the pinout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Capacitive touch sensing relies on a series resistor feeding the touch pad: finger capacitance to ground forms an RC time constant with the resistor, so a 16-key touch interface needs 16 series resistors, which can be embedded as carbon resistors directly on the flat-flex to save board space.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1166" title="EEVblog #1166 - Amazing 1980&#x27;s Touch Screen Calculator Watch!">1166</a></sup> A series resistor from the wiper of a trimmer can also be used to narrow the effective adjustment range of a [[calibration]] control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup>
