A bill of materials, universally abbreviated BOM, is the itemised list of every component that goes into a product: quantities, reference designators, descriptions, footprints, values, manufacturer part numbers and supplier part numbers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> It is the document that connects a design to the physical world, because it is what the purchasing department buys from and what the assembly house builds from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-676" title="EEVblog #676 - RANT: FTDI Bricking Counterfeit Chips!">676</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Producing and optimising it consumes far more of a design engineer's time than drawing the circuit or laying out the board — plausibly 80 to 90 per cent of it, spent on parametric searches, sourcing and cost optimisation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## What a BOM contains

Most CAD packages will generate one automatically as a CSV or spreadsheet, listing quantity, reference designator, description, value, footprint, and where the library has been populated properly, manufacturer and supplier part numbers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup> For a high-volume product the useful form is a spreadsheet that carries the manufacturer's part number, a supplier part number, and at least one alternate supplier part number — a Digi-Key number, a Mouser number, an Element 14 number — plus a column for reel quantity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Serious manufacturing BOMs go further and list a main supplier plus a set of alternative approved suppliers, so purchasing may buy from any approved source without re-engaging engineering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup>

Specifying a part loosely is a real failure mode. A 10 µF [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] whose BOM entry does not pin a specific manufacturer, part number and model can vary between production batches, and a substituted ceramic can introduce audible piezoelectric behaviour that the original did not have.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> The same laxity at the purchasing end produced the well-known bad-[[capacitor]] monitor failures, where dodgy caps were bought in place of the Panasonic parts the design engineer had specified in the BOM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup>

Dave Jones's own practice on the µCurrent was to give the assembler a BOM with an item number against every line, and to number the physical reels to match, so reel five and item five cannot be confused; the entry also carries the description and the component designator it populates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup>

## BOM consolidation

Reducing the number of *different* BOM items — not the number of parts — is the central optimisation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Every distinct line item is another reel to buy, another slot occupied in the pick-and-place machine, another feeder change in production, another opportunity to run out of stock or order the wrong thing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup> The more distinct part types on a board, the higher the risk, the greater the assembly cost, and the greater the chance of getting everything manufactured except for one part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The effect scales: the bigger and more complex the board, the more this matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

The consequence is a counterintuitive rule: it is often cheaper and better to use *more* components than to use the theoretically correct one. If a divider calculation produces an oddball 4.99 kΩ, two 10 kΩ resistors in parallel from a reel already loaded beats buying another reel for a single value, at the cost of some board area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Design practice can bias toward this from the start — fix the bottom [[resistor]] of a divider at 10 kΩ and calculate the top one, so only one oddball value is needed instead of two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The other valid workflow is to complete the design, generate the BOM, and then go back through it looking for consolidations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

Reuse shows up everywhere once the habit is formed:

- Three 330 µF capacitors already in the design instead of a new 1000 µF line item, with the added benefit that they all come off one reel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>
- A second instance of an already-specified beefy [[mosfet|MOSFET]], mounted in reverse and gate-tied, used purely for its body [[diode]] to make a back-to-back protection pair — one less reel on the pick-and-place machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>
- A 1N4007 across a [[battery]] rather than a lower-[[voltage]] 1N4001, because the 4007 is already stocked for protection elsewhere and the parts are cheap enough that a separate BOM item is not worth having.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup>
- Diodes and capacitors already in the BOM used to build a voltage doubler or [[inverter]], instead of a dedicated charge-pump chip costing around a dollar.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup>
- [[transistor|Transistor]] zener clamps reused across several places in a design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup>
- The same [[processor]] carried onto a secondary interface card even though it is overkill there, because the part and its development tools are already committed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup>
- Ballast resistors for [[current]] sharing implemented as [[pcb|PCB]] traces, adding nothing to the BOM at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

Jellybean components exist to serve this discipline. Parts like the [[lm317|LM317]], the LM311 [[comparator]] and the common 7400/4000 logic should sit permanently in the CAD library with all their footprints and multiple manufacturers already attached as BOM and supplier entries, so they can be dropped in without further sourcing work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> The habit extends to their support components: a 240 Ω resistor is the nominal fixed leg of an LM317 adjustable divider, so if LM317s are in use the 240 Ω should be loaded on the pick-and-place ready to go, and reused elsewhere — a 220 Ω requirement somewhere else in the circuit can often be met by the 240 Ω already present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Consolidation also cuts against itself sometimes. An adjustable regulator is one BOM item but needs three placed parts once its setting resistors are counted, where a fixed regulator is one; a dedicated [[crystal-oscillator|crystal oscillator]] module is one BOM item instead of a crystal plus load capacitors, usually more expensive but smaller and pre-characterised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup> And commonality should not be pursued past the point where it costs money: expensive banana binding posts justified on a precision input are not justified on an output that permanently connects to a [[multimeter]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

Mechanical parts count too. Using one type of screw throughout an enclosure and its internal modules reduces the BOM, removes a way to get assembly wrong, and means no tool changes during assembly, disassembly or repair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup> Conversely a [[light-pipe|light pipe]] added to bend an [[led|LED]]'s output to the front panel, a bus bar substituted for heavy copper, a daughterboard that requires an extra cable and connectors, a single tantalum on a board otherwise free of them, and an extra capacitor for a level-shifting scheme are each a new BOM line with engineering, sourcing and assembly cost attached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3_CQKSC1JNE" title="Davinci Resolve Speed Editor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1229" title="EEVblog #1229 - Mailbag">1229</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

## Sourcing and manufacture

Design for manufacture starts by walking the BOM component by component and asking, of each part, whether the chosen assembly house can actually place it — assembly houses differ in their pick-and-place capability and requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> For a 500-part board that is 500 separate judgements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> The next pass is a purchasing pass: every line item is priced on reels or tubes at the intended build quantity, with reels preferred, and excess purchased over what the build needs must be absorbed and recovered through sales.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> A quantity of 1,100 components at 35 cents each to build 1,000 boards is a typical shape for that arithmetic, allowance for machine wastage included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Alternatively the whole package — board, Gerbers, BOM, components — can be handed to a turnkey assembly house that handles panelisation, fiducials, tooling and procurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup>

Specifying a part does not secure it. Once a part is in the BOM the design engineer's control ends and supply chain management takes over, and parts routinely cannot be obtained from the nominal source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-676" title="EEVblog #676 - RANT: FTDI Bricking Counterfeit Chips!">676</a></sup> This is why alternates matter, and why equivalents must be qualified rather than assumed. Two MOSFETs sharing a number in their part codes are not interchangeable: substituting on top-level specifications alone can ship 100,000 units that suffer field failures because the replacement lacks the ESD structure of the original.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> For static switching, matching R<sub>DS(on)</sub> — 21 mΩ in one such comparison — may be sufficient; for switching-mode power conversion, equivalence requires extensive dynamic testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Alternates are also insurance against geopolitical supply disruption for a single-source cheap part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Package variants are a separate trap: ordering the wrong LQFP [[pin-pitch|pin pitch]] from a supplier offering a good deal on 10,000 units leaves stock that cannot be assembled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup>

Manufacturers can also break equivalence silently. A part sold under an identical number can be changed such that a previously connected pin is no longer connected, which purchasing would have no way of detecting; where a manufacturer does assign a new suffix and document legacy-versus-new differences, a properly detailed BOM lets a purchasing officer flag the substitution and refer it back to engineering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup> Mixed-brand jellybean parts appearing on one production board are normal and expected under this system — the design should not care which approved manufacturer's LM358 arrives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Purchasing discretion also cuts quality. A [[power-supply|power supply]] subcontracted to a specialist who specifies Nippon Chemi-Con capacitors can arrive with Jamicon parts fitted, because the purchasing department found those cheap that week.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> Tighter control over the BOM and [[component-sourcing|component sourcing]] is part of what separates a premium instrument manufacturer from one building to a lower price point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup>

Some organisations formalise this to the limit. Large vertically integrated manufacturers maintained internal part catalogues where designers could only select from a pre-authorised list, each item carrying an internal part number mapped by separate documents to the actual generic device — an ordinary 74LS245 bus transceiver, for example, appearing on the board only under a house number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-960" title="EEVblog #960 - Mystery Merry Mailbag Teardown">960</a></sup>

## BOM cost

BOM cost is the primary lever on product cost, and at volume the increments that matter are small. The difference between a 6-cent regulator and a 1-cent one is negligible per unit but becomes real across multiple products at thousands of units each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Chinese distributors such as LCSC serve this end of the market with domestic brands not carried by the Western catalogue houses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup> At the other extreme, specifying a bare die version of a part that costs 5 cents packaged can put a $704 line item in the BOM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup>

Features are traded directly against BOM cost. Most multimeters requiring [[true-rms|true RMS]] have to spend on a dedicated converter such as the AD737; a manufacturer with high enough margin may fit the chip regardless and run it in average mode for build consistency, while a cheaper meter simply omits the converter and saves that money.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup> Instrument front-end topology is re-optimised between models to hit price targets, because a handful of premium analog parts in the BOM makes an aggressive price point unreachable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> A [[microcontroller]] with an integrated [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]] removes both a separate driver chip and the PCB area it needs, which was the deciding factor in one meter's processor choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> A single-chip microcontroller containing [[ram|RAM]], [[rom|ROM]] and I/O made early electronic games possible at a $25 retail price, because everything else was LEDs and moulding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> Choosing a two-cell-maximum battery management part saved a couple of dollars of BOM cost at the price of reducing the achievable output range of the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

Cost-reduction can justify respinning a board several times before production, since a chip a fraction of a cent cheaper multiplied across millions of units pays for the respins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-219" title="Amazon Kindle Fire Tablet Teardown - EEVblog #219">219</a></sup> Ultra-low-cost products are built to the same logic from the start — a single-chip camcorder with external memory and a couple of passives, a promotional power bank with two pouch cells in parallel and a handful of LEDs, a $5 phone reduced to a main [[asic|ASIC]], memory and an RF chipset on one board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-595" title="EEVblog #595 - World&#x27;s Worst Shittiest Camcorder: Teardown">595</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aaxHhCM0P6c" title="Dumpster Battery Bank">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-514" title="EEVblog #514 - $5 Mobile Phone Teardown - LG800G">514</a></sup> Estimating a competitor's or a product's BOM from a teardown is a standard analysis: a $9 process meter's semiconductors totalling perhaps $30 before case and [[lcd|LCD]], a smartwatch costed at quantity 1,000, a $5,800 [[arbitrary-waveform-generator|arbitrary waveform generator]] whose BOM includes power supply, LCD, membrane keypad, mouldings and metalwork, and a tablet whose estimated BOM roughly equalled its $199 retail price — though such estimates tend to understate the buying power of very large purchasers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-220" title="Amazon Kindle Fire Tablet Review &amp; Unboxing - EEVblog #220">220</a></sup>

Cost pressure can also force redesign. Ian Johnston's PDVS2 became too expensive as component prices rose and was redesigned from scratch into the PDVS2 Mini, dropping the [[rotary-encoder|rotary encoder]], moving to a smaller LCD and a custom CNC aluminium housing, which took a substantial amount off the BOM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a4Xpsenpd6E" title="The Amp Hour #643 - Calibration &amp; Repair with Ian Johnston">•</a></sup>

Not every cost decision is legible from outside. Populating termination resistors on a board whose corresponding feature is disabled costs little in parts but consumes production time and feeder capacity, and is hard to justify on BOM-reduction grounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup>

## Documentation and open hardware

A BOM is a required part of any complete hardware release. Publishing schematics and Gerbers alone is not sufficient: the original [[schematic]] and PCB source files, [[firmware]], and the BOM must all be released for hardware to be meaningfully open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup> This is codified in the SPB labelling scheme and in the gear-logo scheme where separate letters mark the release of schematic, PCB, BOM, documentation, and a commercially permissive licence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup> Releases that omit the BOM are incomplete in practice as well as in principle — a hardware wallet project supplying only Eagle schematic and board files leaves a reimplementer unable to buy parts, since generic Rs and Cs are trivial but the specific LCD and similar parts are not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup> A ventilator release consisting of PDF schematics and a [[service-manual|service manual]] without a BOM is unusable for its stated purpose, because the motors and valves are the essence of the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1wrin8FYtEo" title="eevBLAB #72 - BUSTED! Medtronics Open Ventilator FAIL">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TltM74rNasM" title="eevBLAB #73 - Medtronics Open Ventilator + Others UPDATE">•</a></sup> The same question applies to any open-source announcement: whether it includes the complete BOM and supplier information, or only service manuals and miscellany.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1581" title="EEVblog 1581 - Tesla Roadster now OPEN SOURCE?">1581</a></sup>

The practical goal for a modern open design is a BOM that a distributor's upload tool can consume directly. Component suppliers provide BOM import tools that take a CSV of manufacturer part numbers and populate a cart, which removes the manual search step entirely and makes a project genuinely turnkey.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup> KiCad's interactive HTML BOM plugin, installed separately, provides a cross-referenced assembly view alongside the conventional CSV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Getting a project into that state is not trivial: importing a design from another EDA tool can require hours of work to clean up footprints, repair the schematic and rebuild the BOM with proper [[datasheet]] and supplier links.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup> Board release can be automated so that DRC, [[gerber|Gerber]] generation, NC drill files and BOM export happen in one step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup>

Vintage service manuals set the standard the field has largely abandoned. Manuals from the era routinely carried theory of operation, [[troubleshooting]] flowcharts, full schematics, PCB overlays and an exhaustive BOM — running to dozens of pages of part numbers, with individually numbered nuts, screws and every capacitor and resistor given a house part number complete with description, value, tolerance and voltage rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZBVyJaLAZDY" title="Scanning Vintage Computer Service Manuals - Tandy 1100FD Laptop Computer">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-904" title="EEVblog #904 - Hewlett Packard HP85 Professional Computer">904</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-272" title="EEVblog #272 - Manson 9400 40A 3-15V Switchmode PSU Teardown">272</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-756" title="EEVblog #756 - Mailbag">756</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1169" title="EEVblog #1169 - TI 1972 Computer Interfacing">1169</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1429" title="EEVblog 1429 - Sony RX100 Camera Troubleshooting Part 2">1429</a></sup> Scanned and OCR'd, such a BOM lets a repairer search a designator like R901 and jump straight to the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZBVyJaLAZDY" title="Scanning Vintage Computer Service Manuals - Tandy 1100FD Laptop Computer">•</a></sup> The absence of one is immediately felt: a service manual with full schematic and PCB overlay but no BOM reference will not say which op-amp populates the front end, forcing physical disassembly to find out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-529" title="EEVblog #529 - HP 35660A DSA Upgrade Investigation">529</a></sup>
