An HRC fuse — high rupture capacity [[fuse]] — is a fuse built to interrupt a very large fault [[current]] without the fuse body itself failing violently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Instead of a wire in a glass tube, it uses a ceramic body packed with sand or a similar filler that absorbs the arc energy and stops the arc continuing after the element has parted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> In test and measurement equipment it is the single clearest indicator of whether a design takes gross overload seriously: a [[multimeter]] intended for anything beyond low-[[voltage]] [[battery]]-powered circuits should have HRC fuses, and one without them is not safe for that work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup>

## Why glass fuses are not equivalent

A glass fuse is essentially a length of fuse wire in a glass envelope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Under a genuine mains fault the tube can blast open and the arc can continue across the gap, so current keeps flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The consequence is not a quietly blown fuse but an instrument that vents, arcs and can catch fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> The HRC construction is intended to contain that event entirely within the fuse body.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lgtooEtk9R4" title="EEVcomments #1">•</a></sup>

The threat model is specific. Connecting the amps jack of a meter across a mains supply puts the instrument across a source with enormous available energy — a 240 V outlet delivering a nominal 2,400 W is capable of far more than that instantaneously — and it is that instantaneous capability, not the steady-state rating, that the fuse has to survive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Meters built for that duty carry fuses rated accordingly; interrupt ratings of 30 kA appear in high-end industrial handhelds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup>

## Voltage rating

The rupture rating is only half of the specification, and the voltage rating is the half most often overlooked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> A fuse being an HRC type says nothing on its own: a 250 V rated HRC fuse is materially inferior to a 600 V one, and the better instruments use 1,000 V rated parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> Pocket meters have been found carrying 600 mA 250 V fuses, which is not the 600 V or 1,000 V part the enclosure's safety claims would imply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> The 1,000 V HRC fuses are the ones specified into high-quality multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup>

## Resistance and burden voltage

HRC fuses are not electrically transparent. They have appreciable [[resistance]] — often higher than the [[current-shunt|current shunt]] they sit in series with — and that resistance adds directly to the meter's [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> A typical 10 A or 11 A HRC fuse runs on the order of 10 mΩ cold, rising as it heats, which adds roughly another 50 mV on top of the 50 mV developed across a 10 mΩ shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

The effect is far more pronounced on low-current ranges. In the [[121gw|121GW]] the milliamp-jack fuse is about 2 Ω nominal against a shunt of about 1 Ω, so the fuse dominates burden voltage on the 50 mA range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> Routing the 500 mA range through the 11 A fuse instead of the milliamp fuse keeps burden voltage on that range very low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> Cheap substitutes behave differently for the wrong reason: a 500 mA fast-blow fuse rated at only 250 V measured about 0.3 Ω, roughly a third of the resistance of a properly rated part at a similar current rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup>

## Time-current behaviour

An 11 A fuse does not open at 11 A. It takes a considerable time to clear at its rating, which is why some multimeters specify a short-duration overload such as 20 A for around ten seconds without the fuse blowing or the shunt being damaged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> At a genuine fault current the clearing is fast — a 50 A fault through an 11 A fuse opens quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Time-current curves matter when substituting a non-recommended replacement, though in practice HRC fuses of comparable rating fall in much the same ballpark on the average time versus current curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup>

## Scope of protection in a multimeter

The fuses in a multimeter protect only the amps and milliamp ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> They have nothing to do with protecting the voltage input jacks, which rely on their own protection network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Removing the fuses leaves volts, ohms, [[capacitance]] and [[diode]] functions working normally; only [[current-measurement|current measurement]] stops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Fusing is therefore one element of a front end that also includes [[input-protection|input protection]] resistors and thermistors, MOVs, spark gaps, back-to-back protection diodes, high-voltage isolation slots and blast shields.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup>

## Sizes, fitment and access

HRC fuses appear in a full-size format and in smaller 3AG / M205-size ceramic bodies, and meters mix the two — a large HRC for the amps range and a smaller one for milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RDUn3CoVmuc" title="EEVblog Multimeter 3D Printed SLA case">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup> Fitting two full-size HRC fuses alongside four AA cells and an [[sd-card|SD card]] in a compact meter is a real packaging constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RDUn3CoVmuc" title="EEVblog Multimeter 3D Printed SLA case">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup>

Fuse access is a design quality issue in its own right, since fuses are consumables. Good practice puts them behind a dedicated compartment — a sealed rubber boot compressed by the back cover, then an inner cover that pops off to expose the fuses — or behind external screw-in holders, one per range, as on bench meters that provide them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> The opposite pattern is common and criticised: fuses reachable only by removing the entire case, sometimes six self-tapping screws with O-ring grommets, on meters where a blown fuse is an expected event.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-43" title="EEVblog #43 - Fluke 233 Multimeter Review">43</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1457" title="EEVblog1457 - Old School Mailbag - ESC Burnout">1457</a></sup>

## Cost and retrofitting

The reason low-cost meters ship with glass fuses is price. Glass fuses are produced for cents each, while HRC fuses run from tens of cents for small sizes to dollars each for large parts from established manufacturers such as Bussmann.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lgtooEtk9R4" title="EEVcomments #1">•</a></sup> Retrofitting a cheap meter with HRC fuses of the same rating works and does deliver higher rupture capacity, so a gross overload is contained rather than blowing the fuse apart.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lgtooEtk9R4" title="EEVcomments #1">•</a></sup> The caveat is that the rest of a cheap meter is unlikely to be designed or tested for gross overloads to the same standard, so the upgrade helps without turning the instrument into a properly rated one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lgtooEtk9R4" title="EEVcomments #1">•</a></sup>

## Beyond multimeters

HRC fuses are used wherever mains energy enters an instrument, not just at meter input jacks. They appear as dual mains input fuses on bench power supplies, [[oscilloscope]] mains input boards alongside MOVs and suppression capacitors, digital mixer power inlets, bench instrument supplies and even reflow oven controllers driving a solid-state [[relay]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> Television power boards use them in preference to glass parts as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup>

Their presence in a class of instrument that does not obviously need them is notable: an 11 A HRC fuse on a CAT II rated bench multimeter is unusual, since rack-mounted system instruments do not normally see the surge overload conditions a handheld meter encounters in the field.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup>

Their absence is equally diagnostic. Oscilloscopes with built-in multimeters have shipped with no fuse protection whatsoever, or with an automotive blade fuse in place of an HRC part, in instruments otherwise carefully isolated — a design that should not be used on mains or high-energy circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> Budget meters commonly pair an unfused 10 A input with a small glass fuse on the milliamp and microamp ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-880" title="EEVblog #880 - Mailbag">880</a></sup>

## Mechanical mounting

Because the fuses are physically large and heavy relative to the rest of a handheld meter, their retention matters under mechanical abuse. In drop testing a handheld industrial meter, one of the HRC fuses came completely out of its holder while the battery cells deformed against their supports and the back plate began to crack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-868" title="EEVblog #868 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Torture Test">868</a></sup>
