Pulse width modulation is a fixed-[[frequency]] digital [[waveform]] whose on-time, or duty cycle, is varied from 0 to 100% to carry an analog value on a single switching output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Because the waveform is just a digital pin toggling, it can be produced by any [[microcontroller]] IO pin in software or, better, by a dedicated hardware block, which makes it effectively free compared with the alternatives of a precision [[potentiometer]] or a digital-to-analog converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Filtered into a DC level, it substitutes for a DAC; left unfiltered, it directly meters energy into LEDs, heaters, motors and switching regulators, and in some systems it is used purely as a signalling protocol.

## Duty cycle and averaging

The period is fixed — 10 kHz is a typical microcontroller PWM frequency — and only the fraction of that period spent high changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> The average value of the waveform follows the duty cycle directly: a 0 to 1 V waveform that is high 10% of the time and low the other 90% averages to one tenth of its peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> In simulation, a 1 V pulse source with a 100 µs period and a 10 µs on-time produces exactly 100 mV after an RC filter, and an op-amp buffering that node settles to the same value after a short delay.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Pushing the on-time down to 0.1 µs out of 100 µs — one part in a thousand of a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] — yields 5 mV out, which a precision op-amp with only a couple of hundred microvolts of offset reproduces faithfully.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

The same arithmetic governs power rather than [[voltage]] when the load is not filtered. Chopping a resistive heating element off for half the time delivers half the energy, and no amount of pulsing changes that; the element's [[resistance]] is chosen for full-power operation and the laws of physics are not negotiable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup>

## Replacing a DAC

Few microcontrollers have a DAC built in, and an external one costs a couple of dollars against the ten dollars or so of a good 10-turn potentiometer, so PWM plus a [[resistor]] and [[capacitor]] is the cheap third option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A PWM of a given bit depth gives the same voltage resolution as a DAC of the same bit depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> An 8-bit module divides the range into 256 steps of 0.39% each, which on a 5 V reference is 19.5 mV per step — acceptable for a general-purpose bench supply, less so once the output range is doubled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A design wanting more than the 10-bit resolution typical of microcontroller peripherals may be better served by an external 12-bit DAC and ADC instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Absolute accuracy is set by whatever the PWM output swings between, so the microcontroller rail becomes the reference: a part powered from a 7805 inherits that regulator's 5% tolerance, and the modulator output is 5% accurate in absolute terms — not good enough for a precision bench supply without software [[calibration]] or a trimmed gain stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Modern FET output stages swing to within a millivolt or so of the rails, so the rail itself, not the driver, is the limiting term.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Any noise present on the PWM waveform that the filter fails to remove passes through the buffer and appears on the regulated output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

The filtered node must be buffered before it drives anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> An op-amp voltage follower is the standard answer, since no [[current]] flows into its inputs and it therefore does not disturb the filter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The high [[input-impedance]] of that op-amp is also why an RC filter works here at all: a plain RC, even a two-stage one, is hopeless at anything but very low currents, but when the destination draws essentially no current, values of a few to ten microfarads with hundreds of ohms or a kilohm of series resistance will knock a PWM waveform down to negligible ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The same trick lets a function-generator-grade voltage be produced without a DAC at all, fed to a voltage follower and on to a load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup>

## Filtering, ripple and response time

Filter design is a direct trade of ripple against settling time. A single RC with a 3 dB point at 159 Hz, calculated from 1/2πRC, attenuates a 10 kHz PWM poorly and leaves around 10 mV of ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Increasing the [[capacitance]] tenfold moves the corner to 15.9 Hz and, because the response is first order, drops the ripple by the same order of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Adding a second RC stage of 10k and 100n is more efficient than simply enlarging the first: it brings ripple down into the hundreds of microvolts, around 0.1 to 0.2 mV, while keeping the response time near 10 ms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Filter amplitude figures quoted for a [[sine-wave]] do not transfer directly to a PWM waveform, whose harmonic content makes the analysis more involved; in practice the residual is lower than the sine-wave estimate suggests.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Where the PWM swings 0 to 5 V but only 0 to 1 V of control range is wanted, a [[series-resistor]] into the filter attenuates it — adding 10k halves an expected 500 mV to 250 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Because such a control loop is only ever slewed by a human turning a knob at a few hertz, the slow filter costs nothing in usability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

## Hardware modules and pin allocation

Dedicated PWM hardware inside a microcontroller generates the waveform independently of the running code, and is strongly preferred over bit-banging it in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> On AVR parts the PWM function is built on the output compare module, so only the handful of pins carrying OC functions can be used — typically five or six on a small part — and the LEDs or loads must be routed to those pins at [[schematic]] time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> Peripheral pin sharing is a recurring trap in microcontroller selection: [[lcd]] segment drivers, ADC channels, I²C and PWM outputs frequently contend for the same physical pins, and a part can be ruled out because the ADC channels needed are mapped onto the LCD pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup>

Moving IO onto an I²C port expander frees microcontroller pins but forfeits the fast peripheral functions: PWM cannot usefully be generated through an I²C bus, because the expander has no internal hardware to do it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> That trade is sometimes worth taking — RGB LCD backlight brightness is a low-value capability to lose in exchange for cheaper pin count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

Available PWM channels are therefore a real selection criterion, not a footnote. A design needing two PWM channels, a couple of 10-bit ADC channels and 24 IO lines can eliminate candidate parts purely on PWM count, as with an ATtiny offering only one channel where the rest of the family offers at least two; an ATmega8A with three channels in a TQFP32 satisfies it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Some parts advertise three high-frequency PWM outputs alongside differential ADC pairs, which suits high-side [[current-sensing]] in the same design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Manufacturer [[parametric-search]] is more useful than distributor search for this, since the vendor's own site exposes the peripheral counts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Even microcontrollers costing a few cents carry the function: one such part offers three hardware 11-bit PWM generators with a 12-bit ADC, and a five-pin SOT23 variant with a [[comparator]] and internal reference does PWM without an ADC at all — cheap enough to drop into a product purely to generate an 8 kHz waveform so the main processor does not have to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup> Historically, missing a PWM peripheral was enough to lose a design socket outright to a competitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup>

## LED dimming and flicker

PWM is the standard method for setting [[led]] brightness, from individually addressable strips and DMX fixtures switching MOSFETs into 12 V LED strings, to I²C drivers such as the PCA9632 with 8-bit PWM resolution per channel across 16 outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-928" title="EEVblog #928 - Jaycar DMX RGB LED Lights Teardown">928</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1453" title="EEVblog 1453 - Elgato Key Light TEARDOWN">1453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-787" title="EEVblog #787 - Mailbag">787</a></sup> The advantage over a linear dim is [[efficiency]], and pulsing LEDs hard but briefly — 20 mA or more for a short fraction of each cycle — extracts good light output for very little average current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> Taken to its limit, pulse width modulating an indicator LED at a very low duty allows eight years of [[battery-life]] from a sensor light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup> A small strap light generating its brightness levels from a microcontroller PWM still holds 5 V at the far end of its cable at both extremes of brightness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup> Constant-current LED drive is the alternative and is what avoids the problem entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-765" title="EEVblog #765 - LED Panel Lighting Switching Noise">765</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup>

Flicker is the characteristic failure mode. It appears when the PWM frequency falls low enough to enter the visual range of 60, 50 or 40 Hz, and it is worst at intermediate brightness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> At 0% and 100% there is no flicker content because the waveform is not modulating at all; ceiling panels showing significant strobing at half brightness show none at full, and turning them to maximum removes both the strobe on a camera LCD and the modulation itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-364" title="EEVblog #364 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting Install">364</a></sup> Whether it is captured on camera depends on the relationship between the modulation frequency and the [[frame-rate]] — 25 frames per second in one such test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Some products avoid PWM in favour of an adjustable constant current, with no detectable flicker even at the lowest setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup> Long-exposure photography is more forgiving than expected: PWM-dimmed RGB LEDs did not produce artefacts in long exposures despite the concern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup>

The other cost of dimming is conducted and radiated noise. LED panel lights that were capable of PWM dimming generated substantial noise when dimmed and none when run at full brightness on constant current, and a set of studio lights running pulse width modulated is enough to couple noise into a supply-ripple measurement setup and corrupt it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-765" title="EEVblog #765 - LED Panel Lighting Switching Noise">765</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Noise attributed to a switching plug pack can in fact come from the modulation in the luminaires themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup>

PWM cannot rescue a driver from a peak-[[current-limit]]. Where a shift register such as an HC164 must share current between many multiplexed segments, no duty cycle scheme increases the instantaneous current the chip can source; attempting to drive segments at hundreds of milliamps simply exceeds the output rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup>

## Switching converters

Switch-mode power supplies are the classic high-power application. The TL494 is a generic PWM controller — not a complete switch-mode [[voltage-regulator]] as such, but usable for several things, with switch-mode supplies among its primary uses; in a 40 A bench supply it drives a pair of MOSFETs into the main [[transformer]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-272" title="EEVblog #272 - Manson 9400 40A 3-15V Switchmode PSU Teardown">272</a></sup> The same part appears controlling the main transformer in an insulation resistance meter, and a Signetics SG3524D fills the role in a handheld scopemeter's DC-DC section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> A variable-frequency AC source generating a selectable 40 Hz to 440 Hz sine output with typical distortion under 5% does so with PWM technology.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-449" title="EEVblog #449 - Absopulse VFC500 Variable Frequency Converter Teardown">449</a></sup> A mains-driven LED tube converter adds zero-current and valley switching, cycle-by-cycle control and fast transient response on top of the modulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup>

Not all converters run in PWM all the time. Many switch between a normal or pulse-frequency mode at very low load, where PFM gives greater efficiency, and PWM at higher currents, and a discontinuity in an efficiency curve is a signature of that mode change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> The peak [[inductor]] current limit is itself specified per mode — around 2.7 A at 5 V out for one [[boost-converter]] in PWM mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Resonant converters are the other branch: an LLC resonant design switches smoothly rather than hard, in contrast to a conventional PWM converter which varies both pulse width and, in some modes, frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup>

Gate drive at high frequency is its own problem. Isolated GaN driver chips accept the PWM signal on the low-voltage side, cross a galvanically isolated interface inside the package, and drive both gate and source of the [[transistor]] from a totem-pole output stage — one driver per transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> MOSFETs are the natural switching element for pulse width modulating loads, being voltage driven with no standing base current and faster than BJTs into the megahertz region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

## Motors, fans and actuators

A BLDC motor drive takes rectified mains down to a 12 V DC rail and then modulates the gate driver to synthesise the AC that turns the motor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTHcxTst_RA" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz MXO3 3 Phase Power Measurement">•</a></sup> A spacecraft-style motor controller runs its pulse modulation at about 64 kHz, and can afford to leave it asynchronous to everything else because the controllers sit physically outside the shielded aluminium enclosure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HBLKxC4JzTA" title="Space Electronics Circuit Protection">•</a></sup> Locked anti-phase drive is a variant in which the motor is driven forward and then reverse within every PWM cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-551" title="EEVblog #551 - Sydney Mini Maker Faire 2013">551</a></sup>

Fan control depends on the connector. A three-pin fan has a tachometer output reporting speed but no control input, so the only way to vary it is to change the supply voltage; PWM control requires the four-pin type.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4oHUuXgK_i4" title="Soldering Fume Extractor Fan Upgrade">•</a></sup> A fan controller board with a [[temperature-sensor]] ramps the duty cycle on its PWM pin linearly from 10% to 100% between a low and a high temperature threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--9l41zNWlxY" title="Guest Video: Voltlog #142 - Mailbag">•</a></sup> Instrument fans are similarly driven from a temperature sensor through a power transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup>

Modulated drive turns up in unexpected mechanisms — a breast pump's motor drive shows packets of variable pulse width modulation lasting a few milliseconds each, correlated with the pump cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> A toothbrush charger applies pulsed charging to its cell at around 800 Hz, visible as charge ripple appearing and disappearing on the [[battery]] and as a corresponding amplitude change in the transformer waveform as the extra current is drawn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup> An electropermanent magnet exposes an RC-style PWM interface alongside CAN and other buses, and can be operated from a 50 Hz 5 V PWM signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1656" title="EEVblog 1656 - MAILBAG: ElectroPermanent Magnet, DIN PSU&#x27;s, Sponge, Beelink EQ14 N150 PC">1656</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-964" title="EEVblog #964 - Mailbag">964</a></sup>

## PWM as a signalling protocol

In electric vehicle charging the modulation carries no power at all. An EVSE is not really a charger — it contains a large [[relay]] that switches the AC through and a small microcontroller that sends a PWM signal to the car telling it how much current is available, after which the car regulates its own charging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup> The duty cycle of this pilot signal is the message: 50% is the maximum on one 7 kW unit, at which the car elected to draw 29 amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-31gkF0q9VfE" title="Zappi EV Charger &amp; Cable Thermal Imaging">•</a></sup> A solar-diverting charger varies the pilot duty cycle continuously, with the car reading it and adjusting its charge rate roughly once per second; a configured 10% floor sets the minimum charge rate, which means a small amount of grid import when the excess solar falls below it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EYx46kRv2Bw" title="Myenergi Zappi 7kW EV Charger Installation &amp; Testing">•</a></sup> The whole loop is slow, since the controller must respond to the power measurement, change the PWM, and then wait for the car to change its draw.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EYx46kRv2Bw" title="Myenergi Zappi 7kW EV Charger Installation &amp; Testing">•</a></sup> Such units report the pilot duty cycle on their own display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup>

Elsewhere the waveform is the data channel by convention: an optical rangefinder module works correctly in PWM mode while its I²C mode is unusable because of the busy-wait NAK it sends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> A four-channel Cirrus Logic digital audio driver puts out four PWM outputs into external hex inverters and discrete transistor output stages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1514" title="EEVblog 1514 - Sonos: The Juicero of Wifi Speakers? (TEARDOWN)">1514</a></sup> A camcorder system-on-chip includes mono PWM audio intended for an external amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-595" title="EEVblog #595 - World&#x27;s Worst Shittiest Camcorder: Teardown">595</a></sup>

## Generating PWM without a microcontroller

The 555 timer in its standard PWM configuration remains a valid answer for dimming. An adjustment pot sweeps the duty cycle over very nearly the full range — measured from 8.8% up to 99.6% at the output pin, and from 0.3% to 99.2% at the discharge pin — with the frequency changing somewhat as the pot is turned, which for a dimmer does not matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> The circuit works equally well switching a constant-current source on and off as switching a voltage, so an existing current source can be retained.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> The inversion introduced by a PNP output stage shifts which end of the pot's travel corresponds to full brightness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> Discrete 555-based PWM controllers with a pot adjusting the pulse width turn up in commercial products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1291" title="EEVblog #1291 - Mailbag">1291</a></sup> The 558 quad 555 was used for a joystick port, with the joystick potentiometers setting the timer's pulse width and a timer counter chip measuring the resulting pulse length to derive position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup>

A microcontroller PWM output can also drive charge-pump topologies directly: a single pin producing a 10 kHz waveform into a Dickson doubler yields around 4.62 V DC into a 10k load, enough for a small LCD's bias, and tying the pump's reference to another pin allows the output to be switched off entirely under software control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

## Measurement and diagnosis

Whether a load is PWM driven is usually settled with an [[oscilloscope]]. A current probe on an LCD backlight string immediately distinguishes constant-current drive from modulation at around 120 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup> An open-collector output suspected of being a simple logic level turned out to carry a PWM signal, explaining a flicker whose rate changed with temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup> Spectral analysis of the audible tone from an electronic safe lock identified a 4.077 kHz peak matching a 4.075 kHz measurement, confirming that the tone was the PWM signal driving the piezo transducer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> Not every rhythmic waveform is a modulation channel, and identifying what an unexplained pulse train actually drives can remain open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> [[silkscreen]] and package markings can also give it away — a part labelled with PWM feeding thick traces is likely a stepper or motor driver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1011" title="EEVblog #1011 - Retro Teardown - World&#x27;s First HD DVD Player">1011</a></sup>
