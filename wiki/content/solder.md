Solder is the fusible alloy that joins electronic components to a [[pcb|printed circuit board]], and it does two jobs at once: it forms the electrical connection, and it provides the mechanical rigidity that holds the component to the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup> It is common to hear that solder is not meant to act as mechanical strain relief, but mechanical retention is in practice one of its two principal purposes, and joints that lose it fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup> Of everything on the bench, the solder itself — the right type, in the right diameter — is the single largest determinant of joint quality, ahead of the iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup>

## Diameter

Fine solder is the default recommendation: the smallest diameter that will do the job, and certainly under 0.5 mm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> A fine wire gives control over exactly how much metal enters the joint, which is the whole point, because too much solder is itself a defect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Typical working sizes are 0.46 mm for general through-hole and pad tinning,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-181" title="EEVblog #181 - Dead Bug Prototype Soldering">181</a></sup> 0.38 mm for [[surface-mount-technology|surface mount]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KzC12xtHuXo" title="Just Soldering an SMD Adapter">•</a></sup> and 0.35 mm down to 0.3 mm for the finest work such as [[soldering]] directly to a hard drive's micro-actuator flex.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1400" title="EEVblog 1400 - Hard Drive Micro Actuators are AMAZING!">1400</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup> At 0.38 mm the wire is about as small as commonly available, and it is fine enough that it is not really intended for through-hole work — feeding a through-hole joint with it takes a great deal of wire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup> For surface mount the fine wire is preferred precisely because feeding 0.8 mm stock gives far coarser control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

Thick solder still has a place. Soldering a component down to a [[ground-plane|ground plane]], where the board acts as a [[heat-sink|heat sink]], is done with a large chisel tip and 1 mm wire, because in that situation controlling the quantity of solder matters much less than getting metal and heat into a thermally massive joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Carrying only one diameter in a field kit is a limitation: a 1 mm roll is awkward when the task is feeding solder sideways into a broken [[connector]] pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1195" title="EEVblog #1195 - Sony 4K 60&quot; LCD TV Audio Repair">1195</a></sup> A minimal lab stocks both a fine roll and a coarse one, in both leaded and lead-free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup>

## Alloy

Standard 60/40 tin-lead, optionally with a small silver load, is the recommended general-purpose alloy; lead-free is explicitly not the first choice for hand work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> Leaded solder reflows at a lower temperature, which is the practical reason to prefer it even on processes that tolerate both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup> Lead-free tin-copper is used where the process demands it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> Lead-free joints are also implicated in cracking failures: a Garmin GPS whose connector joints had every single one broken is the characteristic presentation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup>

Alloys far from the eutectic behave very differently. A 97/3 solder, used at 180 micron pad width in an IBM multi-chip module, is brittle enough that chips can be pushed off their pads at room temperature with no heat applied at all, and the solder smears rather than deforming like familiar 60/40.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2G7Z9IIQoIs" title="IBM BGA Thermal Futility...">•</a></sup> Only the smallest parts resist this: chip capacitors and the large chips with many hundreds of balls under them have too many joints to shift by force and must be heated off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2G7Z9IIQoIs" title="IBM BGA Thermal Futility...">•</a></sup> Bulk solder for wave machines is supplied as bars, dipped into the molten wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2vJ0c0ioAXY" title="Electronex 2014 - Walkaround">•</a></sup>

## Flux

"Without [[flux]], nothing works."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Metals oxidize too readily for a joint to wet without it, so flux is non-negotiable, whether it comes from the core of the wire or from a separate liquid or gel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Multicore flux-cored wire — a five-core flux formulation, for instance — means no additional flux is needed for ordinary component soldering, surface mount included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup>

Two failure modes destroy the flux before it can act. Loading solder onto the tip and carrying it across to the joint burns the flux off in transit — visible as smoke as the wire touches the tip — and produces a cold or otherwise poor joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Running the iron too hot does the same thing, burning the flux away in smoke.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> The correct sequence is to place the chisel point so it touches both the pad and the pin simultaneously, then feed solder to the far side of the joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

## Technique

The core rules are a temperature-controlled iron, the right solder diameter, flux, a tip that couples heat into both pad and lead at once, and application of solder to the opposite side of the joint from the iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> A working temperature of around 350 °C is a reasonable standard for leaded work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> A joint that is slow to wet may need three or four seconds, and a small amount of solder fed toward the iron itself to establish thermal contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Well-wetted solder flows through a plated through-hole to the far side of the board on its own; where it does not, the joint may have wet only the side the iron was on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> A finished joint is a shiny fillet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup> Freshly soldered thermally massive components stay hot long after the iron is removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

Surface mount uses the tack-and-reflow method: tin one pad with a small lump of solder — not flattened out, or there will not be enough metal left to hold anything — bring the component in with tweezers, tack that one pin down, then complete the remaining joints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Working systematically matters, since it is easy to lose track of which ends have been soldered; doing all right-hand ends then all left-hand ends, or all top then all bottom, makes the omissions findable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

A well or wicking tip is filled with solder so that it bulges just above the surface and then dragged along a row of fine-pitch pins. It deposits only the quantity each pad requires and wicks the excess back into the well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup> Excess left behind on a single pad is enough to ruin the result on a [[bga|BGA]]-style part, where the chip then sits neither flat nor flush.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup>

Throughput depends on the interaction of solder type, tip, and the thermal sink capacity of the pad and part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> Two seconds per joint is an achievable target only if the iron has the reserve for it; small tips intended for fine surface mount work barely melt solder on a large ground plane even at 370 °C, and a low-end iron with no real [[thermal-capacity|thermal capacity]] may fail to heat a pad at all after thirty seconds of contact, even with molten solder applied to improve coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

## Rework and removal

An SMD chip can be removed with nothing more than an iron by covering all the pins on both sides with large blobs of ordinary solder, then alternating the iron between the two sides until everything is molten simultaneously, at which point the part lifts off with tweezers or falls off when the board is tilted. Large surrounding [[ground]] planes that sink the heat away make this fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> Low-melting-point rework alloy is applied the same way — solder added to every pin, with essentially no downward pressure on the iron, since the alloy is brittle and pads and pins lift easily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup> A [[desoldering]] station sucks molten solder out of the hole against gravity, which works readily on a joint at 300 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup> [[solder-wick|Solder wick]] removes what remains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup>

Old solder is not to be reused when the joint is suspect: it is sucked out entirely and replaced with fresh.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> Corroded joints are the hardest case; applying fresh solder to a corroded joint in order to desolder it can still be a miserable operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup>

## Reflow and wave

A reflow profile ramps up, peaks, and cools about as fast as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup> The thick band drawn across the profile is the acceptable temperature range for that specific solder, and every solder type has its own profile; individual parts have their own limits too, and component datasheets often carry recommended profiles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup> Even with a controller that ramps deliberately rather than simply switching the elements on, a converted oven does not necessarily deliver an even temperature across the board — solder on one side can be fully reflowed while the other side has not yet started.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>

In [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]], solder thieving pads are placed on the board deliberately; they exist because of the dynamics of the molten wave and how solder bleeds off the board as it passes through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup>

## Solder as a conductor

Solder measurably reduces [[resistance]] where it is added, contradicting the claim that only a wire through a [[via]] will help. Filling a pad and via with solder while measuring live drops the [[voltage]] across it from 1.312 mV by a significant margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Tinning a bare copper strip produces a resistance decrease on the order of 50%; wicking the solder back off returns the strip to roughly its original 52 milliohms, confirming the change is the added metal and not a measurement artifact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup>

The metal has mass, and enough of it accumulates over a large board that the solder and lead weight must be included in any centre-of-mass calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1278" title="EEVblog #1278 - Mailbag">1278</a></sup> It is also just wire when needed — a length of solder serves as an improvised [[antenna]] for a spectrum analyser sniff test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup>

## Related board and process defects

[[solder-mask|Solder mask]] between the pins is a layout requirement for hand-soldering fine-pitch parts, and getting [[solder-mask-expansion|solder mask expansion]] right is part of that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Gold flash pads cost only cents per board more and improve the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Where the [[silkscreen]] has been printed over a pad, no solder reaches the pin at all and the connection simply does not exist — a defect that can leave half the pins on a package unconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1181" title="EEVblog #1181 - Car ECO OBD2 OptiFuel Fuel Saver SCAM!">1181</a></sup> Old [[adhesive]] stick-on jumper links rely on the underlying solder mask to avoid shorting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-541" title="EEVblog #541 - Mailbag">541</a></sup> Conversely, the presence of proper solder rather than conductive adhesive or hot-bar bonding is a sign of a rigid, reliable interconnect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup>

Molten solder is a hazard in production: splashing is a real risk on a surface mount line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-100" title="EEVblog #100 - Triple Digit Retrospective, Wonder Woman, and More Multimeter Destruction.">100</a></sup> Solder and residual flux left in a [[fuse]] from manufacture are visible on high-speed footage as the element blows, with solder running along the wire as it cools.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup>
