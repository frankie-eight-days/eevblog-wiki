Battery life is the length of time a portable product remains functional on one set of cells or one charge, and it is set by the ratio of the usable energy in the [[battery]] to the average [[current]] the product draws.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> It is one of the few specifications that can decide a product's fate on its own: the choice of battery type and the runtime that follows from it can ruin a product or make it a complete success.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-18" title="EEVblog #18 - The Garmin Yellow Etrex GPS">18</a></sup> Because the number falls out of decisions made early — [[processor]], display, cell format, [[cutoff-voltage|cutoff voltage]] — it is a design constraint rather than a feature that can be added later.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup>

## Estimating runtime

The first-order estimate is capacity divided by average current, and it requires measuring the current the product actually draws rather than trusting the manual, which typically quotes consumption only.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Measurement is the practical route because directly timing a product that runs for hundreds of hours is not viable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> A worked example: 2.5 Ah of AA capacity divided by 150 µA gives 16,666 hours, or 1.9 years of continuous operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> Another: 650 mWh in a CR2032 divided by a 10 µW load gives 65,000 hours, about 7.4 years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1383" title="EEVblog 1383 - Human Battery Energy Harvesting BUSTED">1383</a></sup> A 100 mAh coin cell asked to last three years permits an average draw of roughly 3.8 µA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

Refining the estimate means choosing the right characteristic curve, because manufacturers publish service hours for constant-current, constant-power and constant-[[resistance]] loads, and a real product may be a combination of these plus pulsed activity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Even then, getting an accurate figure from [[datasheet]] curves alone is difficult.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Temperature is a large and often overlooked term: for one cell at a fixed draw, published service life runs from about 2.5 hours at −10 °C to 9 hours at 21 °C, a factor of more than three across a 30-degree span.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Datasheet curves also tend to stop at loads around 100 mW, leaving the ultra-low-drain region — the region that matters for products expected to run a year on a set of cells — poorly characterised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

[[duty-cycle|Duty cycle]] dominates everything else in intermittent products. Holding peak and active currents fixed while stretching the sleep interval moves the average current down proportionally: a device with a 15-second active event every 60 seconds yields about 43 hours, while the same device waking every 1000 seconds yields 560 days.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> The same principle drives design decisions elsewhere — a GPS tracker that logs on detected movement rather than continuously,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> a Z80 portable that sleeps between keypresses,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> and a PIR sensor light pulse-width-modulated at very low current to reach eight years on its cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>

## Cutoff voltage and usable capacity

A product's low-voltage cutoff determines how much of the cell's energy it can reach. Most equipment is designed to stop somewhere around 1 V per cell, but useful energy remains below that, and designing down to 0.8 V per cell maximises the extractable capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Products intended to run on rechargeables must work down to 1.1 V per cell in any case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Measured cutoffs vary widely: one meter holds down to 1 V per cell across four AAAs,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> another quits at 1.1 V per cell,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> and a 9 V [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] gives up at about 7.4 V, roughly 1.2 V per cell, which wastes a substantial fraction of the pack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> A Fluke 37 drops out between 6.4 and 6.5 V on a 9 V battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WWMXJLhPVdA" title="34,000 Hour Battery Life Fluke 37 Multimeter">•</a></sup> The correct way to find a cutoff is to feed the product from a bench supply and wind the [[voltage]] down, which is also the only practical way short of watching a battery discharge in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

## Cell format and chemistry

Cell format is a volumetric energy decision. A 9 V battery contains four small cells internally and therefore stores considerably less energy than the AA cells that fit in a similar footprint, giving poor battery life for no volumetric advantage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> Moving a [[multimeter]] from a 9 V battery to AA cells raised its rated life to 800 hours,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> and one design was deliberately made four or five millimetres thicker to accommodate AA cells rather than AAAs, on the judgment that the extra runtime was worth the thickness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> AAA cells shrink a product but cost runtime.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> C and D cells push capacity further but become unwieldy in handheld gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup>

Lithium primary chemistry occupies the extreme end. Two lithium thionyl chloride D cells of about 17 Ah each, powering a meter drawing 0.5 mA, work out to 34,000 hours — roughly 3.8 years of continuous operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WWMXJLhPVdA" title="34,000 Hour Battery Life Fluke 37 Multimeter">•</a></sup> Implanted and safety-critical equipment relies on the same approach: a neurostimulator's non-rechargeable lithium cell is specified to effectively the shelf life of the battery, on the order of ten years,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1027" title="EEVblog #1027 -  Implantable NeuroStimulator Teardown">1027</a></sup> and parachute automatic activation devices used certified Panasonic cells qualified for 10, 12 or 15 years while retaining low enough ESR to fire a heating element at end of life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1165" title="EEVblog #1165 - Cypres Parachute Safety AAD Teardown">1165</a></sup> Where a device must sit dormant for an unknown number of years, a lithium primary solution with a guaranteed ten-year-plus life is the appropriate choice rather than a rechargeable lithium-ion pack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1136" title="EEVblog #1136 - Banksy Artwork Shredder - Part 2 (Still Trolling!)">1136</a></sup> A large primary pack can carry even an [[led|LED]]-display instrument for about a decade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup>

## Displays and backlights

Displays are usually the largest single load in a low-power instrument. An OLED multimeter display delivers exceptional update rates and contrast but costs most of the runtime: one such meter managed eight hours against the several hundred hours typical of a good [[lcd|LCD]] meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-57" title="EEVblog #57 - Agilent Thumbs Up Furore">57</a></sup> Later OLED instruments improved on this considerably, though an LCD version remains preferable for runtime, and OLEDs are additionally poor outdoors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup> Backlights are a comparable penalty — 12 mA on low and 30 mA on high against a 1.7 mA base draw in one meter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> and 5 mA in another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup> The design response is either a fully reflective LCD with an optional momentary [[backlight]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup> or aggressive power-down of the display supply and backlight rails so only the [[microcontroller]] remains powered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup> Ultra-high-[[efficiency]] edge LEDs allow a front-lit e-ink reader to keep weeks of runtime with the light on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup> Sharp memory LCDs consume roughly 4 µA statically, about double what a three-year coin-cell digital watch budget allows, with dynamic consumption not much above that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

Colour LCD instruments illustrate the cost directly: a combined scope-multimeter drawing 350 mA in [[oscilloscope]] mode returns under 20 hours in multimeter mode and under 10 hours as a scope from a 3400 mAh pack,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup> while a 2000 mAh predecessor manages about 5.7 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup>

## Multimeters as the benchmark case

Handheld multimeters are the clearest worked example of battery life as a purchase criterion, and long runtime is treated as essential for a general-purpose meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> The rough scale is: about 1000 hours at the top of the market and rare, 300 to 500 hours for a good meter, and 100 hours or less as poor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Three hundred hours is taken as a decent figure, with 500 hours plus a reasonable target for a four-AA design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> Data-logging and graphing meters sit at the bottom of the range because memory and faster processors raise the baseline draw.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup>

Measured and claimed figures across the field bear this out. A Fluke 27 and a Fluke 37 are both rated at 1000 hours,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup> and a Fluke 23 at 2000 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1424" title="EEVblog 1424 - Fluke 23 Multimeter Repair">1424</a></sup> A Fluke 87V and a Fluke 117 claim 400 hours, with the 87V measured at 1.1 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup> A Fluke 233 claims 400 hours; at a measured 5 mA from 2800 mAh AA cells the arithmetic gives about 560 hours, comfortably meeting the claim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-43" title="EEVblog #43 - Fluke 233 Multimeter Review">43</a></sup> A Fluke 28 Series II claims 800 hours against an expected 3.5 mA and measures 1.7 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> A [[121gw|121GW]] at about 5 mA on four AA alkalines works out to roughly 600 hours nominal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> A Fluke 17B MAX claims 500 hours minimum at 5 mA and draws 2 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup> A wireless Fluke 87 variant on three AA cells quotes 250 hours minimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4UY3uI6tl1E" title="eevBLAB 121 - NEW Fluke 87VI Multimeter! MAYBE?">•</a></sup> At the other end, a $25 meter draws 1.7 mA,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> a budget meter yields 50 to 70 hours from two AA cells,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup> and a rechargeable novelty meter drawing 22 mA from a 130 mAh cell manages about five hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> A backlit rechargeable meter drawing 63 mA from a 1000 mAh cell returns roughly a dozen hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> The Fluke 189 shipped with about 50 hours, which the 289 raised to 100 hours — still far short of the several hundred hours of the general-purpose meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15" title="EEVblog #15 Part 1 of 2 - Fluke 189/289 multimeter review">15</a></sup>

LCR meters run shorter than multimeters as a class: 20 to 30 hours against 200 to 300 for a meter, so cells get changed often.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-234" title="EEVblog #234 - Agilent U1733C LCR Meter Teardown">234</a></sup> A 9 V LCR meter drawing about 22 mA gives roughly 45 to 50 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup>

Standby draw matters as much as operating draw for instruments that spend most of their life switched off. A meter with 143 µA of standby consumption gives roughly six months on the shelf, which is high for the task,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> whereas a 55 µA standby draw against an 800 mAh 9 V battery yields around 14,000 hours — effectively the shelf life of the cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup>

A recurring engineering position in this area is that battery-powered instruments should be deliberately over-engineered on runtime rather than merely adequate, since the cost is cheap and the failure mode — a flat meter at the moment it is needed — is the worst one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-197" title="RAW! Testing µCurrent&#x27;s, Keithley Current/Voltage Sources, DIY Pick And Place - EEVblog #197">197</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup> Dave Jones has repeatedly proposed a 10,000-hour bench multimeter, an order of magnitude beyond anything on the market, which corresponds to over a year of continuous 24/7 operation and is readily achievable by fitting a large primary battery into a meter with spare internal volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup>

## Rechargeable packs and cycle longevity

For rechargeable systems the relevant lifetime is the number of years the pack survives, not the hours per charge, and partial cycling is the standard lever. Limiting charge to 80% [[via]] a software cap extends pack life, with full charges reserved for long trips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1432" title="EEVBlog 1432 - Owning an ELECTRIC CAR for 12 Months &amp; 17,000km">1432</a></sup> Home storage installations are commonly run between a floor of 15 to 20% and a ceiling short of full, sacrificing usable capacity for longevity; one 15 kWh installation is run across 80% of its nominal capacity to that end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gAu8CvMjDrU" title="15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery - First Analysis">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5C_IT9F4ZkA" title="eevBLAB 128 - Why Dont I Go OFF-GRID Solar/Battery?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aANZNwmnC_4" title="AERL Lithium Home Storage Battery - Cloud Data">•</a></sup> Manufacturers advise against a full 100% discharge cycle every day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eHCzTC9lku8" title="The DOWNSIDE of my Home Solar Storage Battery">•</a></sup> Equipment that offers no way to set a lower float target forces the pack to sit permanently at 100%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8P8Af5SR57U" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery Follow--up">•</a></sup> Pack lifetime is also the limiting term in payback calculations: solar panels last 20 to 25 years while the battery does not, so a storage system that takes 20 or 30 years to pay back cannot do so before the battery needs replacing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wTzQg1N-SLI" title="eevBLAB 127 - Starlink as a Satellite Energy Utility?">•</a></sup>

## Claims and their verification

Battery life is a heavily marketed number, which makes it a frequent target of unsupported claims. The prominent example is a boost sleeve marketed as delivering an eight-fold increase in battery life; that figure holds only for the narrow class of products that cut out around 1.35 to 1.4 V, whereas the vast majority run to 1.1 V and would see improvements closer to 20%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> For some products the effect is negative, because the converter's own efficiency and quiescent draw can halve the runtime, and the maximum [[output-current|output current]] — the parameter that decides whether the device works in a high-drain product at all — went unpublished.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Practical testing bore this out: an MP3 player run on cells discharged to about 0.91 V returned a fraction of the claimed multiple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pa8j-Lje_2o" title="RAW Batteriser MP3 Player Test">•</a></sup> A later 30% claim for a similar concept is a defensible number, and one that design engineers can already realise by incorporating the same boost topology directly into a product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ixbLmBWUqcQ" title="Popular Mechanics Promotes Batteriser 2.0!">•</a></sup> Claims that a device eliminates keep-alive drain are similarly weighed against what already exists: a product cited as wasting nearly all its battery power already achieved a five-year battery life, and no chip turns that into 500 years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup> A nuclear battery claimed to last up to 28,000 years without replacement was the subject of SEC fraud allegations; the supporting circuitry alone bounds any such figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1579" title="EEVblog 1579 - Nuclear Diamond Battery FRAUD Lawsuit by SEC!">1579</a></sup>

## Consumer and portable equipment

E-book readers established the practical ceiling for consumer runtime, with e-ink devices claiming a month with wireless disabled, about three weeks with Wi-Fi on, and roughly a week on cellular.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-108" title="Amazon Kindle 3 3G GSM/WiFi Review - EEVblog #108">108</a></sup> A competing reader quoted six weeks or 14,000 page turns.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-188" title="iRiver Story HD Google Ebook Reader Review - EEVblog #188">188</a></sup> Continuous audio playback on the same hardware measured 24 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-108" title="Amazon Kindle 3 3G GSM/WiFi Review - EEVblog #108">108</a></sup> Runtime falls sharply once an LCD is involved: a 7-inch tablet was rated seven or eight hours,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-220" title="Amazon Kindle Fire Tablet Review &amp; Unboxing - EEVblog #220">220</a></sup> and another ten hours, short of a Sydney–Los Angeles flight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-322" title="EEVblog #322 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Review">322</a></sup>

Laptops span the same range. Vintage machines with reflective LCDs and 4000-series CMOS logic reached seven days of operation from four AA cells, or 20 hours on the earlier model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup> A 1993 colour PowerBook offered about an hour from a full charge,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W91qP8GpgjM" title="Apple Powerbook 165C Laptop">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup> and an x86 handheld a couple of hours, limited by thermal dissipation from a processor that ran hot in an inadequate volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-914" title="EEVblog #914 - Sony VAIO UX Micro PC Teardown">914</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup> Contemporary machines depend on configuration: an 80 Wh gaming laptop is rated 5.5 hours with a Ryzen and RTX combination against 7.5 hours for the Intel and GTX variant, while ultraportables claim 15 to 18 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p287qKMXqxQ" title="Lenovo Legion 5i 17&quot; Ryzen 7 RTX2060 Laptop">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Rmhu0Yvba2Y" title="So, I bought an Apple Macbook Air M2">•</a></sup> Thickness is the usual trade: adding one millimetre to a tablet would allow at least 50% more cell capacity and potentially double the runtime, a trade obscured when marketing optimises for thinness at constant battery life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5B6O1aFA5l8" title="eevBLAB 118 - NEW Apple iPad Pro: Volumetric Engineering vs Marketing">•</a></sup>

Elsewhere the constraint appears in whatever form the application dictates — a 16-hour wearable ESD monitor sized to cover a working day,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1567" title="EEVblog 1567 - Wireless ESD Monitor? - The IONA StatIQ Band TESTED">1567</a></sup> a bike light delivering 12.5 hours at its 300-lumen setting,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gTTsvYbQOT4" title="Olight RN1500 Bicycle Bike Light REVIEW + TEST">•</a></sup> a strap light drawing 1.37 A at full brightness for about 7 hours from 49.5 Wh,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup> an electrostatic whiteboard at 1 mA giving around 9 months from a 3000 mAh pack,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1149" title="EEVblog #1149 - How An Electrostatic Whiteboard Works">1149</a></sup> a wireless microphone whose processor is deliberately clocked well below its 72 MHz capability,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup> a shotgun microphone reaching 100 plus hours,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-182" title="EEVblog #182 - Rode Videomic Shotgun Microphone Hack">182</a></sup> a dosimeter at about 12 months,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-776" title="EEVblog #776 - World&#x27;s Smallest Digital Dosimeter">776</a></sup> and a planetary rover carrying only about two hours of battery reserve, sufficient to drive 500 metres before [[solar-power|solar power]] becomes mandatory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup>
