A transistor is a semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electrical signals and power, and it is one of the basic building blocks of modern electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hZnlDIvdabQ" title="Eleven Labs Voice AI DaveClone vs RealDave">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I4tHtPwYQ_o" title="DaveClone - Testing Eleven Labs AI Voice Cloning">•</a></sup> It is built from semiconductor material and normally brings out at least three terminals; a [[voltage]] or [[current]] applied to one pair of those terminals controls the current flowing through another pair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hZnlDIvdabQ" title="Eleven Labs Voice AI DaveClone vs RealDave">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I4tHtPwYQ_o" title="DaveClone - Testing Eleven Labs AI Voice Cloning">•</a></sup> Because the controlled output power can exceed the controlling input power, the device amplifies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hZnlDIvdabQ" title="Eleven Labs Voice AI DaveClone vs RealDave">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NFVEtIHGedg" title="Descript AI Voice Cloning - DaveClone vs RealDave">•</a></sup> A minority of transistors are packaged individually; overwhelmingly more are embedded in miniature form inside integrated circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hZnlDIvdabQ" title="Eleven Labs Voice AI DaveClone vs RealDave">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I4tHtPwYQ_o" title="DaveClone - Testing Eleven Labs AI Voice Cloning">•</a></sup>

Used for logic rather than amplification, transistors behave essentially as switches with two states, a low voltage state and a high voltage state, giving the zeros and ones of digital logic; a [[processor]] is then a large interconnected array of billions of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1316" title="EEVblog #1316 - Quantum Computing for Electrical Engineers">1316</a></sup> Because a gate is ultimately just transistors turning off and on, gate counts and transistor counts are often used interchangeably when describing a chip's complexity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7bVnsXHO6Uw" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 1 - Introduction To Digital Logic">•</a></sup>

## Two families

Bipolar junction transistors and field effect transistors are physically different in how they operate at the silicon level, not merely different in symbol.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> In a BJT, a depletion region blocks current flow from collector to emitter; once the base-emitter junction is biased and the collector voltage reaches threshold, the collector-base depletion layer shrinks until electron flow runs from emitter through to collector.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup> The result is current gain: a small base current produces a much larger collector-emitter current, which is what makes the device an amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup>

The simple two-junction picture is incomplete. The Ebers-Moll model places a [[diode]] between base and collector as well as between base and emitter, and that second junction causes real trouble in circuits where the base and collector sit at unexpected relative potentials — a transistor that theory says should switch on may not behave as drawn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup>

A [[mosfet|MOSFET]] brings out gate, drain and source instead of base, emitter and collector, and the same reference designator covers both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Hybrids of the two are used deliberately: a cascode arrangement combining a [[bipolar-transistor|bipolar transistor]] with a MOSFET buys output stability, and the cascode is known for exactly that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup> Mixing device types this way is unremarkable in practice — "a transistor's a transistor".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup>

## Identifying one on a board

Q is the industry standard reference designator for a transistor, so a part silkscreened Q3 or Q11 is a transistor of some type — BJT, MOSFET, or otherwise — while D would indicate a diode and REG or V a [[voltage-regulator|voltage regulator]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup> The number is only a sequence position: Q2 simply happens to be the second transistor on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup> The designator is often the first and fastest clue available, identifying an 80 V 40 A N-channel MOSFET or a 60 A N-channel part by its Q label before any part number is legible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup>

Package alone is not diagnostic. The classic three-pin SOT-23 is usually a transistor, a FET, a diode or a dual diode, and five-pin and six-pin variants of the same outline exist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> TO-92 shows up in precision instruments where its presence is itself surprising,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> in emitter-follower supply circuits,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> and in an older round-base variant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup> Larger devices appear in TO-3 and in the MT200 package, which is literally a TO3 heat spreader soldered to a larger heat spreader.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup> Metal-can transistors are characteristic of older gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup>

## Switching applications

The [[open-collector-output|open collector output]] is the archetypal use: an RF detector circuit switches its transistor on once the signal amplitude ramps past a level, pulling the output low through a pull-up [[resistor]] so a [[microcontroller]] can detect the presence of RF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup> The same coupling circuit distinguishes a 23-microsecond test burst from normal operation by whether the capacitively coupled amplitude is enough to switch the transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>

Transistors drive loads that logic cannot: [[relay]] coils,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> piezo buzzers,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> [[led|LED]] indicators,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup> and display blanking, where switching the transistor off pulls the blanking line low so data can be shifted in without the segments showing garbage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> A [[comparator]] with a strobe function needs an external transistor because the strobe is a current-driven input, and that external part is what actually disables the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> RFID and NFC cards modulate their reply by driving a transistor that loads down the secondary coil, and the resulting [[amplitude-modulation|amplitude modulation]] is recovered at the reader.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-889" title="EEVblog #889 - Credit Card RFID/NFC Theft Protection Tested">889</a></sup> An active jamming card can do the same with no external transistor at all, using one internal to the microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-890" title="EEVblog #890 - ArmourCard Active RFID Jamming Teardown">890</a></sup>

Driving an inductive load without a back EMF diode lets the flyback voltage rise until it destroys the transistor and whatever drives it, which is why such diodes are built into relay driver chips such as the ULN2003, one per output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> A relay can also fail to pull in without any device fault: if the rail feeding it sags, the transistor switches correctly but the coil current is insufficient to activate the relay, while all the digital circuitry continues to work normally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup>

Latching power circuits are a standard discrete application. Pressing a switch supplies base current, latching the transistor on and powering the circuit; a [[capacitor]] then charges on an [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]] plus the base current flowing through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup> Such a circuit is deliberately built from jelly bean parts only — basic transistors, resistors, caps and diodes — with no dedicated or hard-to-get chips, and with a minimal part count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup> The same topology appears in commercial gear as a control line turning on one transistor, then another, then a series power MOSFET that holds itself on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

## Analog and power applications

In a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] the series pass transistor's base current sets the trade-off directly: increasing the base resistor starves the transistor of base current, which increases the [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] across the pass transistor but improves [[ripple]] attenuation through the [[rc-filter|RC filter]] ratio.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> In a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]] the same device is driven hard off and on by an [[oscillator]]; while it is on, current flows through the [[inductor]] to the output and stores energy there, and when it switches off that current commutates through the diode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

An op-amp [[constant-current-source|constant current source]] is built by having the op-amp drive the base of a transistor, with the [[output-current|output current]] equal to the reference voltage divided by the [[current-shunt-resistor|current shunt resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> A high-power version of this idea appears as a pass bank array, in which each transistor gets its own op-amp and series base resistor so that constant current is set individually per device, rather than paralleling all the transistors behind a single external [[current-sense-resistor|current sense resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup> A two-transistor constant current circuit — optionally with the second transistor replaced by two series diodes — sets its current from the base-emitter voltage of the first device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup>

Other analog uses turn on device physics rather than switching. Putting a transistor in the feedback loop of an op-amp exploits the diode equation's nonlinearity to produce a logarithmic amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> A differential JFET front end normally requires additional transistors forming a [[current-source|current source]] beneath it, an arrangement common enough that a matched transistor array chip is the likely implementation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> A Darlington pair is used inside a 555 to raise gain so that input pin current stays very small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

Discrete transistor circuits remain viable where integration is unnecessary. A classic super regenerative receiver can be done with a single transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup> A metal detector front end is a two-transistor arrangement with a ferrite-cored drive and sense coil pair, effectively a [[transformer]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> An H-bridge is recognisable from its four transistors even without a part number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> Early FM bug designs used one transistor, with no gain for the microphone and no Q in the coil because the coil was flat on the [[pcb|PCB]] — a design later improved with additional transistors and RF amplifier stages while deliberately keeping output under 30 to 50 milliwatts to avoid interference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-3 - Talking Electronics - FM Bug Kits - Part 3 of 5">280</a></sup> Complexity is not proportional to part count: a system with one transistor, a couple of inductors and some capacitors can absorb a year of work purely on [[noise]] performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1565" title="EEVblog 1565 - UNSW Electronics Lab &amp; Makerspace Tour">1565</a></sup>

A joule-thief style blocking oscillator illustrates the feedback mechanism directly. Once the inductor's stored energy is exhausted, the base voltage falls back down, the transistor switches on again and the current ramp restarts; the collector voltage jumps well above the supply rail when both transistors are off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> Such circuits can push hundreds of milliamps through an LED with no [[current-limiting|current limiting]] resistor, which is a genuine design defect rather than a curiosity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup>

## Parameters that matter

Specification failures usually come from a parameter nobody looked at. A general purpose Toshiba muting and switching transistor is notable only for a VEBO of 25 volts, a figure that is almost impossible to find on other data sheets and follows from the physical construction of the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup> Reverse breakdown of the emitter-base junction is highly variable in practice: a part whose [[datasheet|data sheet]] says six can measure 7.7.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup>

Gain is not tightly controlled either. A discrete oscillator that depended on a particular transistor having a fairly specific gain and VCE produced units whose sensitivity varied all over the shop in production — sensitivity being the one parameter a microphone must not vary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-609" title="EEVblog #609 - Condenser Microphone Design Tutorial">609</a></sup> Avalanche pulse generators are worse still: a fast-edge generator producing perhaps 350 picosecond rise times depends entirely on the transistor, uses a 2N2369, and the parts are not all equivalent, so devices must be selected on test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup>

Thermal performance is specified as a chain of resistances beginning inside the package: theta JC is the [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] from the junction inside the transistor to the case, followed by the resistances of the [[via]], the insulating pad, the heat transfer block and the case itself, with temperature playing the role of voltage in the equivalent circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> Safe operating area curves bound the combination of voltage and current a device can survive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1194" title="EEVblog #1194 - 1985 Electronics">1194</a></sup>

Over-specification is real but cheap. A [[power-factor-correction|power factor correction]] MOSFET in a device drawing about 100 watts may be correctly specified at 500 volts — bang on the minimum for that application — while its 18 amp continuous rating will never be approached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup> The converse argument applies to bills of materials: consolidating a 10-cent high-power transistor and an 8-cent lower-power one into a single part avoids an extra reel and an extra pick-and-place feeder for the sake of 2 cents, and a 1 amp transistor is a perfectly good substitute where only 100 milliamps is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Feeder count is a hard constraint, with 80 feeders being a typical machine maximum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

## Failure and diagnosis

Transistors fail in ways that ordinary tests do not reveal. A component tester may detect the part correctly and report gain, and the device still be faulty through some obscure high-voltage breakdown; semiconductors do weird and exotic things.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1395" title="EEVblog 1395 - Onkyo Repair SUCCESS">1395</a></sup> A switching charger's transistor went high [[impedance]] after roughly three years of continuous service — it kept oscillating, but the collector would not pass much current, and output current fell abruptly to 30 milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup> A MOSFET running away thermally will climb past 50, 65, 70 degrees when it should not get anywhere near that unheatsunk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup>

Single event latch-up from radiation can destroy a transistor outright unless the device is switched off quickly enough and the stored energy is removed as well as the drive; heavy [[power-supply|power supply]] filtering is a liability here, because the bypassing holds energy that keeps feeding the latch-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-897" title="EEVblog #897 - Radiation Effects On Space Electronics">897</a></sup> Counterfeit parts are their own failure class, and swapping cheap fake transistors for genuine ones has resolved faults outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-337" title="EEVblog #337 - HSC School Electronics Projects">337</a></sup>

The proper instrument for characterising a suspect device is a curve tracer, or its modern replacement, a source measure unit that can source and measure both current and voltage and draw the characteristic curve of a transistor or diode junction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup> Semiconductors must be measured out of circuit, because surrounding wiring introduces errors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup> Interpreting in-circuit measurements demands care: a base measuring fine says little,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> and removing a device to prove a hypothesis can itself introduce a new fault, as when reinstalling and then re-removing a power factor correction transistor left a set that had previously worked drawing 3 W and refusing to start.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup>

Removing a power factor correction transistor is instructive about what the device does. Without it there is nothing to switch the inductor, so the circuit degrades to DC through the inductor and diode into the capacitor — inefficient and with no power factor correction, but functional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup> The cost of doing that is not local; if the correction transistors were removed at scale, utilities would need greater [[transmission-line|transmission line]] capacity and more generation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup>

## Protection circuits

Transistors are both what is protected and what does the protecting. A Schottky diode clamping at around 0.2 volts protects PN junctions, transistors and integrated circuits by holding them below the typical silicon threshold of roughly 0.6 volts, whereas a regular PN junction clamp might sit at 0.6 or 0.7 volts or higher at high current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Good [[multimeter]] [[input-protection|input protection]] adds transistors that switch on above a certain voltage level and clamp it down, beyond the primary clamping stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>

Transistors can also be used as clamps directly, in a configuration described as using them the way they should not be used but which appears in a product from a big name manufacturer: back-to-back bipolar junctions form a bipolar clamping Zener that works in either direction against impulses, overloads and ESD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup> The back-to-back Zener clamp built from two transistors turns up as the entire input protection on some oscilloscopes<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup> and as a two-transistor clamp in the non-contact voltage detection front end of a socket tester.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup>

## Cost, scale and history

Shockley is popularly credited with the transistor, though a patent predates him by roughly two decades held by an inventor who could not make the device work; it was Bardeen and Brattain at Bell Labs who got the first junction working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TTH24s0TTVU" title="Physics In The Pub - Famous Patents">•</a></sup> Shockley is not to be confused with Walter Schottky, a different person in the same field.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> The first commercially bought transistors were serious purchases: an AC71 bought around May 1956 cost £3.176, on the order of $100 in present money — against which a modern phone contains billions of transistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fK2KBDo7ISY" title="Dick Smith - Amateur Radio &amp; Adventure">•</a></sup>

That expense shaped practice. Transistors were prized components, salvaged out of scrapped gear and then tested before reuse, which is why transistor analysers were sold as kits and transistor testers were a very popular magazine project through the 1970s and 1980s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-693" title="EEVblog #693 - AVO Transistor Analyser Teardown">693</a></sup> Devices now costing a fraction of a cent, with fully characterised data sheets, have removed the motive entirely — but there was a time when "owning a transistor tester like this was the duck's guts".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-693" title="EEVblog #693 - AVO Transistor Analyser Teardown">693</a></sup>

Integration changed the arithmetic. Early hobby kits advertised an integrated circuit containing three transistors and five resistors in a single inline package as a marvel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup> Apollo-era diode transistor logic modules contained one transistor, two back-to-back diodes and some resistors, forming gates from diodes, transistors and pull-up resistors, with only a couple of transistors per chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup> An 8085 held 6,500 transistors on a three micron process, a figure that would now fit on the head of a pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> Modern phone silicon is estimated in the hundreds of billions of devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup> The first electronic watch, a Bulova Accutron from around 1964, was among the first products built with a transistor at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-911" title="EEVblog #911 - 2016 Sydney Maker Faire Part 2">911</a></sup>

The engineering argument for integration is not that silicon transistors are individually superior — at the individual transistor level silicon can be beaten by other technologies — but that integration allows so much functionality to be combined that no discrete approach competes on capability and cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tjdae8oqYMQ" title="The Signal Path Discussion - Engineering &amp; RF (3/3)">•</a></sup> The same logic runs the other way in instrument design, where a redesign to hit an aggressive price point replaced expensive active parts with transistors, which are usually quite cheap but trickier to design and compensate around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup>

Research devices continue at the other extreme of scale. A modified MOSFET fabricated as a qubit readout device measures about 50 by 100 nanometers and is designed to be strongly non-linear, switching in response to the change in position of even a single electron in its vicinity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1316" title="EEVblog #1316 - Quantum Computing for Electrical Engineers">1316</a></sup>
