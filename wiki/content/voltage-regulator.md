A voltage regulator takes an input supply of uncertain or varying value and holds an output rail at a fixed or set [[voltage]], and it is one of the few parts that appears in essentially every powered design regardless of what else the design does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Two families exist: linear regulators, which drop the surplus voltage across a pass element, and switching regulators, which convert it; catalogue search categories split them further into complete regulators with an integrated pass [[transistor]] and switching controllers that drive an external one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> Regulators are ubiquitous enough that a product with none at all — a toy running its [[microcontroller]] straight off the cells to save every possible cent of bill-of-materials cost — is itself the noteworthy case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup>

## How a linear regulator behaves

A three-terminal [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] has only three legs, so every milliamp entering the input pin must leave either through the output pin or through the [[ground]] pin; the ground-pin component is the [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]], and it is wasted regardless of load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> The classic implementation puts an [[output-voltage|output voltage]] divider feeding back into the regulator, which drives a pass transistor to whatever conduction the loop demands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Where a monolithic part cannot supply enough [[current]], an external series pass transistor is added around it, and instruments built this way give each regulator channel its own pass transistor and current-sense [[resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup>

Regulation requires headroom. The [[dropout-voltage|dropout voltage]] — the minimum input-to-output difference below which the loop can no longer hold the rail — is not a single number but a function of [[output-current|output current]], and datasheets accordingly specify it at several currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> A common jellybean adjustable part is specified at up to 1.2 V of dropout at 800 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> A modern low-cost SOT-23 part measures about 300 mV at full rated current and roughly 150 mV at 100 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Below dropout the output simply follows the input down and the circuit dies; in a thermoelectric-harvesting supply the load kept running on stored charge for a moment once the source fell under the regulator's dropout, then faded out entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup>

Dropout is what makes [[battery]]-powered design awkward. A 5 V regulator fed from four AA cells in series has only about 6 V nominal to work with, and the cell voltage falls steeply, so even a low-dropout part will not regulate across much of the discharge curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup> A design whose supply survives all the way down to about 3.3 V is therefore evidence that the rail is not a 5 V linear regulator at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup> Where a microcontroller supervises the supply, an ADC input reading the incoming source voltage lets [[firmware]] determine in advance whether the regulator is about to drop out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Seen from the battery, a regulated circuit is not a resistive load. Because the regulator holds a constant voltage across the downstream circuitry, the current drawn from the cell is essentially constant regardless of cell voltage, which is a constant-power load and behaves very differently from a fixed resistor during a capacity test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> One handheld meter carries three regulators for this reason, at 4 V, 3.6 V and 3.3 V, the last for its internal ARM [[processor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> A lithium-ion charger in its constant-voltage phase does exactly what a linear regulator does, holding 4.2 V while the current tapers away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup>

## Dissipation, heat sinking and layout

Everything a linear regulator drops it dissipates. Turning 12 V of battery into a 3.3 V project rail throws most of the pack's energy away as heat, which is a strong argument against linear regulation in portable equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-258" title="EEVblog #258 - PSU Housing Design - Part 11">258</a></sup> Where linear regulation is retained, the heat must go somewhere: bench-supply designs mount the regulators on a rear [[heat-sink|heat sink]] with the board deliberately extended so the back panel slides behind it and keeps the heat outside the enclosure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-258" title="EEVblog #258 - PSU Housing Design - Part 11">258</a></sup> Instrument builders bolt regulator tabs to the chassis or to an internal aluminium plate with insulating pads, using the case itself as the heat sink and mechanically restraining the parts against vibration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> Free-standing TO-220 regulators inside an extruded enclosure need no insulating washer provided the case is not being used as the heat path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-644" title="EEVblog #644 - How To Design Front Panels On Extruded Enclosures - µSupply Part 14">644</a></sup>

Under-sizing that heat path is a real failure mode rather than a theoretical one: one bench supply shipped with a heat sink too small for its regulator, pushed the part past its maximum [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]], and had to be re-engineered with a much larger sink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> The alternative to brute-force heat sinking is to keep the drop small in the first place. A tracking pre-regulator — a switch-mode stage whose output follows the required linear output — holds the drop across the output regulators to a minimum and allows a surprisingly small heat sink for a supply rated at ±25 V and 6 V at 3 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup>

Heat sinking also constrains the board. With fixed regulators mounted on a rear heat sink, the circuitry associated with them belongs at that edge of the board, because running the power traces the length of the board to reach it destroys the layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Regulators bring their own passive furniture: an input [[capacitor]], an [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]], and the bulk supply capacitor that sits at the regulator output and serves the low-[[frequency]] charge demands of everything downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup>

## Ripple, noise and precision

Despite the name, regulators are poor filters. They hold the DC output precisely, but attenuation of tens or hundreds of millivolts of input [[ripple]] is limited, and a low-dropout part is a weak tool for removing ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> A [[capacitance]] multiplier — a pass transistor whose base capacitor is multiplied by the transistor gain — removes ripple far more effectively, but it has no error amplifier and no feedback loop, so its output voltage moves with input voltage, temperature and load; it is not a regulator and is best used ahead of one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

Regulator [[noise]] matters where the load is sensitive. In condenser microphone bias supplies a regulator is likely to be noisier than a zener-derived rail, and a zener with a soft knee is quieter still.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-609" title="EEVblog #609 - Condenser Microphone Design Tutorial">609</a></sup> For precision analogue sections the answer is local regulation: instrument designers do not feed op amps directly from the main ±18 V rails but regulate them again on each card, and the same reasoning puts dedicated local rails beside a precision ADC and beside an [[oscilloscope]]'s analogue front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> Fast digital loads impose a different requirement — 1 ns comparators take large gulps of current when they switch, so they are fed from a fast transient-response regulator placed locally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

A regulator is not a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]]. Initial accuracy is typically a couple of percent, and an adjustable part trimmed by hand reaches perhaps 1 to 1.5 percent, which is often not enough where an ADC threshold depends on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Where a low-current digital section needs better, it can be powered directly from a precision 2.5 V, 3.3 V or 5 V reference of 0.1 to 0.2 percent, available for about a dollar, provided the load stays inside the reference's current rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> The vocabulary follows the distinction: dropout voltage is a regulator specification and does not normally appear in a voltage reference [[datasheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> A zener and [[series-resistor|series resistor]] will regulate a rail adequately if [[efficiency]] is unimportant, but the resistor value becomes a compromise as soon as the input voltage and the load both vary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

Temperature affects regulators as it affects every other component, which is why an oven test is worth running on a finished product; an [[arduino|Arduino]] board's rails measured 5.015 V and 3.407 V at room temperature before being cycled over range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup>

## Identifying and checking a regulator

A small three- or five-pin SOT-23 or SOT-223 device with capacitors to ground on two of its pins and disproportionately wide traces running to it is almost certainly a regulator; a SOT-223 package in particular is a reliable giveaway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup> The absence of bypass capacitors on the input and output is equally informative, and rules a part out as a regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup> Whether it is fixed or adjustable can be read off the feedback path: a [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] back to a feedback pin means the output is set by those resistors, while a bare output with only a filter capacitor points to a fixed part, which must then be replaced with the same voltage rather than an adjustable equivalent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup> Distinguishing linear from switching requires looking at the surrounding components — an [[inductor]] nearby means switching.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup>

Regulators are the first thing to measure. The rule is "thou shalt test voltages", and on an unknown or dead board that means finding the regulator-looking packages and probing input and output before consulting a [[service-manual|service manual]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1418" title="EEVblog 1418 - The Most EMBARRASSING Repair!">1418</a></sup> The same check is the first step in bringing up a new board: measuring 3.3 V across a [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] near the microcontroller confirms the regulator, and confirms there is no short pulling the rail down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (3 of 5) : How to program an STM32 using DFU Bootloader">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup> Many boards make this easy because the regulator tab or can is tied to the common ground, so one probe can stay put while the other roams.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Regulators are usually reliable, and the usual way they die is over-voltage on the input — most often caused by a failed upstream supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> When that happens the damage is rarely confined to one part: a single failed mains supply on one instrument took out at least three separate regulators across several boards, so finding one dead regulator is a reason to check the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> The signature of a destroyed linear regulator is a shorted pass transistor, measurable as a fraction of an ohm between input and output — 0.3 Ω in one case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> A regulator with a shorted input-to-output path leaves an unregulated rail at whatever the input happens to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> In supplies where diodes and regulators share the same rail, the diodes generally fail first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup>

Marginal thermal behaviour produces subtler faults than outright failure. A supply whose regulator overheated continued to read 5 V on a [[multimeter]] while its AC content changed enough to reset the digital board beside it — the DC value looked correct and only an AC-coupled scope trace at 100 mV per division revealed the intermittent dropout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Thermal imaging is useful here: three regulators sitting at 70 °C in a repair stand out immediately against the neighbouring parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>

Reverse energy is the other hazard. If a battery is left connected to the output while the input supply is switched off, current flows backwards into the regulator and can destroy it, which is why traditional linear supplies fit a reverse-protection [[diode]] across the pass element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-233" title="EEVblog #233 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 6 - LT3080 Testing">233</a></sup> A zener used to protect a regulator against input over-voltage needs a series resistor to limit its current, without which it simply destroys itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup>

## Jellybean parts and system cost

The staple parts are worth stocking by the handful: the fixed 78xx series and the adjustable [[lm317|LM317]] belong in any basic component inventory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> The 7805 appears in analogue boards of every vintage alongside comparators and op amps, with the 78L05, 7809 and 7812 covering other rails and package sizes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-275" title="EEVblog #275 - PIR Sensor Teardown &amp; Tutorial">275</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup> Negative counterparts — the 79xx series and the LM337 — cover the negative rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> The LM317's usefulness comes from its internal 1.25 V reference, which sets the lowest output it can produce and makes it suitable for the low rails of modern parts; tying its adjust pin to its output through a [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] converts it into a constant-[[current-source|current source]] or current limiter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

Modern digital designs multiply the count. High-density logic wants 3.3 V, 2.5 V, 1.8 V, 1.2 V and sub-volt core rails, so half a dozen supply rails in one product is unremarkable, and three to five regulators per unit is common.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> An [[fpga|FPGA]] design illustrates the ratchet precisely: 3.3 V for I/O, a separate 2.[[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] because the auxiliary supply pin demands it and nothing else in the design does, and 1.2 V for the core — each an extra regulator, extra bill-of-materials cost and extra board area, and each worth planning for at the outset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-636" title="EEVblog #636 - FPGA Demo Boards - DE0 Nano">636</a></sup> Even a part that runs from a single supply in operation may need a different voltage during programming, forcing another rail onto the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

Cost per regulator therefore matters at volume. Generic parts from Asian catalogue suppliers reach roughly 1.5 cents apiece, around a fifth the price of the Western-branded equivalents, and one such part tested well on accuracy, load regulation, dropout, stability into capacitive loads and recovery from short circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> At the other extreme, the most expensive regulator listed on a major distributor was $1,653, held in stock in quantity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup> [[parametric-search|Parametric search]] is the practical route to selection, filtered by output voltage, current, package, dissipation, price and availability — though the category taxonomy is a trap, since a regulator and a regulator controller are listed separately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup>
