Electromagnetic shielding is the use of conductive barriers — cans, cases, plates, tapes, sprayed coatings and meshes — to stop electromagnetic energy crossing from one place to another, in either direction. It keeps a circuit's own switching harmonics from radiating out of the box, and it keeps ambient fields out of sensitive front ends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup> Because the barrier only works when it is conductive and connected, [[shielding]] is inseparable from [[grounding]]: an ungrounded piece of metal over a circuit is decoration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KsBVBtUDYx4" title="Alesis M1 Active 520USB Studio Monitor Teardown">•</a></sup> In practice the amount of shielding in a product is one of the clearest visible indications of how seriously its designers took electromagnetic compatibility.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup>

## What the shield is doing

There are two distinct escape routes for [[noise]] from a switching product. Radiated emissions come from the circuit itself: fast square waves generate large harmonics, and the traces carrying them act as little antennas that radiate RF energy out of the box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> A properly shielded enclosure should in theory suppress that entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> The second route survives the shield: unless the product runs from batteries entirely inside the enclosure, cables must pass in and out, and the internal noise piggybacks onto them as conducted emissions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> A shielded box on its own is therefore only half a solution.

The same barrier works in reverse. An unshielded [[oscilloscope]] front end sits open to whatever is nearby, and mains-[[frequency]] electric fields couple straight into it — an [[esd-mat|ESD mat]] left floating behind the bench makes an effective 50 Hz pickup source, and the coupling to the scope is capacitive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup> The distinction between electric-field and magnetic-field problems matters when choosing the remedy: relays in the same instrument were covered with magnetic shielding tape, but the 50 Hz ingress was an [[electric-field|electric field]] problem, for which a plain metal can is the fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup>

That failure mode is easy to reproduce by omission. Reassembling a scope without refitting the metal can over a channel's front end reintroduces visible 50 Hz on that channel, with no other plausible cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wWymcaiaQXM" title="What happened to my Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202?">•</a></sup> Refitting a substitute can, tack-soldered at one corner and not even fully formed to the original shape, removes the pickup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup> Manufacturers [[solder]] such cans down rather than clipping them, presumably so vibration cannot shake them loose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wWymcaiaQXM" title="What happened to my Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202?">•</a></sup> A comparable effect shows in an unshielded display module driven on the bench, where the readout picks up surrounding noise simply because it is not yet enclosed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1669" title="EEVblog 1669 - Agilent 53131A VFD to LED Display Upgrade MAILBAG">1669</a></sup>

## Oscillators, clocks and self-interference

Shielding is often aimed at a specific aggressor rather than the board at large. A shield inside a [[multimeter]], bent over and bonded by a trace to the chassis wiring, exists to isolate the digital circuitry, and its position immediately adjacent to the crystal indicates the clock is the source being contained.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup> An audio-band [[signal-generator|signal generator]] built around a 1 kHz [[oscillator]] is screened as a complete assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-693" title="EEVblog #693 - AVO Transistor Analyser Teardown">693</a></sup>

Self-interference is the acute case. A super-regenerative receiver contains an LC tank oscillator with positive feedback that genuinely oscillates, so an unshielded one transmits on very nearly the frequency it is trying to receive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup> Two such receivers in proximity, neither properly shielded, spew RF at the receive frequency and swamp one another, producing erratic reception that looks like a receiver fault rather than a shielding fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup>

## Signal integrity and low-level measurement

At the extremes of measurement, shielding stops being an EMC nicety and becomes the only way the instrument works at all. A precision pulse generator of the kind Jim Williams built requires the utmost [[signal-integrity|signal integrity]], and without shielding, 50 Hz swamps the measurement outright — even its [[battery]] box is shielded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> A Wheatstone bridge intended for megohm, gigaohm and teraohm [[resistance-measurement|resistance measurement]] devotes considerable design effort to shielding and guarding, together with careful wiring layout, to prevent leakage and interference from corrupting extremely low currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup> A precision [[resistance]] calibrator shields its wire-wound elements because a wire-wound [[resistor]] is a coil, and nothing should be allowed to couple into it; some of the resistors arrive already shielded at one end of the can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup> Similar reasoning applies to source measure units, whose switch-mode supply is put in its own well-shielded enclosure as a matter of necessity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>

The principle extends beyond electromagnetic fields to any unwanted radiation. In a photon-counting setup, wrapping the lead in black electrical tape left the count slightly high, and adding several turns of aluminium foil over it brought the count down — the same wrap-and-fold technique, applied to light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup>

## Construction and materials

Shielding appears in a wide range of physical forms. Bulk pressed metalwork and die-cast alloy cans account for much of the weight of well-built test equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-323" title="EEVblog #323 - Rigol Scratch&#x27;n&#x27;Sniff">323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-144" title="EEVblog #144 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup> Surface-mount cans soldered to the board shield individual sections and are frequently unopenable without [[desoldering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-521" title="EEVblog #521 - Picoscope 5000 USB Oscilloscope Teardown">521</a></sup> Machined aluminium covers with built-in braces seal off individual RF sections in a spectrum analyser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> Conductive spray applied to the inside of a plastic case shields a product with no metalwork at all,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Yyuk3--FWfs" title="Samson USB GO Mic Teardown">•</a></sup> and nickel plating or screening of a 3D-printed enclosure serves the same purpose in low volumes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> Metallized shielding tape — genuinely metallized, not plain mylar — covers small groups of components such as capacitors and an [[inductor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-907" title="EEVblog #907 - RETRO Teardown: Sony Video Walkman">907</a></sup> Copper shielding tape wrapped around a tape head keeps noise out of the head signal, which then travels to the electronics on shielded [[coaxial-cable|coax]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup> Elsewhere the enclosure itself does the work: a mostly metal-shielded computer case is dense enough that [[antenna]] placement has to be designed around it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1211" title="EEVblog 1211 - Apple&#x27;s Most Beautiful Failure. The G4 Cube">1211</a></sup> and an aluminium housing shields motor-controller electronics well enough that a 64 kHz motor [[pwm|PWM]] frequency need not be synchronised to anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HBLKxC4JzTA" title="Space Electronics Circuit Protection">•</a></sup>

RFI gaskets close the seams. Conductive gaskets are fitted around [[usb|USB]] and Ethernet connectors on scopes,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> and the general product family includes clip-on gaskets, ultra soft shield gaskets and V-shaped gaskets, sitting between metal shields to maintain continuity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup> Conductive RFI sponge, normally an enclosure-shielding component, is occasionally repurposed for something else entirely — in one appliance it serves as the touch contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1523" title="EEVblog 1523 - Dumpster Samsung Cleaning Robot TEARDOWN">1523</a></sup>

Where a shield must be transparent, fine conductive mesh is used. Such mesh measures around 50 ohms across it yet conducts all the way through, allowing an opening in a case to be shielded while remaining see-through — the same principle as a microwave oven door, which blocks 2.45 GHz while permitting vision, though a very fine mesh covers a considerably wider range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup>

## Grounding and bonding

A shield's effectiveness depends on where it connects. Battery contacts decoupled directly to chassis [[ground]], together with a separate shield behind the case metalwork, indicate the designers judged the back metal case alone insufficient and wanted a more localized, lower [[impedance]] ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup> The same intent appears where metal cans are earthed and bonded over to a mechanism's main chassis,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-937" title="EEVblog #937 - Retro Canon Still Camera Teardown!">937</a></sup> where a shielding board placed between the mains wiring and the audio electronics is explicitly earthed,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KsBVBtUDYx4" title="Alesis M1 Active 520USB Studio Monitor Teardown">•</a></sup> and where a shield ties down to the metal frame surrounding a battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-937" title="EEVblog #937 - Retro Canon Still Camera Teardown!">937</a></sup> A large grounded shielding plate under a board can be covered with tough insulating film, not mylar, to guarantee that nothing arcs through to the shield.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup> In an oscilloscope repair the fix was reciprocal: grounding the ESD mat removed it as a 50 Hz source, but the unshielded front end remained the underlying problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup>

Shielding sometimes also serves mechanically. Deep ridges and high walls in a shielded multimeter case, combined with an O-ring, provide blast containment should the meter fail internally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup> A structural inner metal plate in a plastic-backed instrument does double duty as [[heat-sink|heat sink]] and as shielding where shielding is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

## Judging a design by its shielding

Comprehensive shielding correlates with instrument weight, and belt-and-braces metalwork is the norm in mid-range and better test gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-323" title="EEVblog #323 - Rigol Scratch&#x27;n&#x27;Sniff">323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix MDO4000 Oscilloscope Teardown - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-144" title="EEVblog #144 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">144</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup> Notable omissions are therefore informative. A 200 MHz oscilloscope whose analog input circuitry has only partial shielding walls rather than a complete metal can is unusual, since even lower-end scopes in that category typically use a full can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-187" title="EEVblog #187 - Tektronix TDS2024C Oscilloscope Teardown">187</a></sup> Another scope has no shielding at all between its switch-mode supply and the main logic board — less consequential when the adjacent circuitry is digital.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> An inexpensive handheld multimeter may contain no extra internal shielding whatsoever.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

Older equipment shows both extremes. A 1970s home computer combines long unshielded [[ribbon-cable|ribbon cable]] running to an expansion box with no metallized shielding inside the case and no chokes on the I/O lines — an arrangement that cannot work when wide parallel memory buses are involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup> By contrast, a 1980s notepad computer shields the entire keypad backing as well as the main board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup> a portable PC of the same era contains enough shielding to account for much of its mass,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup> a benchtop multimeter of 1980 vintage adds a metallized front panel for extra front-end shielding,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup> and camera, camcorder and portable-audio products of the period apply heavy shielding around transport mechanisms, image sensors and video processing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-375" title="EEVblog #375 - Sony Video 8 Camcorder Teardown">375</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-380" title="EEVblog #380 - Sony CCD Sensor Teardown">380</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup> A workstation-class computer was additionally offered in a Tempest variant — an electromagnetically shielded model for government and military use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1404" title="EEVblog 1404 - The Amazing HP 9845B Computer">1404</a></sup>

At the far end of the scale, the copper box inside a dilution refrigerator shields a quantum computer chip both to reduce electromagnetic noise and to prevent high-frequency photons, as carriers of energy, from reaching the chip and heating it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup> In spacecraft ordnance handling, safety practice combined dead-shorting and twisting the bridge wire leads so they could not pick up broadcasts, solid grounding, and enclosure in an aluminium cylinder, so that no conceivable path existed to pass [[current]] through the wire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup>

## Simple shields versus elaborate ones

Where a shielding problem is real, the simple passive answer is usually the right one. Against RFID reading, an active jamming card is a complex solution to a problem that either carries no real risk or is solved by aluminium foil or an ordinary shielding sleeve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-890" title="EEVblog #890 - ArmourCard Active RFID Jamming Teardown">890</a></sup> Conversely, invoking shielding where no mechanism exists is a marker of pseudoscience: claims that wiping a [[fuse]] box reduces electromagnetic waste appear to descend from an earlier product that was simply a piece of copper or brass presented as a shield.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-870" title="EEVblog #870 - Shonky Z-Energeia Energy Saving Wipes">870</a></sup>
