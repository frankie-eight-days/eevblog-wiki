A clock signal is a periodic digital [[waveform]] that gives a system a shared time reference, against which data is launched, sampled and latched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> It swings between the normal logic levels of the family in use — 5 V and 0 V for a 5 V TTL system, 3.3 V and 0 V for a 3.3 V one<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup> — and its defining characteristic on an instrument is that it is always transitioning, which is what makes it immediately identifiable among the other lines on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Because almost every synchronous digital device depends on it absolutely, a missing or degraded clock is one of the most productive things to look for first when a board is dead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>

## Role in synchronous systems

In a synchronous interface the clock is the reference to which everything else is correlated; a data line has no meaning without it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> A bit-banged transmission is typically a tight loop of bit sets and bit clears with a clock edge between each bit, and without that clock the slave device receives no commands at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Which edge acts is a property of the part: a counter may advance only when the clock goes negative and do nothing at all on the positive transition<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>, while a 74HC164 eight-bit serial [[shift-register|shift register]] moves the data present at its input into Q0 on the positive-going edge and cascades each stage onward on the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup>

The clock also defines the timing budget that the receiving device imposes. [[datasheet|Data sheet]] switching waveforms specify a setup time, tSU, that data must be stable before the clock transition; violating it can drive the chip metastable, leave data unlatched and produce arbitrary misbehaviour throughout a design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup> Timing diagrams conventionally carry a clock along the top precisely so that the rest of the drawing is anchored in real time — drawn properly, the presence of the clock implies that the horizontal axis is to scale, and a diagram that breaks the clock with dashed lines or an S-shaped break is signalling that some interval has been elided.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup>

## Buses that carry a clock

Serial buses are normally described by their clock alongside data: [[i2c|I2C]] carries clock and data lines<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8P8Af5SR57U" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery Follow--up">•</a></sup>, I2S carries data and clock lines typically fed through series resistors<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>, and [[spi|SPI]] adds a chip select to clock and data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-290" title="EEVblog #290 - Mailbag">290</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> Some decoders offer a timeout mode that infers framing from idle time, so clock and data alone can be captured on two channels without the chip select.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> Debug and programming interfaces follow the same pattern, with a clock pin and a data pin into the [[microcontroller]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup>

[[led|LED]] strips and display drivers often add a latch to the clock and data pair, the latch defining when a shifted-in packet is transferred.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1228" title="EEVblog #1228 - Do Digital Scopes Have REAL Verniers?">1228</a></sup> With a scope triggered on the latch rather than the clock, several packets of clock and data can be seen going past while only the one framed by the latch going low is accepted by a given chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1228" title="EEVblog #1228 - Do Digital Scopes Have REAL Verniers?">1228</a></sup> Where the latch is absent the image marches down the strip instead of appearing at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> A shift register chain of this kind may present a register clock, a shift clock and a clear; the clear is optional, since zeros can simply be shifted in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

## Where clocks come from

Most microcontrollers derive their clock from a crystal across X1 and X2, with an internal [[inverter]] between the two pins. This makes X1 a digital input, so an external [[oscillator]] or any other suitable signal generated elsewhere in the circuit can be driven straight into it; X2 typically feeds the internal circuitry and can also be used to drive external logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup> The resulting system clock is often a division of the crystal: a machine running from a 9 MHz crystal was measured at 3.266 MHz, a divide by three.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup>

Dedicated timing chips also exist for this purpose. The Tandy 1000 used an 8245A timing chip designed specifically to produce the clock signals an 8088 [[processor]] requires, and it contained both an unused internal oscillator and a switch to select between that and an external clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-32" title="EEVblog #32 - Tandy 1000 Retro Computer time!">32</a></sup> Programmable logic supplies clocks too — an [[fpga|FPGA]] on a spectrum analyser generates the clocks and control signals that drive the surrounding hardware<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup>, and an FPGA's flip-flop-and-clock architecture is a large part of what distinguishes it from a CPLD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> A microcontroller can be used as a clock source in its own right, generating clocks and [[pwm|PWM]] signals — for example, driving a charge-pump [[voltage]] doubler that turns a 3 V input into 6 V out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup> Instruments and lab boards frequently provide clocks as well: a mixed-signal instrument can synthesise a digital pattern with defined [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]], [[frequency]] and idle time<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup>, a logic trainer offers a clock switchable from 1 Hz through 1 kHz to 100 kHz, plus a negative-going clock for dual polarity work<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1463" title="EEVblog 1463 - Mailbag">1463</a></sup>, and manual single-stepping through a debounced pushbutton lets a counter be advanced by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1720" title="EEVblog 1720 - Mailbag: USSR Bonanza + Logic Training + $69 Logic Analyzer">1720</a></sup> A pattern generator loaded with 10101010 is functionally a clock output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qE-SwtYQM6E" title="Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope Pattern Generator">•</a></sup>

## Finding and identifying a clock on the bench

A clock is recognised on a scope by continuous periodic transitions where other lines are static or data-like.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup> The practical procedure on an unfamiliar board is to probe the accessible test points or header pins and confirm the expected activity: a clock line, and clocked data alongside it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup> Where a signal is present but not identifiable, adjacent behaviour settles it — one side of a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] showing a clean periodic waveform at 3.072 MHz next to a neighbour whose content changes on every acquisition identifies the pair as clock and data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> On a mixed-signal instrument it is worth spending a few minutes labelling channels, including labelling the clock as such, so that captures remain interpretable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>

Whether a clock is running at all is diagnostic on its own. A calculator board showing 10.1 kHz and roughly 203 kHz of clock activity is at least powered and attempting to work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup> Conversely, an absent clock localises a fault immediately: a DAC producing no output was traced to pin four, the clock line, having nothing on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> Clock behaviour can also be state-dependent — an idle machine issued a 400 microsecond burst of clock every 10 milliseconds and only ran a continuous clock once a program was executing, with a further deliberate 24 microsecond dead period inside the continuous run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> A clock that cannot be measured is not always a clock fault: a microcontroller with no supply voltage reaching it will show no clock, and the correct conclusion is a power problem rather than a dead micro.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

## Faults on the clock line

Because the clock is the one line whose absence stops everything, it warrants direct confirmation at the chip pin rather than at a convenient nearby point. In a case where data was demonstrably entering a serial chip and nothing was emerging, the clock proved to be reaching the pad but not the chip itself: pin three of a reworked part had a [[solder-joint|solder joint]] that made contact under probe pressure but not otherwise, and the signal disappeared when the probe touched lightly without added force.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> The lesson is that the clock pin is worth double- and triple-checking before diving into protocol analysis, which in that case would have cost considerable time and found nothing wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

Clock quality is a separate question from clock presence. A 25 MHz clock on a repaired NAS was captured with a low-[[inductance]] probe and shown to be badly degraded — attributed to deterioration of the output driver over time — yet the system still worked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1288" title="EEVblog #1288 - Synology NAS Dumpster Find! (REPAIR)">1288</a></sup> The same tension appeared with an [[oscilloscope]] reference clock whose spectrum, viewed on a spectrum analyser with a probe held near the single-ended clock output pin, showed spurious components at a 67.5 kHz offset that a proper clock would not have; the considered position is that this is "a pretty awful clock" that ought to be fixed on principle, while conceding that if no measurable difference appears in the instrument's performance the practical impact is academic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-699" title="EEVblog #699 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Jitter Fix Testing">699</a></sup>

Probing the clock is itself a source of artefacts. Long [[ground]] leads act as antennas and produce [[overshoot]] and [[ringing]] that is not in the circuit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>, while genuine ringing, undershoot and ground-bounce glitches on a clock edge only become believable when high-fidelity probing technique is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Coupling from the clock is also a common explanation for glitches seen on neighbouring lines during a poorly-grounded capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

## Layout and emissions

Clock routing is one of the constraint-driven parts of [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]]. On a bus, all the clock lines must reach every chip in the chain, which in practice means planning a clear routing channel and dropping vias rather than letting other traces consume the space.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Length matching is applied to a clock together with the data lines it accompanies — for example nine matched traces for eight data lines plus the clock — and excessive [[via]]-hopping between layers within such a group is poor practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Routing a clock too far from the registers it drives, or through additional gates and indirect [[pcb|PCB]] paths, is where [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]] begins to violate timing constraints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> Where a single clock line has to drive a heavy load, adding a buffer stage — a buffer chip, or two inverters in series — offloads the driving source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

For emissions, the fundamental clock rate is not the figure that matters. A computer running a 6 MHz main clock is not fast by modern standards, but radiated emissions are governed by the edge rate of the signal rather than its repetition frequency; even a 1 Hz clock can radiate strongly if its edges are fast enough.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Near-field magnetic probes pick up the loop currents from clocks and other signals circulating across the whole board and back to the supply, not merely the field around the crystal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup> [[bypass-capacitor|Bypass capacitor]] removal elsewhere on a board can visibly upset the clock at the point being probed, which may be exactly the effect of interest or may be unwanted contamination depending on the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Schematic and symbol practice

Clock pins benefit from deliberate placement in [[schematic]] symbols. Differential clock pins should be repositioned so that clock positive can run off to a net and clock negative straight down to ground without a wire crossing the whole symbol.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> More generally, arranging a serial part's pins as data in, data clock and data out produces a compact symbol from which clock and data wires flow naturally to the neighbouring components, saving effort later and producing a more presentable drawing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup>
