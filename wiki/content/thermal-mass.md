Thermal mass is the quantity of heat a physical object stores for a given rise in temperature, and it governs almost every thermal transient encountered on the bench. A large mass resists being heated and, once hot, resists cooling; a small one changes temperature almost as fast as its surroundings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VVOi2dbtxC0" title="EEVacademy #6 - PID Controllers Explained">•</a></sup> In electronics work it matters most in [[soldering]] and rework, where the mass of the pad, the copper attached to it, the component body and the board itself sets how much heat must be delivered and how fast.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup>

## Where the mass lives on a board

The heat sunk out of a joint comes from the whole thermally connected structure, not just the pad. Large [[ground]] planes, heavy copper pours and big wire-termination pads pull heat away from the tip and slow the joint to melting point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> A power package with a [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] on its underside is a worse case than a plain small-outline part: the [[ground-plane|ground plane]] extends across the top of the board and continues on the bottom, so the pad and the leads have to be treated as two separate thermal problems, sometimes by cutting the leads and dealing with the thermal pad on its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Multilayer boards are worse still, because the pad ties into internal copper as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> A two-layer board with only a pin or two touching a small area of plane presents little mass and can be reworked with hot air without a preheater.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

Board mass also shows up in processes other than [[hand-soldering|hand soldering]]. In reflow, the profile depends on the number of thermal reliefs, the amount of copper and the mass of the parts themselves, and varies enough between boards and ovens that it has to be found by experiment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> With a hot air gun, the board underneath has to come up to temperature before the joints will flow, which takes time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup> Assembly lines carry the same spread: boards leaving an SMT process may mix small parts with large thermal mass items such as heavy connectors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>

Very large assemblies are the limiting case. A thick multilayer substrate carrying a ceramic module is about as much mass as a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] ever presents, and it heats slowly enough that sitting it directly on a hot plate rather than above it changes only the time taken, not the temperature reached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OZb-mB3X-30" title="Abandon Lab! - IBM TCM Part 2">•</a></sup> This is what bottom-side preheaters exist for: bringing large-thermal-mass boards and BGAs up to an intermediate temperature, for instance 200 °C, so the hot air gun only has to supply the remainder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OZb-mB3X-30" title="Abandon Lab! - IBM TCM Part 2">•</a></sup>

## Tip mass and soldering iron performance

The tip of a [[soldering-iron|soldering iron]] is a thermal reservoir. The initial surge of heat into a joint comes from heat already stored in the tip, not from the element; only once the tip has cooled does the sensor register the drop and call for power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> A thicker, larger tip stores more and is the better choice for anything but fine work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> Manufacturers sell this difference directly: high-performance and ultra-performance tips differ from standard ones only by extra metal inside, visible as a physical bulge, and they retain heat so the temperature dips less when the tip is applied to a joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> Tip mass is not the same thing as element power, and the two should not be conflated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup>

Construction affects how quickly stored heat is replenished. Where the element is a separate part making imperfect contact with the tip, the extra [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] introduces lag between the element heating and the tip getting hot — unhelpful when a large ground plane or similar mass is drawing heat away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup>

Every iron dips in temperature under load; the question is how far and for how long.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> An iron with very little tip mass drops severely, and takes a long time to recover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup> At the extreme, a very thin fine-point tip has effectively no thermal mass, which combined with a low power budget of a few watts rules out any substantial component and forces a set temperature as high as 450 °C simply to overcome the drop that will certainly occur.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup> A calibrator touched by a tip in free air reads the no-load, zero-thermal-mass temperature; the reading falls as soon as the tip meets a joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-znYVJGfHj9Q" title="EEVblog Meetup Highlights">•</a></sup> The same absence of load explains slow cooling in setback mode, where there is nothing for the tip to dump heat into.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>

The practical consequence is that iron selection is a match to the work. A low-capacity portable unit can be entirely adequate for small, low-thermal-mass work such as mobile phone boards while being unsuitable for heavy gauge joints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5vbg8QEZXfY" title="iFixit FixHub Soldering Iron Review EXPLAINED + More Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup> Soldering to a [[heat-sink|heat sink]] clip is a mild case; soldering to a massive copper wire or block is a different problem entirely, and a low-thermal-mass junction masks the difference between stations that would otherwise be obvious.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup> The usual response to a known high-mass joint is to raise the set temperature somewhat — moving from around 310 °C to 330 °C for a part with a large thermal pad underneath, for example — rather than to wait longer at a temperature that will never get there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup> Where the tip is simply too small for the mass involved, no temperature setting fixes it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

## Hot air, desoldering and low-melt alloys

Hot air stations are sized to the mass they must heat. A low-capacity hobbyist unit suits small boards and simple low-thermal-mass components, while a high-[[airflow]] unit is needed for larger work; the difference only becomes visible on genuinely massive parts, since on a moderate chip two stations at the same temperature and indicated airflow can take a similar time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1659" title="EEVblog 1659 - Quick 861 Pro Hot Air Station REVIEW">1659</a></sup> Because temperature and airflow requirements track the mass of the specific part, production rework benefits from stored presets chosen per component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup>

For [[desoldering]], the correct temperature depends on the mass of the pads, the mass of the components — transformers, large inductors, relays — and the thermal recovery of the gun and nozzle together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup> [[bga|BGA]] memory devices with little mass attached can be reflowed in place without a preheater underneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup>

Low-melting-point alloys work by keeping a joint molten long enough to lift a part. The amount of mass held molten determines how long the working window lasts and how hard it is to sustain: a small quad flat pack is much easier than a large one, and on a large device the [[solder]] can remain liquid for a noticeable time after heating stops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup>

## Thermal mass used deliberately

Mass is often added on purpose. [[thermocouple|Thermocouple]] cold-junction compensation depends on holding both junctions at one temperature, and a design can achieve this isothermal block with large terminal studs and large pads that raise the thermal mass of the whole junction assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Thermoelectric generators require one side of the module to be anchored: without a heat sink providing mass on one face, both plates equalise and the output collapses, so the heat sink effectively pins one plate to ambient because it takes considerable energy to warm it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> A diecast alloy shell can be chosen for thermal mass reasons in an instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-989" title="EEVblog #989 - FLIR ETS320 Thermal Camera Teardown">989</a></sup> Extra solid metal is sometimes added inside a module purely to supply mass alongside a heat sink path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> Solder deliberately left on an exposed pad increases the mass and lowers the thermal resistance through to the pad on the opposite side of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-463" title="EEVblog #463 - More Dumpster Diving">463</a></sup>

Mass is also exploited for measurement fidelity. Instrumenting a reflow oven with a reference board of the same thermal mass as the work makes an independent meter track the controller's reading closely; a thermocouple left in free air, with no mass attached to it, reads differently from one bonded to the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>

## Consequences outside soldering

Thermal mass sets the dynamics of any [[temperature-control|temperature control]] loop. An oven takes a long time to heat because of its mass, and that delay lets the integral term of a PID controller accumulate a large value and drive the system past its set point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VVOi2dbtxC0" title="EEVacademy #6 - PID Controllers Explained">•</a></sup> Conversely, a bare chip in a moving airstream has so little mass that its temperature tracks the chamber essentially in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup> Buildings behave like the oven: concrete and steel retain heat, so a lab that peaks at 28–29 °C on a hot day only falls to about 25 °C overnight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k7FlF0ON1VM" title="Carbon Dioxide CO2 Levels in the Lab?">•</a></sup>

Stored heat can be the point of a system rather than a nuisance. A hot water tank holds usable energy in the thermal mass of the water, which survives a power failure for potentially days and allows the heating element to run at low peak power — a heat pump unit drawing 1 kW for a few hours to charge a 315 L tank, instead of the large instantaneous draw of an instantaneous heater.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pXtSybs9QRs" title="Instant Electric Hot Water Heater vs Heat Pump Hot Water Tank System">•</a></sup>

## Manufacturing artefacts

Differences in thermal mass leave visible marks on finished boards. Hot-air-levelled tin builds up unevenly over large copper areas, so large thermal masses show globs and an uneven surface while narrow traces come out comparatively smooth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-557" title="EEVblog #557 - Retro Sinclair ZX Spectrum Computer Teardown">557</a></sup> Wave or flow soldering can fail selectively on the higher-mass joints: on a board where most parts wetted correctly, the heavier items showed no flow-through at all, a latent defect that sat in the instrument for decades.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The same principle applies to how much copper a joint has behind it — a four-terminal [[current-shunt|current shunt]] with heavy traces and vias attached carries substantial mass at the joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup>

Component packaging carries the same logic. A regulator supplied with a thin tab has less thermal mass than a properly built device with a normal-thickness tab, and thinning the tab is a real reduction in the part's ability to handle heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>
