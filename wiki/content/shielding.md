Shielding is the use of conductive material -- cans, plates, cases, foil, braid, [[ground]] planes, plated case interiors -- to block electromagnetic coupling between a circuit and everything around it. It serves two separate purposes that are frequently confused: keeping outside interference out of a sensitive circuit, and keeping a noisy circuit's own emissions in so the product passes EMC requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> Both jobs can appear in one instrument, and a shield fitted for one reason does not automatically do the other well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>

## Two directions, two problems

A shield placed to protect a circuit is solving a susceptibility problem; a shield placed to contain radiation is solving an emissions problem, and the design decisions differ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> In an amplifier, a shield over the switching supply exists mainly to keep [[switching-noise|switching noise]] out of the analog section, with EMI compliance as a secondary motive -- which is why the unit can be powered up safely on the bench with that shield removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> A small can over a modem chip with nothing but copper on its underside is doing little for the circuit itself and is most plausibly there to satisfy an EMC requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup>

Whether a circuit needs shielding for immunity at all depends on its [[impedance]]. A low-impedance node -- a 50 ohm driven output, for instance -- is hard to disturb, because the interfering sources coupling into it are high impedance and cannot force meaningful signal onto it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Such a circuit may still need shielding to stop it radiating, but that is the other problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Conversely, high-impedance and low-[[current]] front ends are where shielding becomes decisive: down at nanoamp levels, an [[led|LED]] stuck into the middle of an input [[connector]] produced 1.1 nA of generated current, and simply bringing a hand near the input increased the pickup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

## Internal shielding and crosstalk

Much shielding inside instruments is not aimed at the outside world at all but at channel-to-channel and section-to-section coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> In a multi-channel [[arbitrary-waveform-generator|arbitrary waveform generator]], the shielded channels are shielded because they swing large square waves at high [[voltage]] into a low impedance and would otherwise couple into the physically adjacent channel; the coupling works in both directions, so the neighbour on the other side needs treatment too.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Leaving the remaining channels unshielded is a deliberate cost-cutting decision justified by their low-impedance nature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>

RF instruments carry this furthest. Critical RF blocks -- preamps, mixers -- are physically separated and enclosed in machined aluminium blockwork, and the same reasoning that demands physical shielding also demands local regulation per section, because crosstalk can travel by way of a shared supply rail as easily as through the air.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> Wireless microphone hardware separates and shields each section individually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup> One [[signal-generator|signal generator]] goes as far as shielding inside the shield, with an additional tag bonding the inner enclosure to the upper plate beyond what the screw holes already provide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> A high-voltage differential probe is shielded almost throughout, with separate cans for the input and output sections that must be desoldered to reach the front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> A modular test set built around RF cans was partitioned from the outset so that modules could be shielded and still be accessed, serviced, tweaked in production, and recalibrated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1256" title="EEVblog #1256 - GORGEOUS Mystery Test Gear Teardown!">1256</a></sup>

Where internal shielding is absent, the failure is concrete: unshielded superregenerative receivers interfere with each other badly enough that the standard workaround is to physically separate them, a problem of system design rather than anything inherent to the regenerative circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup> Not all internal shields are equally effective -- a small can mounted to the board over a level-control section containing a couple of trimmer pots and a trimmer [[capacitor]] is of doubtful benefit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> In one bench [[multimeter]] the shield is a mains-earth-referenced trace routed to snake around the board, isolating the mains-referenced portion from the measurement circuitry, rather than a can over the digital section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup>

## Ground planes and the PCB as a shield

Copper planes inside the board are themselves shielding. A solid [[ground-plane|ground plane]] and power plane provide shielding against magnetic and electric fields, on top of the lower [[inductance]] and smaller loop area they give the return path -- a measurable EMC difference between a two-layer and a four-layer version of the same circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Even where no metal can is fitted, inner-layer ground plane under the main circuitry provides some protection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup> Signal traces sandwiched between top and bottom ground planes are shielded by them, and [[via-stitching|via stitching]] around the board perimeter closes the edges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1315" title="EEVblog #1315 - Ultrasound Probe Extreme Teardown!">1315</a></sup> Taken to its conclusion, plating the board edges and connecting them to the ground plane forms a shielded box with all signal layers enclosed inside, so signals cannot radiate out of the sides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup>

At extreme sensitivity the [[pcb|PCB]] itself becomes part of the shielding problem alongside guarding, dielectric material selection, and [[via]] fencing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

## Cables and connectors

In a screened twisted pair, the screen keeps out electrostatic [[noise]] -- interference capacitively coupled from nearby conductors such as a mains wire -- while the balanced twisted pair rejects magnetically coupled noise from transformers and current-carrying conductors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-616" title="EEVblog #616 - How Microphone Phantom Powering Works">616</a></sup> The two mechanisms need different countermeasures, and the cable construction addresses both.

Good video cable is layered for the same reason: an outer braid, a foil beneath it encasing the whole bundle, and individually shielded inner pairs to stop crosstalk between the internal signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1028" title="EEVblog #1028 - What&#x27;s All This PC/104 Stuff Anyhow?">1028</a></sup> For critical low-leakage, low-[[current-measurement|current measurement]] the connector of choice is triaxial [[coaxial-cable|coax]], which adds a guard conductor between the inner and the outer shield.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup> [[ribbon-cable|Ribbon cable]] and flat flex runs are shielded too, either with foil wrapped around the cable or with a dedicated shield plate covering the run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup>

## Low-level measurement

Precision low-current and low-[[resistance]] work is where shielding becomes the dominant design constraint. Precision high-value resistors are mounted in double-shielded boxes, isolated with minimum [[capacitance]] to their surroundings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup> In such a fixture the outer case is connected to [[mains-earth|mains earth]] while the instrument input is not earth-referenced; the internal ground can be shielded as a second layer if measurements go low enough to warrant it, though double shielding is not always necessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup> A resistance calibrator puts its mains [[transformer]] inside its own shielded box, and the precision reference resistors and [[relay]] switching are shielded several layers deep.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup>

The payoff is visible on a [[noise-floor|noise floor]]. A properly shielded analyser with a [[50-ohm-termination|50 ohm terminator]] on its front end shows no 50 Hz pickup whatsoever in a laboratory saturated with mains-[[frequency]] fields; connecting a practical circuit instead almost always brings some 50 Hz back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup>

A precision low-frequency instrument built around a [[crt|CRT]] illustrates the complete approach: the front panel that appears to be plastic is nickel-screened and conductive, bonded through a metal tab to the chassis, the CRT itself is shielded, and the front panel mesh exists to stop the CRT scan frequencies escaping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup> The reason is specific -- the scan frequency of tens of kilohertz falls in the middle of the instrument's DC to 100 kHz measurement range, so any leakage lands directly in the measurement band.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup>

## Making the shield actually work

A shield is only as good as its connection. In a handheld meter the arrangement is typically a metal shield or foil insert in the case rear plus a spring contact that grounds it to the board; the absence of that spring and foil is a straightforward place to save cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup> Conductive sponges serve the same function of bonding a board-level shield to case-mounted screening.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup> A shield with no visible spring or contact point is suspect, and unbonded shielding is a plausible explanation for pickup observed on a sensitive ohms range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Nickel screening on interior plastics only works when it reaches a chassis connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8h-5cx-qiqo" title="Dumpster Diving Acer Aspire E1 Laptop Teardown">•</a></sup>

Transformer shielding is a similar matter of optimisation -- getting the shield placement right for common-mode noise performance is an iterative process, far easier with a supplier close enough to visit and adjust in person.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 5 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

A simple bench test of an instrument's shielding is to place an active mobile phone call beside it and watch whether the reading moves at all; it applies to a wide range of instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup>

## Where shielding is spent and where it is skipped

Shielding costs metalwork, mass, and assembly time, so its presence and absence both encode design decisions. Heft is a rough proxy: several kilograms of instrument implies significant shielding and possibly a die-cast RF front end, while a suspiciously light instrument has little shielding inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup>

Common economies include leaving can footprints on the PCB and populating none of them, having designed in the ability to shield and then deciding it is unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1596" title="EEVblog 1596 - NEW Digilent Analog Discovery Pro ADP2230 TEARDOWN">1596</a></sup> A front end may be only partially shielded because heatsinks must protrude through the can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup> Two variants of the same [[oscilloscope]] family can differ in that the higher model encloses the [[power-supply|power supply]] while the lower one leaves it open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup> The bottom of a board may be left unshielded because it already sits hard against a die-cast case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup> A plastic cover over a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] and ADC is not a shield at all -- it is there to keep fan-driven air currents off the parts, with the actual shielding on the base of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Cheap meters omit internal shielding entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

At the other extreme, retro computers and consoles carry substantial soldered-down shield cans that must be desoldered to get inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-491" title="EEVblog #491 - Nintendo 64 Game Console Teardown">491</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> Spectrum analysers carry additional shielding blocks over individual subsystems, and mechanical accommodations -- a cutout in the main cover for a tall [[inductor]], then a plate over the cutout -- keep the enclosure intact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> Shielding also gets added after the fact: a bodged shield tacked down to a couple of pads over a chip is a retrofit for a problem found late.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup> Gaffer tape holding a shield in place is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1278" title="EEVblog #1278 - Mailbag">1278</a></sup>

Sealed applications shield for safety rather than performance. A parachute automatic activation device is enclosed in a fully shielded case with a shielding wire, so that a nearby radio transmitter cannot set off the pyrotechnic charge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-339" title="EEVblog #339 - Cypres Parachute AAD Teardown">339</a></sup>
