A crystal oscillator is a [[frequency]] source built around a slice of quartz that is driven into mechanical vibration and used as the frequency-determining element of an electronic oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-646" title="EEVblog #646 - Gravity Detection Using A Frequency Counter!">646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup> Inside a typical package is a circular sliver of quartz with an electrode on either side; applying a signal across the electrodes makes the disc vibrate, and that mechanical resonance sets the frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-646" title="EEVblog #646 - Gravity Detection Using A Frequency Counter!">646</a></sup> Almost any active chip needs an oscillator of some kind to run at all, and for most digital systems that oscillator is a quartz one, either a bare crystal working with circuitry inside the chip or a complete oscillator module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup> Quartz is what makes accurate frequency cheap: precise time measurement with a high-stability crystal oscillator is easy and inexpensive, which is why entire measurement architectures — the multi-slope integrating converters in bench multimeters among them — are built on timing rather than on precision analogue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup>

## Crystal versus oscillator module

The two words are routinely confused, and the distinction is real. A bare crystal — the classic HC49U can, or its surface-mount equivalents — is a passive two-terminal resonator that does nothing on its own and needs external circuitry to oscillate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-677" title="EEVblog #677 - Mailbag">677</a></sup> An oscillator module, by contrast, contains a crystal plus the sustaining amplifier and output stage, and drives out a finished clock signal directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-677" title="EEVblog #677 - Mailbag">677</a></sup> A typical module in a 14-pin DIP outline uses only four pins and carries small bumps on its underside to stand the metal case off the board so it cannot short exposed pads or vias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Package clues distinguish them in a teardown: a part with one large pad on the end is a bare crystal rather than an oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1336" title="EEVblog #1336 - DT71 LCR Tweezer Destructive Teardown + Lab Update">1336</a></sup>

Microcontrollers illustrate the split. Most contain an oscillator circuit and bring out two pins — the crystal connects across them, and the same pins can instead accept an external clock input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> A 4000-series CD4060 ripple counter is the same idea in discrete form: a built-in oscillator usable with either an RC network or an external crystal, feeding a binary divider chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Where a device has no crystal at all, an internal RC oscillator is usually doing the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup>

Oscillator modules can also hide a divider. A part marked 1 MHz may in fact contain an 8 MHz resonator divided by eight, with pin straps selecting the ratio, which means the real internal activity is eight times higher than the label suggests and can leak into ground planes and radiate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Small surface-mount oscillator modules are cheap enough that replacing a bulky crystal with one is a normal design move — a 3.579545 MHz oscillator in a 2.5 mm by 3 mm package runs well under thirty cents in volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup>

## Accuracy grades

Crystal oscillator accuracy is specified in parts per million rather than percent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1300" title="EEVblog #1300 - &quot;Parts Per&quot; Notation EXPLAINED">1300</a></sup> A plain XO sits in the region of 10 ppm, equivalently one part in 10⁵, with ordinary parts spanning roughly 10 to 100 ppm once the temperature range is taken into account.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup> Temperature-compensated types — TCXO, and the digitally compensated DTCXO in which performance is characterised at the factory and compensation values are programmed in — reach about an order of magnitude better, in the region of 0.5 to 5 ppm with around 1 ppm aging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup> Oven-controlled crystal oscillators, an old technology, hold a quartz crystal at a fixed elevated temperature so that ambient variation in the laboratory no longer matters, reaching roughly 0.1 to 0.01 ppm, or 10⁻⁷ over the span of a year.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup> Rubidium standards move into primary-standard territory at better than 10⁻⁹, one part per billion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup> Ovenised units can be very good over short intervals: one instrument oven oscillator specifies better than 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ over 24 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup>

The same hierarchy shows up as instrument options. Frequency counters and RF signal generators typically ship with an ordinary quartz or temperature-compensated oscillator, with an OCXO offered as an extra-cost module — a card-edge daughterboard plugging into the main board in one signal generator,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup> a plug-in module in a timer counter whose price was in the hundreds to a thousand dollars,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> and a temperature-compensated 10 MHz reference in another generator where the oven option cost a great deal more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> Where an oven module replaces a socketed crystal, the instructions matter; one such module carries the note "Please remove the crystal when you're installing this oscillator module."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> Aftermarket ovens exist for popular counters and improve accuracy and stability by a couple of orders of magnitude for a modest sum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> At the low end of the [[oscilloscope]] market the reference is deliberately ordinary: one scope generation moved from a 25 ppm standard [[time-base]] to 1.6 ppm,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-702" title="EEVblog #702 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Teardown">702</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> while cheaper models simply do without a precision crystal oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup>

An oven costs power and time. The heater in an oven module can draw 5 to 10 W, and a counter with an internal oven fitted draws around 33 W total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> Ovens are wrapped in hard-cell foam to insulate them against outside thermal shocks, and the assembly typically includes a heating coil, a [[temperature-sensor]] and a trim adjustment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1139" title="EEVblog #1139 - OCXO Oven Oscillator Repair">1139</a></sup> The crystal itself is nothing exotic — a regular quartz crystal of a particularly stable cut, held at temperature and insulated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1139" title="EEVblog #1139 - OCXO Oven Oscillator Repair">1139</a></sup> An oven indicator [[led]] may take fifteen minutes or so to signal stabilisation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> [[calibration]] practice reflects this: a precision temperature-controlled crystal oscillator is left powered up for 48 hours before its time base is measured, whereas for an ordinary crystal or RC time base, waiting more than an hour is wasted effort.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-424" title="EEVblog #424 - $3M Agilent Portable Calibration Lab Tour">424</a></sup>

## Drift, aging and environmental sensitivity

Quartz crystal oscillators drift, and not only with temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup> They carry a temperature specification, an aging figure describing how far they move per year, a [[voltage]] dependency, and sensitivity to physical shock and vibration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-646" title="EEVblog #646 - Gravity Detection Using A Frequency Counter!">646</a></sup> The drift is inherent to the quartz itself, arising from physics deep enough that the practical engineering treats it as a characteristic to be measured rather than explained.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup> Linear drift can be measured against a GPS-locked rubidium reference and corrected in software, flattening the curve — but a crystal oscillator is never actually flat over time, even at a perfectly fixed temperature held constant for days, weeks and months.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-457" title="EEVblog #457 - Oscillator Calibration Followup">457</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup> The drift characteristic of a quartz oscillator can also be reset by subjecting it to a suitable shock or vibration, a result Dave Jones established while working on ocean-bottom seismic recorders where stability in the region of 10⁻⁸ was required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-646" title="EEVblog #646 - Gravity Detection Using A Frequency Counter!">646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-61" title="EEVblog #61 - Crystal Oscillator Drift">61</a></sup>

Orientation matters too. Because the quartz element vibrates, its behaviour depends on how it is oriented relative to the gravitational field; rotating the piece of quartz changes the frequency, and flipping it fully over produces the largest change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-646" title="EEVblog #646 - Gravity Detection Using A Frequency Counter!">646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hE45DSUdD58" title="How Gravity Can Change Your Watch&#x27;s Time!">•</a></sup> The effect is typically in the order of 1 to 10 parts per billion, so an 11- or 12-digit counter is needed to see it at all, which is why it is not normally a problem in oscilloscopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JobJswHcnLE" title="eevBLAB #8 - New Tektronix AGO3000 Oscilloscope">•</a></sup> It is well known in the seismic industry, and it has a direct bench consequence: an oven oscillator should not be trimmed while sitting outside its instrument in a different orientation from the one it will be used in, and the reason is gravity rather than any thermal effect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JobJswHcnLE" title="eevBLAB #8 - New Tektronix AGO3000 Oscilloscope">•</a></sup> Shock mounting of quartz oscillators is a design discipline of its own, and materials evaluation for it has extended to cotton wool, foam, sorbothane and stranger candidates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup>

## Mechanical mounting and layout

A crystal is a mechanical part, and it fails like one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> Free-standing crystals flapping in the breeze are poor practice: catch the right vibrational mode and the part will shear off its leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> Well-built assemblies glue or solder the can down to the board — an epoxy adhesive on a shockproof emergency beacon,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup> cans soldered to the board specifically to prevent vibration issues,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-169" title="EEVblog #169 - Samson StudioDock 4i USB Monitor Speaker Review">169</a></sup> a glued-down 4 MHz crystal in a [[multimeter]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup> soldered-down crystals even in inexpensive pocket meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup> Rugged equipment that leaves a 32 kHz watch crystal unsecured is an outlier worth noting, since an unrestrained crystal can move and the leads can shear if the unit is dropped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup>

Layout practice for a bare crystal is short traces from the chip to the crystal, with the load capacitors returning to ground close by.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> There is generally no reason to fit a through-hole crystal on a board that is otherwise entirely surface mount.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Crystals also radiate: probing across one with a near-field loop and a spectrum display shows the fundamental plus harmonics — a 12.28 MHz part producing a clear peak near 36.9 MHz at three times the fundamental — which makes the crystal a natural first stop when baselining a board's emissions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup> Shielding is sometimes added specifically over the crystal region to keep digital hash out of adjacent analogue circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-964" title="EEVblog #964 - Mailbag">964</a></sup>

## Frequency multiplication and system clocking

Modern digital systems rarely run at the crystal frequency. A PLL or clock-management block multiplies a modest external reference up to the internal clock rate: an [[fpga]] running its logic at a couple of hundred megahertz from a 20 MHz external crystal,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> a 400 MHz application processor fed by something like a 20 MHz part,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> a 24 MHz crystal boosted to the 600 MHz a reader's processor requires,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup> a 34 MHz crystal multiplied by pin-strapped ratios to produce roughly 200 MHz of clock,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-491" title="EEVblog #491 - Nintendo 64 Game Console Teardown">491</a></sup> and a 25 MHz main crystal generating everything a [[function-generator]] needs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> The PLL, not the crystal, is often the weak link — a scope's 5 microsecond jitter problem was traced to the PLL generating the sample and FPGA clocks, with nothing wrong with the lower-frequency crystal itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-699" title="EEVblog #699 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Jitter Fix Testing">699</a></sup>

Certain frequencies recur for historical reasons. The 3.579545 MHz NTSC colorburst crystal was the cheapest and most common part available, salvaged out of televisions when crystals were expensive, and it turns up as a main clock in vintage computers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-633" title="EEVblog #633 - Mailbag">633</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup> The 32.768 kHz watch crystal is the standard real-time-clock element and appears alongside the main oscillator in devices needing timekeeping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> Odd values are common too — 4.9152 MHz, 12.288 MHz for audio sample-rate generation, 106.25 MHz driving a bench multimeter's timing, 74.1758 MHz likely custom-ordered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> Older machines derive everything from one crystal by division: 9 MHz divided by three, 15.667200 MHz halved for the system clock, 10.6445 MHz divided by six.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-414" title="EEVblog #414 - Apple Macintosh SE Teardown">414</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup>

## When a crystal is not warranted

Accuracy costs money, and not every application needs it. An RFID jamming card with a crystal fitted is over-specified when the internal RC oscillator of the [[microcontroller]] would have sufficed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-890" title="EEVblog #890 - ArmourCard Active RFID Jamming Teardown">890</a></sup> At the other extreme, cost-down manufacturing substitutes a ceramic resonator for a crystal to save a fraction of a cent, which is unacceptable on a high-speed [[usb]] device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 2 of 2 - Shanghai Special - Dodgy USB Hubs">12</a></sup> Super-regenerative receiver modules are not crystal controlled at all — they are hand-tuned LC tank circuits, with the frequency accuracy that implies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup> For general project work a crystal oscillator can simply be assumed to be close enough; needing a high-accuracy [[frequency-counter]] to verify it means the design already has requirements the engineer would know about.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

## Failure and troubleshooting

Because a crystal is a mechanical resonator, a dead crystal is a plausible cause of a dead microcontroller, and checking for a clock is a standard early step after checking supply rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Confirming oscillation at the expected frequency clears the crystal and points the investigation at the chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> Two cautions apply. First, identify which chip the crystal actually serves — replacing a crystal belonging to a multimeter's analogue front-end chipset makes no difference to a fault in the main processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup> Second, crystals in the classic cans are physically difficult to remove; hot air and dual irons can both fail to release one, and forcing it lifts the pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> A crystal circuit that shows no oscillation at all is a legitimate suspect during instrument repair, though accessible marked test points would usually find the real fault faster.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup>
