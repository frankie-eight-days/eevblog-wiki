Continuity testing is the practice of confirming that two points in a circuit are electrically connected, normally using the dedicated continuity function of a [[multimeter]], which sounds a buzzer when the [[resistance]] between the probes falls below a threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Virtually every multimeter carries the function, marked with a buzzer or sound symbol on the [[range-switch]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> It is the one function almost every user reaches for regardless of field, and it is the primary tool for finding breaks in cables, cracked [[pcb]] traces, and connections that are not where a drawing says they should be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

Beyond fault finding, the beep is a navigation aid: dragging one probe across a board while the other sits on a known net reveals where that net travels, which makes the function central to both repair and [[reverse-engineering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup>

## Threshold and test current

A continuity tester is an ohmmeter with a [[comparator]] on its output, and where the comparator trips matters. [[firmware]] development on the BM786 illustrates the range of choice available: the shipping firmware switched the buzzer on at roughly 270 ohms and required hysteresis out to about 300 ohms to release, while revised firmware moved the turn-on point to approximately 40 ohms with turn-off at exactly 100 ohms, a change requested by users who wanted the buzzer to indicate a genuinely low resistance rather than any conduction path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PSdF0KNsiaY" title="BM786 Continuity Firmware Testing">•</a></sup>

Threshold changes are not free. The revised BM786 firmware traded detection rate for the lower threshold, stretching what should have been a response on the order of 100 microseconds into an effective 100 millisecond pulse, and degrading the latching behaviour in the process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PSdF0KNsiaY" title="BM786 Continuity Firmware Testing">•</a></sup>

Production test equipment uses far heavier stimulus than a handheld meter. Bare-board flying probe testers pass on the order of 150 milliamps through each net during the continuity portion of the test, then run a separate isolation test at a specified [[voltage]] such as 64 volts to check that nets which should not connect do not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup>

## Response speed and latching

The single most important quality metric for the function is how reliably it catches a brief contact. A good tester responds instantly on a fast swipe and latches the beep so the indication survives the moment of contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> A 500 microsecond response time is enough to pick up essentially everything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup> Response speed is worth checking before buying a meter at all, because performance varies widely across otherwise similar instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 1 of 2 - Meterman 37XR Multimeter review">6</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

Failures cluster at the slow end. A meter that only registers eventually, with no latching and no buzzer at all, cannot compete with any serious instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> A wrist-worn multimeter proved useless as a continuity tester.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1706" title="EEVblog 1706 - K8 Wrist Worn Multimeter REVIEW">1706</a></sup> A pocket meter missed contact completely on a bench swipe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 1 of 2 - Meterman 37XR Multimeter review">6</a></sup> The Hioki handheld was "slow as a wet week" in continuity mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> Even a bench multimeter can disappoint, with [[auto-ranging|autoranging]] too slow for everyday continuity work in its normal measurement mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup> Middling performers register but drop enough events to be irritating: a Gossen unit was reasonably quick yet missed a noticeable fraction of contacts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup> and an ANENG unit missed contacts even with gold-plated probes, rating as barely adequate despite being loud and having a visual indicator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> A retro Fluke was not the fastest available but was loud enough to be usable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup>

Good implementations latch and respond instantly together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> Even a highly regarded meter such as the Fluke 87V, generally very quick, can be made to miss occasionally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup>

Latching is itself a design trade-off rather than an unambiguous good, since latching and pulse stretching interact with what is being probed, and large [[capacitor]] values can fool a latched tester.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1671" title="EEVblog 1671 - Beware of Multimeter Continuity Latching">1671</a></sup> Non-latching behaviour produces the characteristic "itchy and scratchy" beeping on a dragged probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PSdF0KNsiaY" title="BM786 Continuity Firmware Testing">•</a></sup> Loudness matters alongside speed, and it depends on mechanical packaging: one pocket meter's buzzer was muffled by the thick TPU rubber case surrounding it and became noticeably louder with the case removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup> A visual alert alongside the buzzer is useful, though it is often left unlatched even when the audible indication latches.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1424" title="EEVblog 1424 - Fluke 23 Multimeter Repair">1424</a></sup>

Placing continuity on its own switch position rather than behind a soft button on a shared ohms position is a meaningful ergonomic gain, since it removes a button press from an operation performed constantly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup>

## Probes and contact resistance

The tester is only as good as the metal touching the board. Gold-plated probe tips make measurably better contact than the nickel-alloy plating used on cheaper leads: a gold-plated probe resting under nothing but its own weight will hold continuous contact, while the industry-standard non-gold-plated leads may fail to register even when pressed with considerable force.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uV9mW0rpRxg" title="eevBLAB #33 - Why Gold Plated Probes Matter">•</a></sup> The difference shows up in ordinary probing as well as in continuity testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uV9mW0rpRxg" title="eevBLAB #33 - Why Gold Plated Probes Matter">•</a></sup> Swapping to gold probes visibly improves the apparent continuity performance of a meter that seemed poor with its supplied leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup>

Tip geometry matters independently of plating. Round, blunt tips are a persistent annoyance where sharp points are needed to reach small pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup> Gold plating does not rescue a slow comparator, however: a sufficiently poor tester will still miss contacts made with good probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup>

A meter should be verified as working before it is trusted for a result, by shorting the probes and confirming the buzzer sounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup>

## Fault finding and repair

Buzzing out connections is the standard method for locating breaks in corroded or damaged boards, and on a badly corroded board every single suspect trace has to be checked individually because visible copper says nothing about whether the conductor is intact underneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Gbn_51IoJiM" title="Amiga 2000 Teardown &amp; Power Up">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup> On an Acorn Archimedes with [[electrolyte]] damage, common pins on adjacent ROMs that were unambiguously supposed to be connected showed no continuity at all, and multiple breaks existed in the affected traces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup> Repair of such a board requires buzzing out every connection and repairing the faulty ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup> Corrosion travels further than it appears to, reaching vias well away from the visible damage, which widens the set of nets that must be checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup>

The test is also the quickest way to confirm or eliminate mechanical failures. A suspected micro-crack in a [[solder-joint]] can be checked by buzzing from one side of the joint to the other, a fast enough test to run as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-afErPINq8qc" title="Mini Arcade Game Console Repair OOPSIE!">•</a></sup> Conversely, the test can exonerate a suspect: a crack apparently visible on a wide trace was shown to be intact, and a crack of that size on a trace of that width is implausible in the first place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m3sQHx5aMmU" title="BM786 Switch Issue - Part 2">•</a></sup> A track that looked black and rotted turned out on measurement to be exactly the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup>

Where the far side of a board is inaccessible, buzzing a pin out simply to establish whether it goes anywhere at all is a useful first move.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup> The same probing can map the row-and-column structure of a contact array.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup>

Distinguishing a short from a break is part of the same workflow. Probing across capacitors that sit on power rails finds shorted parts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> and a reading of 12K to ground on a suspect node establishes that the node is not shorted, redirecting the diagnosis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> A surface-mount [[inductor]] can be checked for continuity through itself and separately for a short to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1299" title="EEVblog #1299 - Dumpster Laptop REPAIR">1299</a></sup> In a mains filter with no internal fusing, an open between the input and output pins localises the fault to one side of the common-mode choke.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

Flexible circuits are a recurring offender: on a camera repair, neither the [[battery]] positive nor the battery negative path had continuity through the ribbon, and the ribbon had to be reinstalled and re-tested after each intervention to confirm a repair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup> Continuity checks also catch documentation errors: a [[service-manual]] claiming a signal appeared on pin 12 was contradicted by a measurement showing continuity to nothing there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup>

## Reverse engineering and pinout determination

Tracing an unknown board is largely a continuity exercise, and it is where response speed pays off most directly, because the technique is to hold one probe on a known point and drag the other along IC pins and other candidate points looking for the beep.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Inner-layer vias that disappear from top and bottom layer photographs can only be resolved this way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Deduction narrows the search first: a trace that vanishes under a package must emerge somewhere, so buzzing the candidate pins confirms it, as when an op-amp pin 3 was shown to connect to pin 6.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> Where components obscure the copper, desoldering them to look underneath is often faster than randomly buzzing pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup>

Test points removed from a production board can still be reached at the bed-of-nails pads, and scraping a continuity tester along a suspected quadrant of pads identifies the one that connects to a known pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup> Failure to buzz out a connection that should exist is itself diagnostic — an unbuzzable ground pin traced back not to a broken link but to an incorrect pin-out in the [[schematic]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XUyjRm1Upjs" title="That moment you realise the Schematic &amp; PCB don&#x27;t match">•</a></sup>

Connector polarity on unlabelled equipment is determined the same way: buzzing a DC jack's pins to the grounded chassis identifies which is negative,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup> buzzing between the internal battery negative and the jack's centre tab revealed a centre-negative input on a device where centre-positive would have been assumed,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-940" title="EEVblog #940 - Mailbag">940</a></sup> and the ground pin of an audio jack is identified by continuity to the chassis or an external metal part, which is usually common ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

The technique scales beyond the bench. Aircraft panels reused in a 747 simulator had to be continuity-tested from the rear before re-pinning their connectors, because wiring diagrams vary between aircraft, fleets, and panel numbers, and the panels installed were not the ones originally fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1268" title="EEVblog #1268 - DIY Boeing 747 Cockpit Simulator Full Tour">1268</a></sup> At the other extreme, a suspected connection between an [[fpga]] and downstream hardware was verified by writing a dedicated project that drove data onto every pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup>

## Verification before power-up

Continuity checks belong before power is applied, not only after something fails. On a freshly hand-assembled board, buzzing out the power rails to confirm nothing is shorted is the last step before first power-up, with the input capacitor and each regulated rail checked in turn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> The same discipline applies to construction: on a home-made EMC probe, the inner conductor must be confirmed shorted to the shield at the far end before the shield gap is cut.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1178" title="EEVblog #1178 - Build a $10 DIY EMC Probe">1178</a></sup> When identifying which screw terminal carries which supply rail, continuity from the terminal back to the known wire resolves it without guesswork.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup>

Continuity is equally the go/no-go test for conductive-ink and other non-standard conductors, where an unbroken-looking line can still fail because the trace is not thick enough to carry a path end to end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup>

## Related instrument features

Continuity appears alongside [[diode]] test, resistance, and [[capacitance]] in the standard function set of general-purpose handheld meters,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> and it is included on specialist instruments such as SMD tweezer-format LCR meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> Meters that break capacitance, [[frequency]], and ohms onto separate switch positions but leave continuity multiplexed miss an opportunity, since continuity is the function most deserving of its own position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup>
