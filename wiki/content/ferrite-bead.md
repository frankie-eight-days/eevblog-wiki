A ferrite bead is a small block of ferrite placed around a conductor so that the conductor becomes a lossy [[inductor]] at high [[frequency]]. A wire with a bead threaded onto it is "essentially an inductor"; the ferrite contains the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] that surrounds any [[current]]-carrying conductor and makes that [[inductance]] more effective than the bare wire alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> Its purpose in a product is almost always electromagnetic compatibility — knocking the sharp edges off fast switching [[waveform|waveforms]] and stopping high-frequency energy from travelling along power rails, signal lines and cables where it would be radiated or conducted out of the box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1021" title="EEVblog #1021 - Retro Radio Shack TRS-80 Color Computer">1021</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup>

## Impedance rather than inductance

Beads are specified by an [[impedance]] at a stated frequency rather than by an inductance value, and packages are sometimes marked with that figure — a bead may carry a number such as 200 ohms which is its impedance in quotes at frequency, not a DC [[resistance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup> Values in the low hundreds of ohms are typical; a bead in a consumer audio product's power-key line is a 220 ohm part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> Because the DC path through a bead is essentially a short, a bead sitting in a signal line is transparent to the function of the circuit until something makes it open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-884" title="EEVblog #884 - EEVBlog BM235 Multimeter REPAIR">884</a></sup>

Where more attenuation than a single bead can give is needed, the usual escalation is more turns: winding the conductor through the ferrite several times, or moving to a wound coil, is the standard way of making the same core more effective.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> Cable assemblies take the opposite approach and simply use many discrete beads along the run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup>

## Where they get fitted

Power entry is the most common location. Beads appear on IEC mains inputs alongside the filter can and the [[voltage]] selector,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup> on external DC input jacks for [[noise]] suppression to help a product pass CE compliance,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-234" title="EEVblog #234 - Agilent U1733C LCR Meter Teardown">234</a></sup> and on the wiring leaving a switch-mode supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup> A DC-DC converter module quoting less than 75 mV of [[ripple]] and noise is not quiet by bench standards; a ferrite bead on the output, followed by a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]], is the way to bring that down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

They are equally common on individual leads and traces: on the [[cathode]] of a rectifier [[diode]] to take the edge off the waveform,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> on the lead of a regulator or diode inside a laptop,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-639" title="EEVblog #639 - IBM L40SX Retro Laptop Teardown">639</a></sup> on the negative sense line of a bench supply but pointedly not the positive one,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup> on a BNC input where DC is also being fed up the [[coaxial-cable|coax]] to a masthead amplifier,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-956" title="EEVblog #956 - Countersurveillance Monitor Teardown">956</a></sup> and around the [[ground]] [[input-jack|input jack]] wiring of a handheld meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup>

Cabling and flexible interconnect attract the largest ferrites — across an [[lcd|LCD]] [[ribbon-cable|ribbon cable]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-985" title="EEVblog #985 - Siglent SDS1202X-E Oscilloscope Teardown">985</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1261" title="EEVblog #1261 - Dumpster Diving 55&quot; LCD TV">1261</a></sup> on the ribbon cables of a front panel,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1513" title="EEVblog 1513 - Dumpster Tossers Have No Shame!">1513</a></sup> on every channel of an ultrasound probe's cable bundle,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1315" title="EEVblog #1315 - Ultrasound Probe Extreme Teardown!">1315</a></sup> on the cables of a plasma television so the product meets its EMI limits,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup> and on flat-flex, where a purpose-made flat-flex ferrite is an uncommon and worth-keeping salvage part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-488" title="EEVblog #488 - Dumpster Dive Parts Salvage">488</a></sup>

The practice is old. A 1980s home computer used ferrite beads to take the edge off 5 V TTL running around a double-sided [[pcb|PCB]] with no [[ground-plane|ground plane]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1021" title="EEVblog #1021 - Retro Radio Shack TRS-80 Color Computer">1021</a></sup> a contemporary 16-bit machine carried beads and ceramic capacitors on all its external ports,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> and one minicomputer-era board carried what appear to be beads on the leads of every single [[ceramic-capacitor|ceramic capacitor]] on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1404" title="EEVblog 1404 - The Amazing HP 9845B Computer">1404</a></sup>

## Layout and integration

On a board, the bead is normally placed immediately adjacent to the [[decoupling-capacitor|decoupling capacitor]] it works with; crowding a bead and a [[capacitor]] together where space is tight is ordinary [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]] practice, even when the board in question had room to spread them out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> Beads are also placed in the ground path, deliberately separating one ground region from another — for example a current-input ground from the system ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

Mechanical integration can go much further. One projector [[power-supply|power supply]] had mounting posts and bead cradles moulded into the fan assembly, sized around a known number of turns, so the wiring loom and its ferrites were designed as one piece — a level of integration design effort that is unusual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1546" title="EEVblog 1546 - INSANE $30k 4K Panoramic DLP Projector TEARDOWN">1546</a></sup>

## Choosing the wrong value

A bead is not a free component. Because it is an inductance, it can resonate with the [[capacitance]] around it, and getting the value wrong can create the emissions problem it was fitted to prevent. In one bench [[multimeter]], beads placed between the current-input ground and the system ground were the wrong value and resonated near 13 MHz against the ground planes' capacitance, causing both conducted and coupling-mode failures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup> The fix was not extra [[shielding]] and not extra components: the internal shielding was unchanged, the beads on the voltage inputs were untouched, and the correction amounted to changing bead values — and a [[resistor]] — on existing production boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup> Diagnosing this kind of interaction takes substantial engineering effort, and the same mechanism is known to occur when bypass capacitors resonate with trace inductance at a particular clock frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

The general lesson is that beads are a mitigation to be verified, not a talisman. Two beads sitting loose inside a rechargeable toothbrush around a drive coil made no measurable difference to the waveform at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup>

## Failure and diagnosis

Because a bead is normally a near-short at DC, an open bead reads as a broken connection and can take out a whole function. A production multimeter was repaired by tracing a fault down to a single failed RFI bead; the [[soldering]] was sound and the component itself was bad, and the isolated nature of the failure argued for a one-off part defect rather than a bad batch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-884" title="EEVblog #884 - EEVBlog BM235 Multimeter REPAIR">884</a></sup> Continuity checking a bead in a signal path is a standard step when hunting a break: beads in [[oscilloscope-channel|oscilloscope channel]] circuitry were checked and found not open,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> and a power-key line was traced through a bead to the switch when the path measured open elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>

## Substitution and rework

When EMI requirements do not apply to the immediate task, a bead can be bridged. A zero ohm resistor is the correct substitute; a mod wire is worse, because a length of wire can act as an inductor in its own right unless it is genuinely low impedance, so proper surface-mount zero ohm parts are preferred.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-884" title="EEVblog #884 - EEVBlog BM235 Multimeter REPAIR">884</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup> A board whose [[silkscreen]] designates a bead position but which is populated with zero ohm resistors reveals the designer's original intent and a later change of mind.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

## Analogue and RF uses

Beads are not only an emissions tool. Isolating an amplifier input with a ferrite bead raises the source impedance at high frequencies, which is one countermeasure against the negative-impedance [[oscillation]] that emitter-follower output stages exhibit into a capacitive load; beads work up in the hundreds of megahertz, well above the [[transistor]] bandwidths involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup> Beads have also been proposed across switching components such as a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] to damp [[ringing]] in a power supply, as one of several possible mitigation strategies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup>

Used as ordinary inductors in an RF context, salvaged beads are unpredictable. An unmarked SMD bead substituted into a tuned circuit shifted the resonance to around 125 MHz and behaved poorly at the top end, compared with a 500 nH salvaged inductor that gave about 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup>

## EMC workflow

The bead's role in development is as a cheap, late variable. In-house pre-compliance conducted emissions testing lets a design be changed — adding a few ferrite beads, adding shielding — and then re-run on the identical setup to see what each change did to conducted and radiated emissions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> The [[121gw|121GW]] multimeter's development went through EMC issues that required a redesign, with RF beads added at various points in the circuit across several prototype rounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> Conversely, a large ferrite appearing across a ribbon cable in an otherwise tidy instrument reads as a late EMI fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-985" title="EEVblog #985 - Siglent SDS1202X-E Oscilloscope Teardown">985</a></sup> Well-executed instrument power supplies are recognised in part by their attention to EMC with beads and related parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup>
