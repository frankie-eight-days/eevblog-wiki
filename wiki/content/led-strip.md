An LED strip is a long, narrow [[pcb|printed circuit board]] carrying a row of surface-mount LEDs, used as a linear light source in place of a lamp or fluorescent tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> Because LEDs are [[current]]-driven devices with a significant forward drop each, a strip is not a [[voltage]] load in the ordinary sense: it is one of the classic applications for a [[constant-current-source|constant current source]], which supplies a set current and lets the [[compliance-voltage|compliance voltage]] settle wherever the [[led|LED]] stack demands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Strips appear in three broad roles — purpose-built architectural and bench lighting, addressable display strips, and the [[backlight]] assemblies inside [[lcd|LCD]] panels, which are the most common salvage source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup>

## Construction and segmentation

A strip is built from repeating segments, each holding a small group of LEDs wired in series-parallel with its own current-setting element, so the strip can be cut between segments without breaking any group.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5mueaTsus8o" title="Wall Wash LED Strip Measurement - Part 2">•</a></sup> Segment length varies: an architectural strip may run 12 segments to the metre at roughly 85 mm each, while a display strip is cuttable every two pixels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> One 600 mm lighting length carried eight LEDs arranged series-parallel to present four LEDs' worth of forward drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> A backlight strip pulled from an [[lcd-display|LCD panel]] measured 61 cm and was made up of two-LED series groups at roughly 3 V each, giving about 5.9 V per group.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup>

Maximum board length is set by manufacturing, not by the circuit. The panel size used for SMT loading and [[soldering]] caps a single-pass board at around 600 mm for most fabricators; strips of up to 1,200 mm in a single pass exist, but that length is pushing the friendship in physical handling and through the assembly process, and carries a price premium.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> The practical answer is to build to a standard panel, fit a joining [[connector]], and chain lengths — an approach that let one manufacturer offer anything from 0.6 m to 5 m, with 3 m being the longest that would fit in a car.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> Display strips are sold as five-metre rolls by default and joined end to end, the join being visible between half-metre sections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup>

## Driving and current setting

Two schemes dominate the per-segment current-setting element: a plain [[dropper-resistor|dropper resistor]], or an active constant current chip driver on each string.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> Functionally they are interchangeable — both simply drop the surplus voltage — but the passive version tends to behave more linearly as the supply is wound down, while both eventually drop out once the supply falls below the string's compliance requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> A 24 V architectural strip drew just over 100 mA per segment, about 2.4 watts each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup>

Series stacking sets the supply voltage. Five 600 mm lengths presenting four LEDs of drop apiece stack to 20 LEDs, roughly 60 V at 3 V per LED, driven at 350 mA for about 20 W per length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> Six salvaged 6 V backlight strips in series need 36 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup> Salvaged backlight strips can carry high compliance requirements on their own: one long strip needed a 48 V compliance voltage to run at 20 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup> Such a strip will light visibly at 10 mA and remain lit down to 1 mA from a laboratory [[current-source|current source]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> Driver silicon for strip applications is typically a step-up LED controller with a dimming input, such as the MP3398L, rated to 350 mA maximum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1286" title="EEVblog #1286 - Mailbag">1286</a></sup>

Efficacy improves when the LEDs are underdriven. A part specified at about 97 lm/W at 350 mA reaches closer to 110 lm/W when each LED sees only 175 mA in a series-parallel arrangement, giving roughly 2,200 lm from a 20 W strip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> Driving harder yields more light at some cost in efficacy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> In a fluorescent-tube replacement installation, per-tube consumption fell from 35 to 40 W down to 17 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup>

## Rated versus measured output

Manufacturer power ratings on architectural strip are not reliably met. A strip specified at 32.6 watts per lineal metre was assembled to exactly one metre — 12 segments — and measured 27.25 W at its rated 24 V and 1.13 A; adding back the roughly 0.4 W lost in the supply leads still leaves it about 4 W short of specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5mueaTsus8o" title="Wall Wash LED Strip Measurement - Part 2">•</a></sup> A companion strip from the same family, rated 19.2 W and using a single dropper resistor per segment, met its specification precisely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5mueaTsus8o" title="Wall Wash LED Strip Measurement - Part 2">•</a></sup> The shortfall in the first strip, which used an active constant current chip driver, is consistent with a production-stage circuit change never reflected in the published [[datasheet|data sheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5mueaTsus8o" title="Wall Wash LED Strip Measurement - Part 2">•</a></sup>

[[color-temperature|Colour temperature]] claims deserve the same scepticism. Strip lights advertised at 5500 K bought to match an existing 5400 K installation measured differently enough that they could not be mixed in the same room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup> Where colour accuracy matters — product and PCB photography in particular — the specification to buy on is colour rendering index, with 95+ CRI strips available at 5000 K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6ZH2KeplSrs" title="DIY Product/PCB Photography Light Box - Part 3">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup> For domestic use, 3000 K is preferred because it is close to incandescent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup>

## Addressable strips

Addressable display strip is sold as a five-metre roll with a small controller offering a handful of pre-programmed effects — colour wipes and fades between two colours — aimed at shop windows and discos.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> The strip itself is more capable than that controller: it behaves as a [[shift-register|shift register]], data entering one end and leaving the other, so an arbitrary image can be piped through it and strips joined indefinitely, limited chiefly by the current-carrying copper tape running along the strip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup>

The interface matters for photographic use. A strip with separate clock, data and latch lines loads the whole frame and then presents it across all LEDs on one command; without a latch the image marches visibly down the strip as it is clocked in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> A later generation reduced this to clock and data with a propagated latch command.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> Latching matters because LEDs are unforgiving in long exposures, which is the exposure mode light-painting depends on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> A practical light-painting rig combined a 64-pixel strip, PC-side software converting text or images to strip-compatible pixel data, and a 900 MHz XBee radio treated simply as a serial pipe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup>

## Salvage from LCD panels

Modern LCD televisions and monitors use LED backlights rather than cold [[cathode]] fluorescent lamps, and both the edge-lit and direct-backlit variants contain usable strips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-916" title="EEVblog #916 - Work Bench Lighting From A Dumpster LCD TV">916</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> In an edge-lit panel a single strip runs along one edge of a light guide; total internal reflection carries the light along the guide until etched dot patterns on the back scatter it out through the front, backed by a white reflective film.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> Direct-backlit sets instead mount rows of strips in channels behind the diffuser, sometimes on right-angled bracket PCB assemblies added specifically for extra heat sinking, though the thermal coupling achieved that way is poor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup> Strips are held down with double-sided tape and lift out once the retaining clips and paper backing are removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-916" title="EEVblog #916 - Work Bench Lighting From A Dumpster LCD TV">916</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1758" title="EEVblog 1758 - A Greek Dumpster Tragedy">1758</a></sup>

Salvaged strips are well suited to under-bench lighting, architrave lighting, light boxes and similar improvised fixtures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-916" title="EEVblog #916 - Work Bench Lighting From A Dumpster LCD TV">916</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup> Replacement strips ordered for a repair are a different matter: strip variants for nominally the same television differ in LED count and in whether the assembly is one long board or several, so an order can arrive physically incompatible even after checking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OdovWOP7ik4" title="OF COURSE this happened">•</a></sup> A replacement strip bought from a Chinese supplier for 20 Australian dollars delivered was specified at 3 to 6 volts, 333 milliamps, 2 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GgIRnbm9gYI" title="Chinese Packaging Madness!">•</a></sup> Other equipment yields strips too — the erase lamp that floods a photocopier drum with light to clear residual toner is a usable LED light strip in its own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-303" title="EEVblog #303 - Photocopier Extreme Teardown">303</a></sup>

## Mounting and thermal design

Because a strip is a distributed heat source along a narrow board, the extrusion it sits in normally does the heat sinking; architectural profiles carry fins in the lower section for exactly this, with different output strips fitted to different faces of the same profile so one washes up and one down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> For a high-power outdoor installation predating readily available strips, individual Cree XPG LEDs bonded with thermal [[adhesive]] to a large aluminium plate provided the [[heat-sink|heatsink]]; the strips were made deliberately thin so the fixtures were invisible from [[ground]] level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup> That installation ran 11 years before individual LEDs began to fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup>

Where the drive current is low, thermal design largely takes care of itself: at the tens-of-milliamps to few-hundred-milliamps level typical of a [[battery]]-powered inspection lamp, an existing reflective backing serves as adequate heat sinking, and a 1,000 mAh cell running strips at 500 mA still gives a couple of hours of use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> Mounting position is a design variable in its own right — placing two strips on the outside edges of a magnifier housing widens the light angle compared with mounting them inboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup>
