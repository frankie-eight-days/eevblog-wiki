A solderless breadboard is a plastic block carrying rows of sprung metal contacts on a 0.1 inch grid, into which component leads and jumper wires are pushed to form a circuit without [[soldering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> It is the default first physical realisation of a design: a circuit is lashed up on a breadboard, measured, corrected, and only then committed to a [[pcb|printed circuit board]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> Its limits are entirely parasitic and mechanical — the contacts add [[capacitance]], [[resistance]] and lead [[inductance]] that a finished board would not — so what can usefully be built on one is bounded by [[frequency]], [[current]] and switching speed rather than by complexity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup>

## Construction

Under the plastic top the board is nothing more than individual metal strips seated in a baseboard, one strip per five-hole column, with separate longer strips forming the power buses along the edges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup> The strips lift out once the baseboard screws are removed, and they can be soldered to directly — a wire soldered onto the underside strip for a particular pin is a way to inject a signal into a running breadboard circuit without any visible connection on top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup> Versions exist without a rigid baseboard at all, the strips held in place by nothing more than a paper backing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup> Individual boards are usually adhesive-backed so they can be stuck to any base, and larger assemblies are built by ganging several standard boards together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1130" title="EEVblog #1130 - Mailbag Monday">1130</a></sup>

There is no meaningful dimensional standard beyond the 0.1 inch hole pitch. Add-on boards and power modules built to plug into the power rails of one breadboard frequently will not seat in another: pin spacing differs, the rails sit at different offsets, and rail polarity is not consistent between brands, with some boards putting the positive rail where others put ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> The practical consequence is that a breadboard accessory is compatible with a specific breadboard rather than with breadboards in general.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup>

## Parasitic capacitance

The capacitance between two adjacent contact columns is approximately 2 pF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> This figure held across four different breadboards of different brands, because the capacitance is set by the physical dimensions of the contacts and their spacing, and both those dimensions and the dielectric constant of the moulding material vary little between manufacturers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> Coupling from a power strip to a signal column, and coupling across the central dividing channel, are both substantially lower — of the order of a few tenths of a picofarad across the divide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> Forcing pins into the contacts raises the measured capacitance slightly, as closer spacing implies, but not enough to matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> The figure is stable enough with frequency to be used directly in a simulation when diagnosing why a breadboarded circuit oscillates: two picofarads per contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup>

This is not a purely theoretical concern. Capacitive coupling through the board persists even after the component under test is physically removed from it, which is enough to produce spurious responses when characterising delay lines and similar parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup> The breadboard's capacitance also loads logic outputs, adding to the slew of edges alongside the input capacitance of the driven chip and any track capacitance, and it is that total load capacitance that sets the fan-out limit of a logic family.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

## Frequency and topology limits

Both capacitance and inductance are high on a breadboard, and the practical ceiling for general-purpose work is a few megahertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> A [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching power supply]] is the clearest case of what does not belong on one: fast switching edges, large currents and physically large switching loops formed by long jumper leads combine to make the result perform badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> A high-frequency DC-DC converter built on a breadboard will behave erratically because of parasitic capacitance and everything else the layout introduces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> Attempting to probe such a circuit compounds the problem, since a breadboarded switcher is already a poor measurement environment before the probe ground lead is considered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> The converse also holds: a 555 timer, or a [[microcontroller]] doing its fast work internally and driving its I/O at modest rates, gains nothing from being moved to a PCB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Simulation and breadboarding fail in complementary ways. A simulator lets component values be swept freely without stocking parts, but does not reproduce the parasitic effects that appear the moment a circuit is physically built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup>

## Contact quality

Breadboard contacts are a real and measurable source of error. In a precision 1 A current source built from 0.02% resistors, the residual error beyond the expected budget was attributable to [[contact-resistance]] in the breadboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> Measurements taken on a breadboard drift as the wiring is disturbed, and voltages across a [[resistor]] string visibly wander because of unreliable contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Precision work on a breadboard is therefore bounded by the interconnect rather than by the components; a circuit good to 0.03% on a breadboard was considered to have earned a dedicated board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

One specific and avoidable cause of bad contacts is the adhesive used on bandoliered axial components. Glue remains on the lead ends when a resistor is pulled from the tape, and pushing that lead straight into a breadboard gives a contact that may not touch the spring at all; the fix is to trim or scrape the lead ends before insertion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> The same residue also makes those leads difficult to solder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>

## Packages and interfacing

A breadboard accepts through-hole leads only, which is why DIP packaging retains value well beyond hobbyist use — a DIP part can simply be pushed into a board and used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-96" title="EEVblog #96 - The TI LaunchPad MSP430 Development Board">96</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-510" title="EEVblog #510 - Mailbag">510</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Surface-mount parts reach the breadboard [[via]] adapter boards that carry the SMD footprint on one side and a 0.1 inch pin header on the other; these also serve as substitutes for obsolete DIP versions of parts still available in SO or smaller packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KzC12xtHuXo" title="Just Soldering an SMD Adapter">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> Adapters of this kind are worth stocking specifically so a new part in an awkward package can be tried at all, and some carry decoupling and filtering provision on the underside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> The same technique makes a single surface-mount [[capacitor]] measurable on an [[lcr-meter]] by soldering it to an adapter and plugging that into the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup>

Interfacing to the outside world is the breadboard's weakest point. Pre-cut jumper wire sets are the basic accessory, supplemented by leads terminated in breadboard pins at one end for external components too bulky to plug in directly, and by pin-to-banana adapters for connecting instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-51" title="EEVblog #51 - A tour of the EEVblog Electronics Lab">51</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-893" title="EEVblog #893 - Mailbag">893</a></sup> Purpose-built interface boards that present screw or push terminals on one side and breadboard-compatible wires on the other exist to replace wires hanging loosely off the board, which matters when a prototype has to survive being transported and demonstrated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-807" title="EEVblog #807 - Mailbag">807</a></sup>

## Failure modes and traps

Pulling a DIP chip out of a breadboard can extract the spring contacts with it, damaging the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (5 of 5): Spooky Action - Hardware Testing">1306</a></sup> Breadboards are not rated for reflow temperatures, so attempts to solder to them by reflow risk destroying the plastic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup> A subtler trap is phantom powering: a microcontroller or 74-series logic device whose VCC pin has been left unconnected on the breadboard can still be powered through the protection diodes on its other pins, so the circuit appears to work while remaining intermittently faulty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

Wiring errors on a breadboard are frequently mechanical rather than conceptual. In one university laboratory exercise, a student who found jumper wires too close together to insert comfortably spread a single op-amp circuit across eight separate op-amp packages, using one pin from each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup>

## Alternatives

Stripboard and matrix board, which carry continuous copper strips on a soldered board, are the traditional permanent counterpart, and versions exist whose track pattern deliberately mimics breadboard layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-54" title="EEVblog #54 - Electronics - When I was a boy...">54</a></sup> Snappable scored PCBs with the same five-hole strip-and-bus pattern give a solderable equivalent of a breadboard section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup> Purpose-designed proto boards intended to replace breadboards address the wiring problem directly by allowing SMD parts to be soldered down and modules to be interconnected via their edges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1681" title="EEVblog 1681 - Mailbag: Nice DIY CC Load,  Amazing Proto Boards, Ian Johnston">1681</a></sup> Software-switched jumperless breadboards, which route connections electronically from a netlist, work but occupy a narrow niche: the build area is small, the workflow requires a command-line tool, and for ordinary wiring to a Raspberry Pi or Arduino a conventional breadboard with headers is far cheaper and simpler.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup>

## Role in learning and development

The breadboard is the standard vehicle for undergraduate laboratory work, where students build a circuit on one and test it against the bench's signal generator, supply, [[oscilloscope]] and SMU.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1565" title="EEVblog 1565 - UNSW Electronics Lab &amp; Makerspace Tour">1565</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mW-1FMGpsFg" title="UNSW Talk - Part 2 - Job Tip, What I hated about university EE, Contracting + more">•</a></sup> It is also the recommended entry point for self-teaching: a breadboard, a 555 timer, some parts and a set of jumper leads, with the expectation that the circuit will not work first time and that debugging it is where the learning happens.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zyuRcsM0gjI" title="eevBLAB #10 - Why Learn Basic Electronics?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-302" title="EEVblog #302 - Electronics Beginner Advice">302</a></sup> A breadboard, several of them in varying sizes, and a stock of pre-cut leads are treated as basic lab equipment rather than optional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-51" title="EEVblog #51 - A tour of the EEVblog Electronics Lab">51</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup>

In development work the breadboard's role is verification before commitment. A constant-current lab supply was built up on a breadboard and its regulation and heat-sink temperature measured there before the board was laid out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> A soft-latching power switch, a peak detector, a Cockcroft-Walton multiplier, a bandpass filter for Bode plotting and an overload detector were each lashed up on a breadboard to confirm behaviour and expose traps before any further work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> What a breadboard cannot validate is a product: form factor, enclosure, manufacturability and [[bill-of-materials]] are untouched by a working breadboarded circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-88" title="EEVblog #88 - There&#x27;s More To Electronics Than Just Circuit Design">88</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zyuRcsM0gjI" title="eevBLAB #10 - Why Learn Basic Electronics?">•</a></sup>
