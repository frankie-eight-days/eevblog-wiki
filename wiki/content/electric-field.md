The electric field, usually abbreviated E-field, is the field set up by separated electric charge, and it is one of the two components — the other being the magnetic or H field — that together make up electromagnetic radiation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> It matters in practical electronics far beyond physics classes: it is the mechanism by which a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] switches, by which an [[lcd-segment|LCD segment]] turns dark, by which a [[capacitor]] stores energy, and by which one half of a board's radiated emissions escapes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-486" title="EEVblog #486 - Does Current Flow Through A Capacitor?">486</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1273" title="EEVblog #1273 - EMC Near Field vs Far Field Explained">1273</a></sup> [[voltage|Voltage]] itself is defined in terms of it — voltage is the integral of electric field intensity along a path, which is why the classical definition gives no voltage across a coil, where there is no electric field through the winding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UStV3zyhgnQ" title="Dave &amp; Electroboom - The Amp Hour">•</a></sup>

## Field control inside semiconductors

The field effect [[transistor]] takes its name directly from the effect. With the device off, two depletion layers block conduction from source to drain; applying a positive gate voltage — not a large one, a couple of volts depending on the type — establishes an electric field strong enough to overcome the depletion region barrier, forming a channel between the two heavily doped n-type regions through which electrons flow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup> The switch is turned on by the field alone, with no gate [[current]] required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup>

Gallium nitride exploits a far more intense field. Because GaN is polar, mechanically straining the crystal generates charge; growing a thin GaN layer on silicon and capping it with a layer that squeezes the surface — aluminium gallium nitride — produces a heterojunction so intense that it generates a "6 megs per centimeter electric field" across a distance of only a few angstroms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> That field pulls electrons to the surface and confines them so tightly that they behave quantum mechanically, forming a unified electron gas.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

Cell geometry in power devices is a field-management problem. Hexagonal cells, with every internal angle at 120 degrees, let the depletion regions meet exactly in the middle, giving the lowest possible peak electric field.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> Aligned square cells leave a hole in the middle that becomes a high-field point and causes premature breakdown; offset squares retain 90-degree angles whose curvature lowers the breakdown voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> Radiation hardness is a related concern: a device designed correctly does not respond to a charged particle passing through it with a spike in the electric field capable of destroying it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

## Capacitors and dielectrics

Charging a capacitor builds equal and opposite charges on its two plates and sets up an electric field in the dielectric between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-486" title="EEVblog #486 - Does Current Flow Through A Capacitor?">486</a></sup> No electrons cross the dielectric — as far as conduction goes it behaves like an open switch — yet current measurably enters one terminal and leaves the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-486" title="EEVblog #486 - Does Current Flow Through A Capacitor?">486</a></sup> The resolution is that the changing electric field between the plates creates a changing [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]], and that gives the displacement current its equivalent through the dielectric.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-486" title="EEVblog #486 - Does Current Flow Through A Capacitor?">486</a></sup> On the conductor side, ordinary current language works fine; once inside the insulator the correct description is changing electric fields creating changing magnetic fields, with energy stored and transferred in those fields, and it all falls out of Maxwell's equations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-486" title="EEVblog #486 - Does Current Flow Through A Capacitor?">486</a></sup>

For everyday design work the useful abstraction is the simpler one: treating current as flowing through the capacitor is easier from a design point of view in every practical aspect, even though the physical reality is charge accumulating on the plates and a field between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ItoRt1buLkM" title="Initial reaction to the Veritasium video &quot;The Big Misconception About Electricity&quot;">•</a></sup>

Field strength distribution also sets a floor on how small a [[ceramic-capacitor|ceramic capacitor]] can shrink. The dielectric is barium titanate, a granular structure, and between the electrodes the grains stack up; the design goal is to have some number of grains in the stack so the electric field is divided across each grain, so that one grain failing does not fail the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup>

## Displays

Liquid crystal displays are electric-field devices, not current devices. In a twisted nematic [[lcd|LCD]] the liquid crystal fluid sits between top and bottom electrodes and is affected by the field between them — essentially not a current, just an electric field between the positive and negative plates — which changes the orientation of the crystals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Applying a field across an individual red, green or blue element turns the polarization of light through that element on or off, so the colour filter behind it either passes or blocks light; three such elements make one pixel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup>

Because the drive is a field rather than a current, an LCD has essentially no static [[power-consumption|power consumption]], with the switching [[capacitance]] accounting for nearly all of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1670" title="EEVblog 1670 - BM786 Multimeter BATTERY LEAKAGE REPAIR + GIVEAWAY">1670</a></sup> The corresponding side effect is that the extremely high [[impedance]] of the panel lets stray charge persist: rubbing a finger along a disconnected LCD raises segments that then stay lit, because the charge simply remains on the glass and the field holds them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1670" title="EEVblog 1670 - BM786 Multimeter BATTERY LEAKAGE REPAIR + GIVEAWAY">1670</a></sup>

## Radiated emissions and EMC

Which of the two fields dominates a given emission depends on the source impedance. Low-impedance, high-current sources — heavy switching currents in a trace, or fast switching dumping energy into bypass capacitors and interplane capacitance — generate predominantly magnetic field.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1273" title="EEVblog #1273 - EMC Near Field vs Far Field Explained">1273</a></sup> High-impedance sources that pass little current generate electric fields; a static 5 V [[power-supply|power supply]] rail is an example.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1273" title="EEVblog #1273 - EMC Near Field vs Far Field Explained">1273</a></sup> The rule of thumb follows: at high voltage and low current the E-field dominates and an E-field probe is the right instrument, while high-current nodes call for H-field work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup> In the near field the H field presents a very low source impedance and the E field a very high one, with the boundary between near and far field set by wavelength, at lambda over 2 pi.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1273" title="EEVblog #1273 - EMC Near Field vs Far Field Explained">1273</a></sup>

In the far field the two are locked together. The standard picture shows the electric field oscillating along one axis and the H field at 90 degrees to it, the two propagating together; at a sufficient distance in wavelengths they combine into the electromagnetic radiation that a test house measures during EMC compliance testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> Board layout determines the mix: large loop areas that cannot be avoided across a big multi-chip board radiate mostly magnetic field with an electric field component as well, and whether a given structure emits E, H, or combined field depends on source and load impedance, [[frequency]], bypassing and capacitive loading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup>

## Electrostatics and ESD

Charge on a non-conductor produces a field, and that field induces charge on the nearest conductor — only on a conductor, since a non-conductor will not take an induced charge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup> The chain runs from a charged shoe sole to the skin of the wearer to whatever conductive surface is next in line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup>

That chain is measurable. Once a plate is charged, the electric field at a distance can be measured, and surface DC voltmeters that do exactly this are commonplace instruments, calibrated for a specific standoff such as 1 kV at one inch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1567" title="EEVblog 1567 - Wireless ESD Monitor? - The IONA StatIQ Band TESTED">1567</a></sup> Wireless body-voltage monitors work through the same sequence — electric field to charge density to total charge to voltage — which is how a body voltage reading is obtained without a wire to the wearer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1567" title="EEVblog 1567 - Wireless ESD Monitor? - The IONA StatIQ Band TESTED">1567</a></sup> The relationship breaks down in a few scenarios: standing next to a grounded workstation or wall makes the charge distribution on the body asymmetric, so the reported voltage runs slightly high or low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1567" title="EEVblog 1567 - Wireless ESD Monitor? - The IONA StatIQ Band TESTED">1567</a></sup>

## Sensing and unintended pickup

Non-contact voltage detection is E-field pickup. In a socket tester the sensing element is a bare strip of copper along the top edge of the board acting as an [[antenna]] for the electric field.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> The same coupling appears as an interference mechanism: 50 Hz pickup on [[relay]] contacts inside an [[oscilloscope]] is an electric field problem rather than a magnetic one, which is why magnetic [[shielding]] tape over the relays does not fix it and a metal can does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup>

Piezoelectric ceramic bender transducers work the field in both directions — they bend when acoustic pressure is applied and equally bend when an electric field is applied across them, behaving electrically as a capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1400" title="EEVblog 1400 - Hard Drive Micro Actuators are AMAZING!">1400</a></sup> At the other end of the scale, beam position monitors and related pickups placed inside a synchrotron beamline detect the electric and magnetic fields produced by the electron beam itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-836" title="EEVblog #836 - Tour Of The Australian Synchrotron">836</a></sup>

## Fields as the carrier of energy

Maxwell's realization was that light consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields, perpendicular to each other and in phase, so that when one is at maximum so is the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup> Poynting's vector, derived from conservation of energy, applies wherever electric and magnetic fields coincide, giving a flow of energy that can be calculated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup> A [[battery]] alone has an electric field but no magnetic field because no charges move, so it loses no energy; connected into a circuit, its electric field extends through the circuit at the speed of light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup> Surface charge on the conductors creates an electric field outside the wires while the current inside creates a magnetic field outside them, and the combination in the surrounding space carries the energy — the fields, not the electrons, which barely move at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup>

None of this is new material for engineers, who are taught drift velocity, Maxwell's equations and the Poynting vector as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup> The field-propagation description is not wrong, but it is of little use to a practising design engineer: no working engineer reasons about energy transport as the propagation of electric fields.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ItoRt1buLkM" title="Initial reaction to the Veritasium video &quot;The Big Misconception About Electricity&quot;">•</a></sup> The exception is at the [[pcb|PCB]] level, where dielectrics, [[transmission-line|transmission line]] behaviour and energy in the dielectric genuinely start to matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ItoRt1buLkM" title="Initial reaction to the Veritasium video &quot;The Big Misconception About Electricity&quot;">•</a></sup>
