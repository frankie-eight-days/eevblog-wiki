A PCB trace is a strip of copper etched onto a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] that carries [[current]] between components. It is a conductor like any other, so it obeys Ohm's law: pass current through it and it develops a [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]], dissipates power, and heats up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> It also has [[inductance]] — every piece of wire and every trace on a board has inductance in some way, shape or form — and [[capacitance]] to whatever copper lies near it, which is why a trace behaves as a circuit element rather than an ideal wire once [[frequency]] or current rises.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Trace geometry is therefore a design parameter, not a drawing detail: width, copper weight, length and layer all set electrical behaviour.

## Resistance and sheet resistance

The [[resistance]] of a trace is most easily computed from sheet resistance, which is quoted per square rather than per unit length. One-ounce copper is close to 0.5 milliohms per square, and since a square of any size has the same resistance, the calculation reduces to counting how many squares long the trace is and multiplying.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-732" title="EEVblog #732 - PCB Sheet Resistance">732</a></sup> Doubling the length doubles the resistance; tripling it triples it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-732" title="EEVblog #732 - PCB Sheet Resistance">732</a></sup> The method assumes the connection at each end spans the full width of the trace — a surface-mount pad at each end effectively touches the entire edge, which is the case that makes the square-counting valid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-732" title="EEVblog #732 - PCB Sheet Resistance">732</a></sup> The same approach applies to any material whose sheet resistance is specified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-732" title="EEVblog #732 - PCB Sheet Resistance">732</a></sup>

The numbers involved are small enough that measuring them requires a low-ohms capability: a four-and-a-half digit meter resolving down to 10 microohms is adequate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> A 165 mm length of bare 1 oz copper roughly 2 mm wide calculates to around 86 milliohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup>

Because trace resistance is predictable and repeatable, it is sometimes used deliberately as a component. Paralleled linear pass devices need small ballast resistors to share current, and a 10 milliohm ballast can be implemented as a trace rather than a bought part — typically a 10 thou or 20 thou wide track in 1 oz or 2 oz copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> The same trick has limits: where a predictable low resistance really matters, a length of thick gauge wire soldered across the board is more calculable than the copper, which is why boards are sometimes found with fat jumper wires bypassing the traces to get lower [[impedance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

## Current rating and temperature rise

Sizing a trace for current is a thermal question before it is an electrical one: the designer must choose an acceptable temperature rise in the copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Calculators default to a 1 °C rise, and a common design value is 10 °C, though giving away 10 °C of self-heating is only justified in high-power work that is already at the physical limit of how wide the traces can be made.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Vias are treated the same way, except that a [[via]] is made by a less controlled manufacturing process than a regular trace and so deserves more margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup>

Concrete figures from the IPC-2152 model: a 100 mil (2.54 mm) wide external trace in 1 oz copper comes out around 5 milliohms and carries a nominal 3.28 A, at which point it rises about 10 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Scaling up, a 25 mm (one inch) wide 1 oz trace manages only about 12.6 A, and one inch is about as wide as a trace is worth making before it consumes an unreasonable amount of board area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Doubling to 2 oz copper roughly doubles the rating; a cheap prototype service supplying half-ounce copper limits the same geometry to about 8 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup>

The standard's model has known blind spots. Internal and external layers return the same answer from the calculator, but in practice they differ — forced air over the board gives outer traces better thermal performance than inner ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Proximity to a plane matters too, and calculators expose a "plane present" option for it, because heat conducts from a narrow trace into an adjacent plane through the thin prepreg between them; on an inner layer that separation is small, on an outer layer it is larger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Simply having the plane there improves matters even when no current flows in it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup>

For a genuinely large current — 70 to 80 A into a [[mosfet|MOSFET]], for instance — a single layer is not realistic unless the traces are enormous and a large temperature rise is tolerated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> The workable answer is thick copper, two heavy traces top and bottom, probably part of an internal plane as well, and optionally [[solder]] plating over the top to add capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Standard 1 oz copper on four layers still falls short at that current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> [[via-stitching|Via stitching]] between the layers helps, but stitching until the cows come home is counterproductive because every hole removes copper surface area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup>

## Tinning and solder mask removal

Leaving the [[solder-mask|solder mask]] off a power trace so that the wave solder deposits a layer of solder on the exposed copper is a cheap and very common way to lower resistance and raise current-carrying capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> It appears wherever a designer wanted more current without paying for 2 oz copper — on [[relay]] power traces, on charger output tracks, on rectifier and [[led|LED]] driver boards, and on rails running to a main [[processor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-764" title="EEVblog #764 - New Lab Ceiling LED Lighting Installation">764</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> Controlled measurement puts the improvement at a 15% to 50% reduction in resistance from tinning with 60/40 lead solder, with the thickness a wave solder process actually deposits landing near the top of that range, effectively halving the resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> It is not as effective as ordering thicker copper, but it does the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup>

## Voltage drop in real designs

Trace resistance turns into a design constraint when supply margins are tight. Modern [[fpga|FPGA]] cores tolerate very little: margins in the tens of millivolts mean the drop along the power traces has to be budgeted explicitly, and a common tactic is to set the regulator slightly above nominal — 0.93 V instead of 0.9 V — to buy back headroom, converting a 30 mV budget into roughly 60 mV to spend on copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> The trap is adding [[output-ripple|output ripple]] on top of the DC drop: a trace calculation that looks fine on its own can breach a 125 mV limit once [[ripple]], a change of operating mode drawing extra current, and temperature effects are stacked on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> The complete budget includes not just the traces but the switching behaviour of the converter's output stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

Where a single [[capacitor]] feeds a load, all of the current including peak current flows down one trace, and in high-power designs the loss in that copper is significant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Splitting the bank into several capacitors each with its own trace to a common star point divides the current — three parallel paths carry a third of the current each — and reduces the drop by Ohm's law, so smaller traces suffice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Similarly, measuring a regulator's [[output-voltage|output voltage]] must be done directly at the device terminals; probing further away includes the drop caused by load current in the wiring and board copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

## Propagation delay and high-frequency behaviour

Signals do not cross a board instantaneously. The traditional layout rule of thumb is roughly 1 ns of [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]] per 15 cm, or 6 inches, of PCB trace, so a 15 cm run costs a nanosecond of skew — the basis of length matching on DDR memory, where data is clocked on both clock edges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> The delay is real enough that a trace can substitute for a discrete delay line component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1079" title="EEVblog #1079 - Mailbag">1079</a></sup>

At high frequency a trace snaking across a board above a [[ground-plane|ground plane]] is a [[transmission-line|transmission line]], and the energy is carried in the field between conductor and return path rather than spread uniformly through the plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup> The consequence for compliance work is that traces radiate: unless the product is sealed in an effective metal enclosure, all the little traces inside act as antennas, which is the radiated emissions problem at the centre of EMC testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> Trace capacitance also loads logic outputs, slowing edges alongside the load's [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]] and any other components on the bus, which is part of why logic families specify a maximum fan-out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

Magnetic coupling falls away quickly with distance — the field surrounding a trace drops roughly with the square of the distance — which is why a sensor intended to read trace current has to sit right on the board rather than an inch above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

## Layout practice

Classic double-sided layout runs all the traces on the top layer in one direction and all the traces on the bottom in the perpendicular direction, with components oriented in rows to suit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The convention long predates modern tools and survives in four-layer digital boards, where power and [[ground]] occupy the inner layers and the signal layers run horizontal and vertical respectively.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-994" title="EEVblog #994 - CERN/Fermilab Particle Accelerator Boards!">994</a></sup> Right-angle corners in traces are the visible signature of early autorouting and of old hand-taped artwork, and are held in low regard by layout convention — "All the electrons are going to fall out."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1256" title="EEVblog #1256 - GORGEOUS Mystery Test Gear Teardown!">1256</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-769" title="EEVblog #769 - Mailbag">769</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-363" title="EEVblog #363 - Gold Phone Teardown">363</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup> The counter-position is that the fuss over corner geometry is overstated: "45° PCB traces, highly overrated."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup>

Autorouters remain worse than a competent human at the hard cases. A router that completes 231 of 238 nets and gives up on six trivially routable connections is a normal outcome, and the failures are often ones a designer can see a path for at a glance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup> The characteristic behaviour is rip-up and retry: when the algorithm boxes itself in, it tears out placed traces and tries again, sometimes taking a route all the way around the board and back rather than the direct path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup> The pragmatic use is to hand-route the critical nets first — clock lines and similar — and then let the router handle individual non-critical nets one at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup>

Escape routing around dense parts sets much of the constraint. Vias crowding a [[bga|BGA]] fanout can leave room for only one trace between them, which is an argument for blind and buried vias: they free up inner-layer routing density and leave the ground plane intact rather than chopped up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Angled pads on a footprint are sometimes chosen specifically so that traces can pass between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Where a designer wants deliberate copper shapes rather than connectivity, a trace can be extended from each protected pin to form one side of a spark gap against a surrounding polygon pour, so that a transient flashes over at the input [[connector]] instead of propagating along the trace into an IC pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup>

Panelization can also carry traces. The panel frame is part of the PCB, so traces, circuitry and connectors can be placed on it, and signals routed out of the individual boards through the breakout tabs to a test connector on the frame — perhaps four signals per board with four tabs, using around 10 mil traces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Overlaying the panel and board Gerbers joins the copper automatically at the [[gerber|Gerber]] layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> This is only possible with breakout tabs; V-grooving is cheap and snaps apart easily, but the cutting wheel saws away any trace crossing the groove, top or bottom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> The router bit used on tab routing is typically around 2.4 mm and the routing paths are not as precise as drawn, which has to be allowed for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## Manufacturing limits

Gerber output resolution truncates trace widths: at 2:3 format, one mil of resolution, a trace drawn at 10.5 thou renders as 10 or 11 thou, and finer geometry requires 2:4 or 2:5 output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup> Over-etching during fabrication can open a trace entirely — a common enough defect that bare-board electrical test exists specifically to catch breaks of that kind before assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1673" title="EEVblog 1673 - JBL Partybox 310 Repair - Part 2 (SPOILER)">1673</a></sup> Etch faults in the other direction produce a short between adjacent tracks, and on a brand-new board that has never been powered a short between two pins is the first thing to suspect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup>

Solder mask sets the limit on fine-pitch routing between pins. When a trace runs between IC pins at a pitch too small for mask to be placed between them, solder pulls across during reflow and produces bridges that look like faults but are unavoidable given the layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup> The same absence of mask expansion can be exploited deliberately, though it may be a sign of poor layout where the trace could simply have been routed around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup>

## Failure modes and repair

Traces fail by corrosion far more often than by anything electrical. [[alkaline-battery|Alkaline battery]] leakage eats copper away entirely, including under the solder mask, leaving whole traces gone and vias destroyed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZlDf1d18Kag" title="Another Horror Alkaline Battery Leakage Teardown. RIP Magellen GPS">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup> Spilled liquid does the same locally: in one repair a corrosive spill ate through a single power switch trace while leaving every neighbouring trace on the same board untouched, the selectivity itself being the diagnostic clue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1673" title="EEVblog 1673 - JBL Partybox 310 Repair - Part 2 (SPOILER)">1673</a></sup> Water ingress produces general breakdown of traces and corrosion that spreads under the mask, which is why such repairs often fail again months later — the rot continues out of sight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> Vintage boards showing corroded copper may have micro-breaks in many traces at once, requiring every one to be buzzed out individually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Gbn_51IoJiM" title="Amiga 2000 Teardown &amp; Power Up">•</a></sup> Mitigation is to scrape back the affected copper, neutralise it, and seal with [[conformal-coating|conformal coating]] or UV-curable solder mask.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1668" title="EEVblog 1668 - HORRIFIC Alkaline Battery Leakage in Rare Prototype Multimeter">1668</a></sup>

Mechanical damage is the other category. A gouge or chunk taken out of a trace by tooling or rework is a plausible fault; a light scratch across a wide trace is not, because a crack will not form in copper of that width.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m3sQHx5aMmU" title="BM786 Switch Issue - Part 2">•</a></sup> Traces close to mounting holes can be scraped by hardware, which is why fastener kits include insulating washers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-462" title="EEVblog #462 - Video Editing PC Assembly">462</a></sup> Grinding a board to gain clearance risks cutting traces and ground planes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-934" title="EEVblog #934 - Raspberry Pi Supercomputer Cluster PART 1">934</a></sup> When removing a fine-pitch chip by slicing its leads with a knife, a shallow blade angle keeps the edge off the traces underneath; a steep angle also puts lateral force on the pins and can rip pads off, since pads without a trace attached are weaker than those with copper running out of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Gross electrical overload peels tracks straight off the substrate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-84" title="EEVblog #84 - High Energy Multimeter Destruction">84</a></sup>

The standard repair is to scrape back the solder mask either side of the break and bridge it — a short [[solder-bridge|solder bridge]] if the gap is small, a mod wire if the trace is thin or the surrounding copper has started to lift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> Where a chip and its short run of copper are both suspect, the copper is rarely the culprit: a centimetre of trace between two ICs is far less likely to be faulty than the solder joints at either end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

## Following traces on a finished board

[[reverse-engineering|Reverse engineering]] a board begins with high-resolution photographs of each side, marking off traces on printouts with a highlighter until the circuit closes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup> Backlighting the board with a diffuse source — a light box rather than a point source — makes traces visible through the substrate and can reveal inner-layer routing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Single-sided boards are by far the easiest, since nothing hides beneath the components; double-sided and multi-layer boards force [[desoldering]] of parts to see what runs underneath, and a four-layer board simply cannot be read by holding it up to the light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-164" title="EEVblog #164 - Agilent Fly To The Moon">164</a></sup> Where the copper cannot be seen, continuity measurement fills the gap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-164" title="EEVblog #164 - Agilent Fly To The Moon">164</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> Traces hidden under a component are a classic trap — a right-angle trace concealed by a [[resistor]] body can invalidate an otherwise sound deduction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup>

Solder mask colour has a direct effect on this work. Black mask, gloss especially, makes fine traces very hard to see, a serious nuisance when hacking or testing prototypes; white mask has the same problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-928" title="EEVblog #928 - Jaycar DMX RGB LED Lights Teardown">928</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> Matte black is more tolerable than gloss and contrasts well with white [[silkscreen]], but no black mask is good for reading copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup> Clear mask gives the best contrast and is the easiest to debug against.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup>

Traces also carry information when read as layout. Polarity can be deduced by following the trace from a polarised capacitor to a connector pin, or from a switch contact to a barrel jack sleeve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-573" title="EEVblog #573 - Mailbag">573</a></sup> The width and destination of copper indicates function: an unusually massive trace at a [[multimeter]]'s input studs is there for thermal reasons rather than current, and a heavy trace next to a relay with the mask left off is unmistakably the switched power path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup>

## Measuring current in a trace

Ordinary current probes require a conductor to be threaded through a clamp, which on a finished board means cutting a trace and installing a wire loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> A positional current probe removes that requirement: a fluxgate magnetometer head placed against the board reads the current in the trace directly through the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]], with the probe held perpendicular to the trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup> Because the field falls off with roughly the square of distance, the head must be right on the copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> The instrument is relative, so absolute measurements require [[calibration]] against a built-in reference trace of known width — around 3 mm giving roughly 3 V peak-to-peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> In use it maps current paths as well as magnitudes: on a board with a split ground plane, all return current is forced through the single narrow trace bridging the two halves, and the probe shows the current there and nothing in the isolated fill regions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> The same technique reads charging and discharging [[waveform|waveforms]] in the power traces of a live product without breaking into them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>
