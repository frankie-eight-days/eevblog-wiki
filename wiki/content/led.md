A light-emitting [[diode]] (LED) is a semiconductor diode that emits light when forward [[current]] passes through it. It is a current-driven, non-linear device: brightness is controlled by the current through the junction, while the forward [[voltage-drop]] is a consequence of that current, so an LED must never be connected directly across a low-[[impedance]] [[voltage-source]] without [[current-limiting]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> Light output is roughly linearly proportional to current over most of the operating range, even though the current–[[voltage]] characteristic is non-linear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

## Electrical characteristics

Forward voltage depends on the LED's material and on the operating current. A typical red LED drops about 2 V at its 20 mA rated point, falling to roughly 1.8 V at 10 mA and about 1.6 V at 1 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> White LEDs drop around 3 V each, with some high-voltage parts dropping 5 V per die.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Because the device is non-linear, the supply must exceed a minimum [[compliance-voltage]] before any current flows at all: a 1 V, 100 A supply cannot light an LED, while a current source with adequate compliance voltage will.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering! WTF!">1455</a></sup> A string of nine series LEDs measured 45–46 V total drop at 1 mA, and a six-LED architectural strip extinguished below about 15–16 V, a little over 2 V per LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup>

LEDs emit usable light at far below their nominal rating. A standard LED gives reasonable brightness at 1 mA (about 1.6 mW for a red part), and a high-[[efficiency]] LED can be visibly lit at 30 µA in room light; emission persists down to extremely small currents, which is the basis of photon-counting experiments probing the turn-on threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering! WTF!">1455</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup> Conversely, the eye's logarithmic response means that halving current from 20 mA to 10 mA produces little apparent brightness change, so running indicator LEDs at full rated current is usually unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

## Current limiting and drive circuits

The standard drive is a series "dropper" [[resistor]] sized from the rail voltage minus the LED forward voltage, divided by the desired current — for example, 2 V across a 100 Ω resistor gives 20 mA, and a 7.2 V [[battery]] driving a 5.6 V LED string at 250 mA needs a resistor dropping 1.6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> For order-of-magnitude work, a 10 V rail with a 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ resistor brackets the usable 1–10 mA range for a standard LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> Where brightness stability matters, a constant-current circuit replaces the resistor; an [[lm317]] with a 2.5 Ω sense resistor makes a 500 mA constant-current source, which shared across 24 LEDs gives about 20.8 mA each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

Placing LEDs directly in parallel is poor practice because they do not share current evenly unless matched at the semiconductor level; a series ballast resistor per branch forces sharing, although same-batch parts are often reasonably matched, and some designs rely on the output on-[[resistance]] of driver chips as an incidental ballast.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup> Apparent exceptions such as keychain flashers with an LED straight across a coin cell work only because the battery's internal resistance acts as the series element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> Driving an LED from a low-impedance source with no limiting produces current spikes of hundreds of milliamps that can exceed both the continuous rating (commonly 20 mA) and any pulse rating, drastically shortening LED life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-135" title="EEVblog #135 - Kindle Case Mythbusting">135</a></sup>

## Failure modes and reliability

Gross overvoltage is destructive: 15 V across a red LED with no current limiting heats the bond wire until it glows, smokes, and fuses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup> Sustained operation within current ratings (20 mA is safe for virtually any LED; many are rated 30–60 mA) still fails when thermal design is poor — cheap LED products have burned from inadequate board copper and bad FR4 rather than overcurrent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> LEDs are also temperature-sensitive during assembly, being notorious for not surviving reflow temperatures, and moisture-sensitive parts ship in sealed bags with desiccant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup>

White LEDs are blue dies with a phosphor conversion coating; with age, heat, and time the phosphor degrades and the output shifts blue, which is why aged [[lcd]] backlights develop a blue tint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OdovWOP7ik4" title="OF COURSE this happened">•</a></sup>

## Thermal design and efficacy

High-power LED design is dominated by heat extraction. The thermal path is characterised as a series chain: junction-to-solder-point resistance from the [[datasheet]], then solder-point-to-[[heat-sink|heatsink]] resistance measured with a [[thermocouple]], allowing die temperature to be inferred from input power and sink temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Metal-core PCBs exist specifically to pull heat out of the back of power LEDs, with thermal conductivity options around 1–2 W/m·K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> A worked example at 35 % wall-plug efficiency puts 1.4 W of waste heat per 2.2 W LED into the sink, 11.2 W for an eight-LED strip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup>

Efficacy figures differ sharply between bare emitters and finished products: diffused flat ceiling panels deliver about 50 lm/W while the LEDs inside them are around 120 lm/W, the difference being lost in the light-guide and diffusion stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Retrofit LED tubes have cut consumption from 35–40 W per fluorescent tube to about 17 W, using series-parallel strings presenting roughly 60 V of forward drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> For photography lighting, colour rendering index matters more than raw output; unbranded LEDs have garbage CRI, and name-brand high-CRI strips are required for accurate colour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup>

## Optical properties

Typical viewing angle is around 120°, but relative intensity falls by roughly an order of magnitude at the shallow angles at which a driver views a road surface, so daylight-visible marker LEDs need about half a watt each; a Philips Lumileds Rebel amber part is rated at 350 mA (1 W) and the comparable white part at 700 mA (2 W) for rated flux.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup> Even illumination from point sources requires engineered optics: edge-lit displays use a handful of LEDs firing into a diffusion film or light guide to produce shadow-free light across a whole screen, as in the four-LED Kindle frontlight, and compact products route light from right-angle board emitters through light pipes to the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup>

## LEDs as general-purpose circuit elements

The stable forward drop makes an LED useful as a crude [[voltage-reference]] or offset. A red LED provides about 1.8 V at low current, used to generate the offset in a tracking pre-regulator (with the bonus of acting as a power indicator) and as the fixed-voltage element in a two-[[transistor]] constant-current source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup> LEDs turn up as ordinary diodes in unexpected places, such as a calculator using one for its 1.6–1.85 V drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup> An LED paired with a phototransistor forms an optocoupler; where no commercial part meets the isolation requirement, a discrete LED and phototransistor can be faced across a gap as a homebrew coupler.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup> LEDs respond essentially instantly to current, so a microsecond overload pulse that would light an LED invisibly briefly needs a pulse-stretching stage to hold the indication for about a second for the human eye.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup>

## LEDs as light sensors

The effect is reversible: an LED illuminated by external light generates a photocurrent like a photodiode, usable as a crude light sensor or a source for femtoampere-level current measurements, and this photon interaction underlies low-current emission experiments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup>

## Testing and measurement

[[multimeter]] diode-test mode will light an LED and show its forward drop, but only if the meter's open-circuit test voltage exceeds the LED's threshold; raising the test voltage from 1 V to 5 V is what allows modern white LEDs to be tested.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> Instruments with low test voltages fail entirely — one LCR tweezer cannot forward-bias even a 1.8 V green LED — while 2 mA of test current is enough to verify individual LEDs populated on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> Component testers automatically identify anode and [[cathode]] on arbitrary pinouts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f_SdM6sXHD4" title="FNIRSI SG-003A Process Meter + DSO-TC2 Oscilloscope &amp; Transistor Tester">•</a></sup> SMD LED polarity markings are inconsistent between manufacturers — a notch marking the cathode on one part marks the anode on another — so parts should be physically verified before placement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>

## Displays and multiplexing

Seven-segment LED displays come in common-cathode and common-anode variants, which determines whether a logic one or a logic zero turns a segment on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup> Individual LEDs are cheap enough — about a cent each on a 3,000-piece reel — that discrete LEDs can replace manufactured seven-segment modules, a technique also used historically when 50 discrete LEDs cost less than one display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-142" title="EEVblog #142 - Electronics Magazine Memories">142</a></sup> Pin-limited microcontrollers use charlieplexing to drive disproportionately many LEDs, for example 20 individually addressable LEDs from five I/O pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-556" title="EEVblog #556 - Mailbag">556</a></sup>

Driver-chip current limits dominate multiplexed designs. A shift register rated at 25 mA per output must split that current across every segment it feeds — 40 segments sharing 20 mA get half a milliamp each — so driving many segments simultaneously at outdoor brightness is impossible without per-segment drivers or resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> Parts such as the 74HC595 can drive LEDs and small seven-segment displays directly only at modest currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> Omitting dropper resistors entirely and relying on chip output impedance both limits brightness and raises certification questions about how LED current is being limited at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup>
