RAM is fast volatile read/write memory: the working store a [[processor]] scribbles in while it runs, as distinct from the [[rom|ROM]] or flash that holds the program.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> The distinction between a microprocessor and a [[microcontroller]] rests on it — a microprocessor needs external memory, both RAM and program memory, while a microcontroller has both built in along with the IO.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> That built-in memory is what let a single chip replace a board of parts and drive the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] down far enough to put a microcontroller inside a $20 toy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> Because RAM is volatile, it is also the thing that must be kept alive by a backup cell, or lost when power goes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-947" title="EEVblog #947 - Chronos High Speed Camera Review">947</a></sup>

## The canonical architecture

A vast range of equipment reduces to the same three blocks: a processor, some ROM, some RAM, plus whatever IO the job needs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-641" title="EEVblog #641 - Dumpster Cash Register Teardown">641</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup> A TRS-80 Model I is a Z80 with its RAM and its ROM and essentially nothing else but 74LS series logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup> A dumpster cash register needs a processor, some RAM and some ROM, a thermal printer driver and a communications interface, and little else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-641" title="EEVblog #641 - Dumpster Cash Register Teardown">641</a></sup> The same layout appears in an HP 3457A [[multimeter]] alongside the main processor,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> in a Stanford Research instrument built round a Z80 with ROM, RAM and a GPIB interface,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup> in a 1990-vintage instrument with one RAM and two ROMs implying a 16-bit bus,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1008" title="EEVblog #1008 - Mystery Teardown">1008</a></sup> in an old-school industrial controller around a Hitachi 6303 with an 8255 for peripherals,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> and on an off-the-shelf 6809 controller card carrying serial IO, RAM, ROM and a few miscellaneous chips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> An HP 85 is unusual in having ROM and RAM and no glue logic at all, because HP did all their own custom ASICs rather than the address decoders an IBM PC of the period needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup>

Where double-sided board fabrication was expensive, memory chips were stacked physically on top of one another — RAM stacks or memory stacks — with only the chip select or one address line bent out, since every other pin-out is identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-680" title="EEVblog #680 - Mailbag">680</a></sup> It is ugly but it routes easily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-680" title="EEVblog #680 - Mailbag">680</a></sup>

## Capacities, historically

Early micro RAM was measured in bytes. An Intel MCS-85 8085 design kit shipped with a processor, a monitor ROM, one RAM chip and a keyboard/display controller;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> a second RAM chip brought one example to 512 bytes total, and the extra port expansion added a further 256 bytes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> A Motorola 6804 mask-ROM micro in a 1980s voice recognition product had 1.6K of program ROM and 30 bytes of RAM;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup> the PIC16C57 running a MicroTAC's charge algorithm had a 2K-word one-time-programmable program store and 72 bytes of RAM, one 8-bit timer, and no UART or ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-492" title="EEVBlog #492 - Vintage Motorola MicroTAC Mobile Phone Teardown">492</a></sup> A Hitachi HD 63 P0 had a couple of hundred bytes of RAM and piggyback machine pins on top for the EPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup>

Personal computers of the period were measured in kilobytes. The ZX81 had 1K built in, with the US-market Timex Sinclair 1000 doubling it to 2K,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-872" title="EEVblog #872 - Mailbag">872</a></sup> against a $99 price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-872" title="EEVblog #872 - Mailbag">872</a></sup> An HP 85 came standard with 8K expandable to 64K [[via]] rear expansion packs,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-904" title="EEVblog #904 - Hewlett Packard HP85 Professional Computer">904</a></sup> though at least one example carries 16K on the main board where 8K was the specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup> A TRS-80 Color Computer eventually shipped with 64K at a $399 launch price in 1980 dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1021" title="EEVblog #1021 - Retro Radio Shack TRS-80 Color Computer">1021</a></sup> A Cambridge Z88 had 32K built in, expandable with 128K packs up to around 4 meg.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> An Apple IIc came with 128K and a built-in 140K floppy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-788" title="EEVblog #788 - Apple IIC Teardown">788</a></sup> An IBM PCjr was $669 for 64 kilobytes or $1269 for 128 kilobytes, and in either case came with no storage at all — a large part of why the machine failed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> A fully populated PCjr with the extra 64K card and a 500K expansion reaches 640K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> An Amiga 500 had 512K,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> an Amiga 2000 2 meg in two banks,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Gbn_51IoJiM" title="Amiga 2000 Teardown &amp; Power Up">•</a></sup> a Sharp X68000 1 meg,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-746" title="EEVblog #746 - Sharp X68000 Retro Computer Teardown">746</a></sup> an Acorn Archimedes A3000 2 meg,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup> a Mac SE 4 meg across four SIMM slots at 1 meg per slot,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-414" title="EEVblog #414 - Apple Macintosh SE Teardown">414</a></sup> and an IBM L40SX 18 meg, of which the 16 meg was directly usable and the extra 2 meg available as EMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-639" title="EEVblog #639 - IBM L40SX Retro Laptop Teardown">639</a></sup> An HP 9845B took 512K boards, three of which expanded it to 1.5 meg, while another of its memory boards used 4116 chips organised 16K x 1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1404" title="EEVblog 1404 - ## RAM in modern microcontrollers

Even [[current]] parts are RAM-poor by desktop standards, and the number is a primary selection criterion. A Padauk part in the 150 series has 64 bytes; the next one up doubles that to 128, of which a trivial program used four.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1141" title="EEVblog #1141 - Padauk 3 CENT Micro - Programmer">1141</a></sup> A 3-cent micro in SOT23 carries 64 bytes alongside a [[comparator]] and internal reference but no ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup> An MSP430F2013 in a bench [[power-supply|power supply]] has 2K of flash and 128 bytes of RAM, chosen for its 16-bit sigma-delta ADC with internal reference rather than its memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Larger parts move into kilobytes: an STM32 with an [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]] offers 32K of RAM against 256K of ECC flash,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> an ARM Cortex-M0 board 8K RAM with 64K flash,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-715" title="EEVblog #715 - Mailbag">715</a></sup> an ESP8266 module roughly 64K, enough to run applications without an external micro,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-998" title="EEVblog #998 - How To Program ESP8266 WiFi With Arduino">998</a></sup> a PIC24FJ128GL303 8K,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup> and an STM32-class Cortex-M4 in a smartwatch 256K plus a separate 4K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup> Where a part is executing from RAM rather than flash, its quoted run-mode current can differ sharply — a headline figure measured with code running from RAM may be on par with or worse than a competitor once the code runs from flash.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup>

RAM also turns up outside the processor. An LCD driver chip's internal display RAM can hold the frame data so a memory-limited microcontroller does not have to, and can be read back later for manipulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> A socket-tester [[lcd|LCD]] controller carries 32 by 4 bits of RAM for the same purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup>

## RAM in instruments

In a logic analyser the acquisition RAM is filled continuously through a circular address counter while the instrument waits for a trigger, so pre-trigger data is already captured; the trigger, after any delay, flips a flip-flop and stops the fill.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup> One such design used 32K of memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup> Scope [[memory-depth|memory depth]] is a cost lever: cut-down models appear with RAM omitted from the board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup> or with half the RAM of the model above.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup> A high-speed camera holds a capture in RAM until it is explicitly saved and warns before a new recording discards unsaved data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-947" title="EEVblog #947 - Chronos High Speed Camera Review">947</a></sup>

Magnetic core memory is the ancestor: a sense line reads back the state set or reset in each ferrite ring, with an access time around a microsecond, putting it in the region of 1 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-paQ3zIsz1-8" title="Magnetic Core Memory">•</a></sup>

## Electrical and physical behaviour

RAM is not a passive presence on a board. Under thermal imaging, the RAM in an 8085 design kit runs hotter than the processor itself — 47 °C for the lower device against 42 °C for the upper, with the difference attributed to which one is actually being accessed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3zAWgNjx5Ew" title="Intel MCS-85 Design Kit THERMAL IMAGING">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3zAWgNjx5Ew" title="Intel MCS-85 Design Kit THERMAL IMAGING">•</a></sup> Its address lines are among the most active signals present, which makes them a useful EMC probe target: a near-field probe held over the RAM picks up high-level signal, comparable to holding it over the ROM or the address decoder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup> A single address line from the driving chip to the RAM chip is the natural signal to compare between a two-layer and a four-layer version of the same board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Removing every [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] from the side of a board carrying the instruction register, the RAM and the ROM did not stop the circuit working, though the clock lines were another matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

Physical damage tells: small amounts of surface wash fluid around the legs of RAM chips leave visible residue even in a 12 V section of a board, while the same fluid at 120 V levels elsewhere etched arcs clean through the [[pcb|PCB]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>

## Cost, scarcity and obsolescence

RAM was expensive when these machines were new, and stayed valuable enough that expansion RAM boards turn up with the chips pilfered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> ROM and RAM are by far the most common parts to go obsolete in long-lived instrument designs, and a DDR substitution is not a drop-in: DDR has registers that must be configured, so the [[firmware]] changes with the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 5 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Modern PC practice

Module count and channel configuration matter as much as total capacity. A four-channel board is populated with a set of four 8 GB modules of a matched speed grade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1098" title="EEVblog #1098 - 4K Video Editing PC Build">1098</a></sup> Adding a second 8 GB stick to bring a machine to 16 GB also enables dual-channel operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NLwgFYt1pjA" title="Dumpster HP ProOne 400 PC Touch Screen">•</a></sup> Boards report a memory mismatch when banks disagree,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NLwgFYt1pjA" title="Dumpster HP ProOne 400 PC Touch Screen">•</a></sup> and a BIOS may simply refuse a module it does not like — five beeps on power-up, cured by fitting a different stick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Lhmg1jUNxVc" title="Yet another dumpster dive i7 machine!">•</a></sup> Older machines with DIP switches for memory settings were superseded by BIOSes that auto-detect how much RAM is fitted and ignore the switches entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup>

Because it is one of the few socketed parts left, RAM is both the first thing stripped from a discarded machine and a standard diagnostic swap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-466" title="EEVblog #466 - Dumpster Dive Night Raid">466</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1212" title="EEVblog #1212 - Dumpster Diving PC Motherlode!">1212</a></sup> Swapping the RAM along with the CPU and thermal compound revived one machine, and the original RAM then worked as well, pointing at a contact problem rather than a failed module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-oGNyQ_A5kZI" title="Winner Winner Dumpster Dinner">•</a></sup> A machine that resists every substitution — CPU, power supply, video and memory — indicates a dead motherboard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7mDglCZTC9o" title="Dumpster PC Find">•</a></sup>

The consistent engineering judgment is that capacity requirements are routinely overstated for ordinary use: 8 GB is enough for a general office machine,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-okaYXwzHzoY" title="Dumpster Diving PC&#x27;s">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1342" title="EEVblog #1342 - Asymmetrical Dumpster PC Stacking">1342</a></sup> 16 GB is plenty for anything a small N150-class mini PC will run,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1656" title="EEVblog 1656 - MAILBAG: ElectroPermanent Magnet, DIN PSU&#x27;s, Sponge, Beelink EQ14 N150 PC">1656</a></sup> and 20 GB in a recovered all-in-one is not worth upgrading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-esJN76rykfQ" title="Dumpster Diving 27&quot; iMac Upgrade">•</a></sup> Shipping a machine with only 4 GB, or charging a large premium to move from 4 GB to 8 GB, is treated as a rip-off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kF7sJbAQRHY" title="Microsoft Surface Pro (5) Unboxing &amp; Setup">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1212" title="EEVblog #1212 - Dumpster Diving PC Motherlode!">1212</a></sup> Video rendering is the exception where large capacities genuinely apply, with 128 GB in a dual-Xeon workstation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-871" title="EEVblog #871 - Find Aliens With Your Raspberry Pi!">871</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3J1JdeHBKd8" title="20 Core Dual Xeon Processor Upgrade!">•</a></sup>

Soldered memory is the standing objection to modern designs. Where a machine is made thin enough that neither the RAM nor the storage can be upgraded, the eventual failure of the flash renders the whole product scrap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1407" title="EEVblog 1407 - Right to Repair with iFixit Founder Kyle Wiens">1407</a></sup> Screwed-in and socketed assemblies are preferred wherever the mechanical design allows it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1727" title="EEVblog 1727 - REPAIR: Casio CT-X700 Keyboard">1727</a></sup>

## Backup and retention

Because the contents vanish with power, RAM in portable equipment is commonly held up by a small secondary cell. A Tandy 200's rechargeable [[battery]] exists specifically to protect the RAM,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup> and a Toshiba T1000LE carries a sub-battery of series N cells that keeps RAM alive through a soft power-off and a main battery change so the machine resumes its previous state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> Even after decades, the silicon generally survives: a Compaq Portable board pulled out and run from a bench supply still had working mask ROM and working RAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1354" title="EEVblog #1354 - Compaq Portable Repair - Part 2">1354</a></sup>
