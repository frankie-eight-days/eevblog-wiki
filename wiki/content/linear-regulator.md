A linear regulator is a [[voltage-regulator|voltage regulator]] that holds its output constant by continuously varying the [[resistance]] of a series pass [[transistor]], dissipating the difference between input and output as heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> It is one of the oldest and simplest building blocks in electronics — the three-terminal 7805 and the adjustable [[lm317|LM317]] have been in production for forty years and more and remain the default choice for a fixed low-[[current]] rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Its two defining characteristics follow directly from the topology: it is quiet, because nothing inside it switches, and it is inefficient, because every volt it drops is burned in the pass element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

## Internal topology

Inside a three-terminal linear regulator there is an NPN series pass transistor, an op-amp, a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]], and two feedback [[voltage]]-divider resistors — and that is the entire device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The op-amp compares a divided sample of the output against the reference and drives the pass transistor to null the error, making the whole thing a negative feedback loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> In a fixed-output part such as the 7805 the divider resistors are inside the chip; in an adjustable part such as the LM317 they sit outside and connect through the feedback pin, but the operating principle is identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Voltage dividers of this kind appear in the feedback path of essentially every regulator topology, linear and switching alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

The circuitry differs remarkably little from that of a standard buck switching regulator; the distinction is the mode of operation rather than the parts list.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> A switching pass element is either off or fully saturated and dissipates almost nothing in either state, whereas the linear pass transistor sits in a continuous loop and dissipates continuously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The same loop determines transient response: the time taken for a load change to propagate around the feedback loop and be corrected at the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> A sharp load step therefore takes finite time to compensate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

Regulator ICs also carry protection circuitry — overcurrent, overvoltage, overtemperature — layered around this core, but that does not change the basic operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

## Dropout voltage

Every linear regulator carries a [[dropout-voltage|dropout voltage]] specification: the minimum input-to-output differential below which it falls out of regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> A standard linear regulator may demand two or three volts, which is crippling in low-voltage systems.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> An LM317 is typically taken as a 2 V minimum drop at full current, so a 5 V output needs at least 7 V in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> A 7805 is likewise around 2 V, though the exact figure depends on the specific part and the load current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> Dropout is temperature dependent, and data sheets present it as a parametric curve of input-to-output differential against temperature rather than a single number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

Low-dropout regulators exist to remove this constraint, reaching a few hundred millivolts and in some parts 0.1 to 0.2 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> They achieve it by substituting a PNP pass transistor for the NPN, which permits a lower saturation voltage but complicates loop stability — an LDO is inherently far less stable than a standard NPN linear regulator, and the ESR and value of the [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] must be chosen with care.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> A second LDO penalty is [[ground]] current, which can bear a direct relationship to [[output-current|output current]] and waste useful power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The trade is summarised simply: linear regulators are simpler and more stable, at the cost of a greater [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] between input and output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

Some parts specify dropout in more than one way. The LT3080 quotes both a control-pin dropout and a VIN dropout; the two pins are normally tied together, but a circuit that happens to have a higher control voltage available elsewhere can exploit the separation to obtain a lower input dropout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> It also carries a minimum load current requirement, without which it will not regulate at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

## Dissipation and thermal design

Because the pass transistor drops the full input-to-output difference at the full load current, the dissipation is simply that difference multiplied by the output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> A bench supply rail taking 12 V in, delivering 5 V out at 0.7 A dissipates about 5 W — enough that no small [[heat-sink|heatsink]] will cope, whatever the [[airflow]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Linear regulators found running at 80 °C inside an instrument with ample free internal volume represent a failure of thermal design rather than an inherent limit, even where die-temperature margin remains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> Linear bench supplies mitigate this by switching [[transformer]] secondary taps as the [[output-voltage|output voltage]] is set, so that the pass element never sees the full transformer voltage across it; dissipation and heatsink temperature rise visibly just after a tap changeover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Delivering 3.3 V from a 40 V rectified secondary without tap switching would be untenable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

[[efficiency|Efficiency]] is not intrinsically poor — it is set entirely by the voltage ratio. A linear regulator dropping 5.5 V to 5 V is 90% efficient, comparable to a buck converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

## Current behaviour and battery-powered products

For a linear regulator the input current equals the output current, less only the [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> Quiescent current is typically two orders of magnitude below the load and can be ruled out of a [[battery]]-life estimate on those grounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Physically, current entering a three-terminal regulator can only leave through the output pin or the ground pin, and the ground-pin component is the small amount the regulator needs to do its own job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> A device powered through a linear regulator therefore presents an essentially constant-current load to its battery, regardless of [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] — which is exactly the load condition battery data sheets characterise, though the tabulated currents rarely match the one required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup>

The cost is unusable [[battery-capacity|battery capacity]]. A single-cell lithium polymer battery feeding a 3.3 V LDO with 50 mV of dropout cannot be discharged below about 3.35 V, which moves the effective cutoff far up the discharge curve and strands roughly 45 to 50 percent of the cell's energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> Alkaline cells hold usable energy down to 0.8 V, yet a [[multimeter]] running from three AAAs through linear regulators may stop near 1.2 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> This is accepted deliberately in precision instruments, where measurement performance and simplicity are worth more than the wasted energy; a switching supply near a dual-slope ADC is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> The alternative, where efficiency matters more, is to move from a linear regulator to a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-964" title="EEVblog #964 - Mailbag">964</a></sup>

## Noise, and use in instrumentation

Linear regulators are much quieter than switching regulators, which reduces the [[shielding]] a product needs and is the reason precision instruments use them throughout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup> Bench multimeters, source-measure units, RF signal generators and low-[[noise]] [[oscilloscope]] front ends are built with linear local regulation on the analog side, often several rails' worth of small regulators placed close to the loads they serve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> The engineering position is stated bluntly: "None of that switching regulator rubbish."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> A high-performance linear regulator can be specified at 40 µV of output noise, a figure beyond the reach of ordinary bench measurement gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

Quietness is not the same as [[ripple]] rejection. Ripple on an input rail passes largely through a linear regulator to its output, especially at the frequencies produced by a switching front end, so a 3.3 V linear regulator fed from a noisy [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] will carry that noise forward.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup>

The standard compromise is a tracking pre-regulator: a switching converter whose output follows the required output voltage by only half a volt to a volt, followed by a linear regulator that cleans up the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup> The switching stage delivers the efficiency and keeps the heatsink small, while the linear output stage delivers noise far below what the switcher alone could manage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> The arrangement works with a buck pre-regulator from a rectified mains rail and with a boost pre-regulator ahead of a low-noise linear part in battery-powered designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-329" title="EEVblog #329 - Tracking Pre-Regulator LTspice Simulation Part 2">329</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> A small linear regulator on the output of a switching converter is likewise the usual way to power a high-resolution ADC from a switched rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>

## Stability

The feedback loop can oscillate if the [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] is wrong — wrong value, wrong type, or placed too far from the regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> This applies to linear regulators, LDOs, and to any circuit built around an error amplifier, including voltage-reference-based current sources.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> Manufacturers' recommended output capacitance is a stability requirement rather than a suggestion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Simulation captures the direction of these effects — changing output capacitance visibly changes the loop behaviour — but cannot account for [[pcb|PCB]] parasitics, supply noise, and the other real-world effects, so a simulated loop is no substitute for building and measuring the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Network analysis equipment can be used to measure the stability of an off-the-shelf linear regulator and determine what output capacitors it actually needs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup>

## Parts and cost

The 78xx family is the archetypal jellybean regulator, with the negative-rail 79xx counterparts used to generate the split supplies analog circuits need.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> Adjustable parts such as the LM317 set output voltage with an external divider; the lower [[resistor]] should be kept low enough that the error term stays insignificant — around 220 Ω in typical practice, and certainly under a few hundred ohms to stay within the [[datasheet|data sheet]] specifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Fixed-voltage accuracy for a standard linear regulator is typically 1.5% for a good part and 2 to 3% for an ordinary one, so a part specified at 0.5% is unusual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 1 of 2 - Meterman 37XR Multimeter review">6</a></sup>

At the low end, fixed 3.3 V LDOs are available from Chinese catalogue distributors for around one to one and a half cents in volume, with 300 mA parts available in several packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> This matters because modern [[fpga|FPGAs]] and dense logic need many low-voltage rails — 2.5 V, 1.8 V, 1.2 V, sub-volt cores — and a product may carry three to five regulators, so the per-part cost multiplies through the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> A Zener shunt regulator remains cheaper still, which is why the topology persists, but it draws roughly a thousand times the quiescent current and is understood in industry to be wasteful; linear regulators displaced it as soon as they became available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

Linear regulators also appear as blocks inside larger devices: alongside a [[boost-converter|boost converter]] in a combined step-up plus 500 mA linear regulator IC, and inside automotive power-management chips carrying buck, boost, linear and watchdog functions together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup> Lithium-ion charger ICs are commonly linear types, driving the pass transistor with a DC voltage; the charger operates in constant-current mode by sensing the drop across a shunt, then transitions to behaving as a linear voltage regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> Charge-pump voltage doublers and inverters are frequently followed by a linear or low-dropout regulator to turn a rough output into a clean regulated rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

## Identification in equipment

A linear regulator can generally be identified on a board by the absence of an [[inductor]] near it, since switching converters require one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup> Small SOT-23 and SO-8 parts scattered around a board with only decoupling capacitors nearby, and running cool to the touch, are typically linear regulators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup> Discrete implementations show up in older equipment as a TO-3 or similar series pass transistor bolted to the chassis or heatsink, driven by a separate control circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1153" title="EEVblog #1153 - 1970&#x27;s Programmable Calculator Teardown">1153</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The presence of linear regulation on a secondary side, rather than switching regulation, implies the upstream circuit is only a [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] and filter capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> Product architectures range from a mains transformer plus a single 7805 for a sensor board, to instruments carrying a dozen locally regulated rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-513" title="EEVblog #513 - Mailbag">513</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup>
