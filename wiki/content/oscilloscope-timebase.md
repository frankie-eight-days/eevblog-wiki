The [[time-base|timebase]] of an [[oscilloscope]] is the horizontal axis control: it sets how much time each screen division represents, and so decides what fraction of a signal's behaviour is visible at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup> Setting it is one of the two fundamental acts of using a scope, alongside setting the vertical scale, with the trigger configuration following from both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Almost every other property of a digital scope — capture window, achievable [[measurement-resolution|measurement resolution]], [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]], even whether a fault is visible at all — is downstream of where the timebase is set.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1183" title="EEVblog #1183 - RIFA Madness (Schaffner Repair)">1183</a></sup>

## Divisions, sequences and steps

Timebase settings are conventionally quantised into a 1-2-5-10 sequence, so the coarse horizontal control jumps in large steps rather than moving continuously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Scope Bandwidth Hack Investigation">879</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AVuVCrYMBwk" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2004 Timebase Quirk">•</a></sup> A fine or vernier horizontal control exists to fill in between those steps, allowing the sweep speed to be trimmed to line features up against the graticule instead of accepting the nearest coarse setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Scope Bandwidth Hack Investigation">879</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup>

Departures from the standard sequence occur. The Rohde & Schwarz RTB2004 follows 1-2-5-10 at 1 ns per division and again from 500 ns per division upward to the region of 500 seconds per division, but between them substitutes 20, 40, 80 and 200 nanoseconds per division, with no 100 nanoseconds per division setting available at all; the cause is presumed to lie in the acquisition architecture and [[sample-memory|sampling memory]] depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AVuVCrYMBwk" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2004 Timebase Quirk">•</a></sup> Some low-cost instruments abandon the convention entirely and step in a 1-2-4-8 sequence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1127" title="EEVblog #1127 - Mailbag Monday">1127</a></sup> The Zoyi ZT-703S corrected an over-range step of 1.25 microseconds per division present on the earlier 702, moving to conventional steps, and also fixed a timebase accuracy defect the earlier model showed at 500 nanoseconds per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup>

Timebase accuracy is straightforward to verify against a known [[frequency]]: ten divisions at 1 microsecond per division against a 1 MHz reference, or a 1 Hz signal spanning the screen at 200 milliseconds per division, confirms the sweep is calibrated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup>

## Range

Fast-end limits vary enormously by class of instrument. Pocket and budget scopes reach 5 or 1 nanoseconds per division, though the displayed [[waveform]] at those settings may be worthless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup> Mid-range bench instruments reach a few nanoseconds per division in real time,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> and high-[[bandwidth]] instruments go to 500 picoseconds per division, at which point a 500 MHz sine occupies roughly four divisions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fM4bbKVw2og" title="Uni-T 500MHz 4CH ARB Signal Generator Unboxing">•</a></sup> Equivalent-time sampling extends the effective [[sample-rate|sample rate]] beyond the converter's real-time capability at fast settings, at the cost of no longer acquiring in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup>

The slow end runs to tens or hundreds of seconds per division — 50 seconds per division on some instruments, with others slower still, and 2.5 seconds per division on handheld units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1678" title="EEVblog 1678 - Oscilloscope Trigger Warning">1678</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup>

## Reading measurements from the timebase

With the timebase known, the graticule becomes a ruler. Counting divisions against a horizontal setting of 20 microseconds per division yields a time interval directly — around 95 microseconds in one phase measurement, converting to roughly 34 degrees against a cursor-derived 33 degrees, so a graticule reading lands within about a percent of a cursor measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup> Where a measurement is limited by [[display-resolution|screen resolution]], the remedy is a faster timebase: a 0.2 microsecond resolution improves once the two features of interest are expanded across more of the screen, provided both remain on screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup>

The timebase also fixes the width of the capture window: 1 microsecond per division across ten divisions gives a 10 microsecond window, which against events recurring every 15 milliseconds makes the probability of catching one on any given sweep very low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup> The same arithmetic drives search strategy when hunting a narrow event. A 100 nanosecond pulse is invisible at 5 milliseconds per division and resolves properly at 20 nanoseconds per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1183" title="EEVblog #1183 - RIFA Madness (Schaffner Repair)">1183</a></sup> Conversely, measuring a roughly 10 millisecond idle period at 10 milliseconds per division supplies the number needed to set up a pulse-width trigger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-297" title="EEVblog #297 - Canon LANC Bus Reverse Engineering">297</a></sup>

## Interaction with memory depth and update rate

On a digital scope the timebase, the record length and the waveform update rate are coupled. The slower the timebase, the longer the instrument must acquire to fill its memory, and the lower the update rate falls: one instrument sustained a 52 kHz update rate at 2 nanoseconds per division, 22.7 kHz at 2 microseconds per division and 1.8 kHz at 50 microseconds per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> At 50 milliseconds per division the dead time between acquisitions reached roughly 150 milliseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Headline figures of a million waveform updates per second are therefore meaningless at a slow timebase, where filling memory dominates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Manufacturers' update-rate claims generally hold only in a particular window of timebase settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> Anomalous behaviour exists too: one Tektronix instrument produced the same trigger output regardless of time per division, right up to 10 nanoseconds per division, suggesting the limit lay in the display update rather than in acquisition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup>

Deep memory allows a capture taken at one timebase to be zoomed into afterwards, which is why maximum [[memory-depth|memory depth]] is worth selecting before a single-shot capture of an event that may need later examination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

## Dual timebase and delayed sweep

A dual timebase, also described as a delayed sweep or B timebase, runs a second, faster sweep within a window of the main sweep, so a region of interest can be expanded without losing the overall context.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> On analog instruments the horizontal mode switch selects A, B or mixed operation, with a separate B trigger; the resulting split traces on a powered-up but untested instrument are normal and not a fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Where a full dual timebase was unavailable, sweep magnification of ×5, ×10 or ×50 provided a partial substitute that behaves similarly without the full capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup> A four-channel analog instrument with expanded dual timebase could put eight traces on screen from four inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1194" title="EEVblog #1194 - 1985 Electronics">1194</a></sup>

Most modern digital scopes implement only a zoom window sharing the main timebase, which is not a true dual timebase; the Rigol DS1054Z is an example, and genuinely independent dual timebases remain rare on the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup> Where they exist, each channel gets its own timebase and its own independent trigger, which is more capable than the older alternate-trigger arrangement because a single-shot capture returns both channels simultaneously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup> The implementation cost is why manufacturers avoid it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup> Two cautions apply: functions may be disabled in the mode — one instrument refuses single-shot capture while dual timebase is active — and measurements read visually off screen can be misread if the two channels are on different timebases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup> Entry-level Tektronix TBS models offer dual timebase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1235" title="EEVblog #1235 - How To Align Signals On A Digital Oscilloscope">1235</a></sup> Despite the capability of high-end analog instruments with dual timebases and high bandwidth, single-shot capture on even a low-end digital scope is the more useful facility in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup>

## Slow-timebase pitfalls

Several traps appear only at slow sweep speeds. Acquisition modes such as high-resolution averaging can completely conceal an [[oscillation]] at 20 milliseconds per division, with no on-screen indication that the display is being filtered; speeding the timebase up reveals the signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> [[aliasing|Aliasing]] likewise announces itself through an implausible timebase setting rather than through anything obviously wrong with the trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1127" title="EEVblog #1127 - Mailbag Monday">1127</a></sup> Pre-trigger or pre-arm time becomes significant at slow settings, because the instrument must fill its pre-trigger buffer before it will accept a trigger at all; some instruments provide a front-panel ready indicator for exactly this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1678" title="EEVblog 1678 - Oscilloscope Trigger Warning">1678</a></sup> Roll mode, where the trace scrolls continuously rather than sweeping, is the appropriate choice for watching slow events unfold in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup> A single notch of timebase change can also alter whether an event triggers reliably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup>

## Timebase-dependent faults and bugs

Because so much of a digital scope's signal path is reconfigured by the horizontal setting, a large class of defects is timebase-dependent and identified by sweeping the setting. Display artifacts on one instrument appeared only at settings faster than 50 nanoseconds per division — at 20, 10 and 5 nanoseconds per division — and were traced to the display engine rather than to sampling, since dot mode showed a clean trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> A faulty Tektronix TDS3054 misbehaved below a certain sweep speed and was stable above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-564" title="EEVblog #564 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Teardown Repair">564</a></sup> The Rigol MSO5000 showed captured data present at one timebase setting and absent at the next, and failed to expand about the centred point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup> The Rigol DHO800 lost a single-shot capture taken at 500 milliseconds per division with 25 megapoints of memory as soon as the waveform was moved horizontally, with or without the vernier engaged, suggesting a memory allocation problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EVRdTY4LNhg" title="Rigol DHO800 Disappearing Waveform BUG">•</a></sup> An RTB2004 acquisition defect appeared at 100 milliseconds per division and was not expected at faster settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ott9syzNLuE" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2004 Acquisition Bug Part 2">•</a></sup> The Siglent SDS5000X, driven down through the timebase by pinch-zoom, reached 200 picoseconds per division and then folded back around to the slowest setting of 1,000 seconds per division — a fault the horizontal knob does not exhibit, since it correctly stops at 200 picoseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup>

A dual timebase zoom window that fails to redraw its waveform until the display is otherwise updated is a [[firmware]] defect rather than intended behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup>

## Controls and usability

The horizontal control is the most-used control on the instrument after the vertical, and burying it costs real time at the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Pushable horizontal knobs that re-centre the trace or swap the active window between main and zoom displays are a worthwhile convenience.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> [[touchscreen|Touchscreen]]-only instruments are frequently worse: pinch-zoom on the timebase may be absent entirely, leaving only horizontal panning,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup> or the timebase adjustment may be hidden behind an undocumented tap or press-and-hold gesture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup>

Turning the timebase up while moving the horizontal position is also a standard technique for exposing detail that a slower sweep smears out, such as common-mode [[switching-noise|switching noise]] riding on a supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup>
