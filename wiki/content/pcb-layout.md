PCB layout is the stage of electronic design in which a completed [[schematic]] is turned into a physical board: components are given positions, and the connections between them are realised as copper traces, planes and vias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zyuRcsM0gjI" title="eevBLAB #10 - Why Learn Basic Electronics?">•</a></sup> It is a distinct discipline from circuit design — the two are often performed by different people, and a schematic that is electrically correct can be ruined by a physical implementation that is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Layout determines loop areas, trace impedances, thermal paths, clearances and mechanical fit, so it governs whether a working circuit remains a working product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

## Placement dominates

The routing of traces is the visible output, but the decisive work happens before any trace is drawn. Component placement is repeatedly given as roughly ninety per cent of the job: "PCB layout is like 90% placement" is stated as a settled position, and the same figure is applied to 1980s four-layer digital boards as to modern designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Parts cannot be thrown down arbitrarily and routed afterwards; on a complex board, days may be spent on placement alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> The ratsnest display, which shows unrouted connections as direct lines, is the primary tool for this stage, because crossed bundles between adjacent chips reveal immediately that two parts should be swapped or rotated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

Placement is normally organised by function. A design starts from a high-level block diagram, is broken into building blocks, and the board is laid out in essentially the same arrangement, which also allows individual sections to be shielded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> On dense professional boards the functional grouping is readable from the outside: uneven gaps between otherwise similar memory devices indicate a boundary between functional blocks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> Where a system is genuinely modular, whole sections may be routed separately and then moved in as blocks, and identical sections can have their routing duplicated rather than redone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup> Duplicated channels are laid out identically across channels for exactly this reason, which has the side effect of making the channels easy to compare during repair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> Where a board carries front-panel switches directly, placement is constrained further, since functional groups must follow the physical positions of the controls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1258" title="EEVblog #1258 - MORE Dumpster Wandel And Goltermann!">1258</a></sup>

Chip pinouts are themselves designed to make placement work, with internal blocks arranged so signals flow through the package in stages and produce a sensible board layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup> Where a device's pinout works against the routing — the 74374, whose D inputs and Q outputs are interleaved on both sides of the package — a functionally identical alternative with all inputs on one side and all outputs on the other is preferable purely for layout reasons.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup>

## Loop area and electromagnetic compatibility

The single rule most consistently emphasised is minimising loop area. Keeping loop areas small is described as the golden rule of [[pcb|PCB]] layouts, and applies to the entire [[current]] path — the outgoing conductor, the [[ground]] return, and the bypass capacitors in the power system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The tighter the layout and the tighter the loop area, the fewer problems arise with both EMI emission and susceptibility.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Large loop areas are named as one of the worst things that can be done in layout, and one of the errors beginners make most often; a typical failure is a [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] whose ground return loops the long way around other components before reaching the regulator's ground pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup>

The comparison between two- and four-layer construction resolves the same way: a well-executed two-layer board can outperform a badly executed four-layer board, but the ground and power planes of a four-layer stack make good results easier to reach and make catastrophic errors less likely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Split ground and power planes on a four-layer board reintroduce the problem and are treated as a serious error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Near-field probing gives a practical feedback loop — comparing board revisions with an H-field probe shows whether tightening the loop area actually reduced a particular spectral peak, and the measurement need only be comparative rather than absolute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup> Improved layout is one of the standard measures, alongside common-mode chokes and differential-mode filtering, for reducing conducted emissions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-oVkrc3gF7ns" title="Conducted Mode EMC Emissions Demo">•</a></sup>

Switching converters are the most sensitive case: without tight loops, a converter with internal references can show additional DC and dynamic switching errors under load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Layout is also capable of undoing exceptional component performance — a film [[capacitor]] with 25 to 40 picohenries of internal [[inductance]] can easily be outperformed by the inductance of the traces reaching it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Poor layout with signals crossing ground paths produces visible [[switching-noise|switching noise]] on supply rails independently of whether bypass capacitors are fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Bypassing

Bypass capacitors are placed around the outside of a chip and aligned in vertical and horizontal rows, both for routing sanity and as a matter of craftsmanship.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Where a [[datasheet|data sheet]] specifies several different capacitor values in parallel, laying them out in a neat row on the outside of the package satisfies a design review but not the electrical intent; the capacitors should be as close as possible to the power pins, on the underside of the chip if necessary, even at the cost of fitting fewer of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup> On fine-pitch parts the difficulty is fanning out the signal traces while keeping the capacitors close, and the compromise is often to group them clear of the escape routing — which works, but is not optimal, and higher-performance designs move to six or eight layers to avoid the trade-off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-464" title="EEVblog #464 - Mailbag">464</a></sup> Data sheets for switching and regulator parts routinely include layout requirements and example layouts, and following them is offered as the most direct way to learn how a particular circuit should be laid out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The layout section of an ADC data sheet is the authoritative source for questions such as where analogue and digital grounds should meet — at the chip, typically — and a part whose data sheet offers no layout guidance is a reason to choose a different part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup>

## Layers, jumpers and traditional two-layer practice

The traditional two-layer arrangement runs traces predominantly horizontally on one side and vertically on the other, an approach visible on machines from the 1980s onward and still used on modern two-layer designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1080" title="EEVblog #1080 - Gigatron TTL RISC Kit Computer Review">1080</a></sup> On single- and double-sided boards, jumper links are the measure of quality: getting to zero jumper links is described as the holy grail of any PCB layout engineer, and the number of visible links is used as the test of whether a double-sided board was laid out well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> A board that has to jump a ground connection right across itself because a multi-layer stack was unavailable is evidence of that constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> Older single-sided consumer boards frequently accepted large numbers of hand-fitted jumper wires because manual labour was cheaper than a second copper layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup>

Clock rate sets how much of this matters. A machine running at 2.4 MHz tolerates a sprawling layout with long inductive traces and ceramic bypass capacitors scattered anywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup> At the other extreme, a board carrying a 33.8 picosecond edge makes layout critical, and a clean eye diagram on a high-speed link is the visible result of correct controlled-[[impedance]] traces, correct terminations, correct [[shielding]] and low clock jitter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-340" title="EEVblog #340 - USB 3.0 Eye Diagram Measurement">340</a></sup> Even the PCB traces feeding a fast pulse generator have enough inductance to distort the edge and produce [[overshoot]] and undershoot if laid out carelessly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup> For memory interfaces, a full timing budget is often unnecessary on modest designs; matching trace lengths and stopping there is the practical position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> A clock routed too far from the registers it drives, through additional gates and paths, is the classic way [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]] becomes a design problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

## Routing techniques

Several techniques recur. Rotating a fine-pitch quad package by 45° allows signals to escape from all sides where a square orientation leaves no room, and is standard practice on size-constrained boards such as graphics cards and dense motherboards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1171" title="EEVblog #1171 - Yihau WEP SMD Rework Station Meltdown!">1171</a></sup> Pin swapping — reassigning [[microcontroller]] or [[fpga|FPGA]] pins to suit the routing — is a routine way to relieve a trace that cannot otherwise be brought where it needs to go, though it costs effort later in the pin-mapping layer of the [[firmware]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup> Components can be moved to the underside of the board where vertical space allows, and connectors or chips can be mounted in cutouts or upside down when the pinout makes the conventional orientation force extra layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup>

Trace widths carry information. Since a competent layout engineer routes all members of a bus at the same width, a trace that is noticeably thicker than its neighbours is a deliberate exception and marks a different net.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> A trace can be bypassed altogether: for a single high-current connection, an ordinary half-ounce or one-ounce board plus a heavy gauge wire soldered on at production is an acceptable solution, carries large currents, and can be snaked around components on an otherwise dense board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Where routing space runs out entirely, designers have used a switch body or metal case as a jumper, which works but creates a dependency on the mechanical part for electrical continuity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GoKbPDADG0c" title="The Incredible Switch Replacement Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1219" title="EEVblog #1219 - Don&#x27;t Trust Switches - Toy Repair">1219</a></sup>

Layout is iterative rather than single-pass. Rip-up and retry is normal, changes are made continuously, and there is sometimes value in beginning the layout before the schematic is finished.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-245" title="EEVblog #245 - PSU Design Part 10 - PCB Layout Editing">245</a></sup> A final tidying pass removes the residual fragments left behind by pushing and shoving traces around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup> Traces that arrive at a pad at an awkward angle are treated as poor layout even when electrically connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup> The last ten to twenty per cent of routing is the hard part: early routes are easy to place, but once the board is 80 to 90 per cent routed, remaining connections have to jump between layers repeatedly to find a path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Selective routing performed without pre-routing the critical nets first tends to lock the layout in and block later work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup>

## Design rules and manufacturability

[[design-rule-checking|Design rule checking]] catches clearance violations — for instance traces failing a 10 thou clearance requirement — and polygons that have become too thin to carry their intended current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-245" title="EEVblog #245 - PSU Design Part 10 - PCB Layout Editing">245</a></sup> Fabricator rules extend beyond trace width and spacing to pad-to-track and [[via]]-to-track specifications, which can be laborious to program into the CAD tool and can cause a board to be rejected outright after the layout work is complete.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup> [[ground-plane|Ground plane]] pullback is set before flood filling, to a value that leaves acceptable spacing between the fill and pads, and the appropriate value is specific to the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> Manufacturers modifying supplied [[gerber|Gerber]] data can break the design: expanding a ground plane around pads has been observed to close an intentional gap in a copper path, redirecting current away from where the designer relied on it flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup>

Assembly imposes its own constraints. [[solder-mask|Solder mask]] must be present between fine-pitch pins, and [[solder-mask-expansion|solder mask expansion]] must be set correctly, or hand and machine [[soldering]] both become harder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Deliberately omitting mask expansion in a confined area can force a trace to be routed around rather than through, and the result — bare copper between pads — is readily mistaken for a fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> Annular rings that are needlessly small on a board with no density pressure make [[hand-soldering|hand soldering]] difficult for no benefit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> Boards laid out for [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]] should be oriented for a known direction of travel so that [[solder]] drags across IC pins correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> Reflow profiles are affected by layout: large ground planes with inadequate thermal relief change how the board heats and can cause components to tombstone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> Ceramic capacitors placed too close to mounting points can crack when the board flexes during assembly, which has [[led]] to fires in shipped products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Thermal pads that are not designed correctly cause parts to be pulled out of alignment by the surface tension of the solder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>

High-[[voltage]] and mains sections require explicit clearance. Silkscreened dimensions such as a 1.5 mm minimum around high-voltage input components, or a 9 mm input clearance, function as design notes from the circuit designer to the layout engineer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Isolation slots are the standard means of increasing creepage between mains phases where the copper clearance alone is marginal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup> Where a spark gap is wanted, simply removing solder mask between two conductors is the easy and lazy layout solution — crude but effective — and better-controlled geometries exist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup>

## The layout role

On a complex product the schematic may be divided among several engineers, but the board is usually laid out by one person, because collaborative layout works better in theory than in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup> The layout engineer routes to the netlist and is not necessarily the circuit designer; electrical knowledge is required, but detailed understanding of what the circuit does is not guaranteed, which is one route by which functional errors survive into hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup> Notes on the schematic are the mechanism by which intent is transmitted — a star ground marked explicitly, a note that three parallel capacitors must be arranged in a particular physical configuration, a minimum clearance figure — and a good designer writes them down partly because the layout may be done months later by themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Laying out a board someone else designed is harder than laying out one's own, where the circuit is already held in mind along with the modular structure and the mechanical arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

Independent layout contracting exists as a profession, taking customer schematics with their footprint and placement requirements and returning a routed board; a DDR3-bearing design of moderate complexity took in excess of about 200 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup> The field is under pressure because companies increasingly expect their circuit engineers to lay out their own boards, and those engineers, while capable, are frequently inexperienced at it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup> A month or two of work for a single complex board is not unusual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

## Mechanical and system constraints

Layout is rarely free of the enclosure. Deciding the case, the user-interface components, the connectors and the [[heat-sink|heatsink]] before starting the board constrains the layout substantially, and produces a shaped board with cutouts rather than a simple rectangle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> The traffic runs in both directions: a mechanical designer may demand a cutout through the board to gain height clearance for a component, forcing everything to be routed around it, and a PCB designer may have to tell the case designers what the board requires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-937" title="EEVblog #937 - Retro Canon Still Camera Teardown!">937</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> Slots for coaxial cables, angled LEDs to suit a [[light-pipe|light pipe]], thermal pads aligned across multiple chips, and brackets designed into the board outline are all evidence of coordination between layout and mechanical engineering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> When the case dimensions are fixed first and prove insufficient, the layout engineer is forced into compromises such as splitting a duplicated channel's circuitry into a remote area of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-969" title="EEVblog #969 - Vintage $80k NLE Video Editor Teardown">969</a></sup>

Cost and layout interact directly. Adding a dedicated display driver next to the [[lcd|LCD]], rather than routing all segment lines back to a larger microcontroller, is cheaper and may allow a two-layer board where a four-layer board would otherwise be required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> Conversely, extreme density forces layer count upward, with ten or twelve layers used to fit the passives on compute modules.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1019" title="EEVblog #1019 - Mailbag">1019</a></sup> A good layout designer can generally make room for an extra [[resistor]] in order to eliminate a bill-of-materials line item.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

## Autorouting

Autorouters have a narrow domain of usefulness. On 1980s digital boards where devices were placed in neat rows and all traces on one layer ran in a single direction, early autorouters handled the task without difficulty, and this remains the standard example of the technique working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup> Good placement is the precondition: with placement done properly, the automatic routing routines work fine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Selective autorouting of non-critical sections, such as connections between two chips where [[signal-integrity|signal integrity]] is not critical, is a legitimate time-saver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup>

Manual routing generally produces a better board, and the value of an experienced human lies in anticipating the layout far ahead — reserving space for routes that will be needed a thousand connections later, held in the designer's head rather than encoded as constraints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup> Autorouted sections remain identifiable afterwards by right-angled traces and paths no human would draw, and mixed boards showing both automatic and manual routing are common.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup> Even a modern cloud autorouter presented with a competently placed, unrouted board is measured against the same standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup> The prevailing engineering judgment is that layout deserves care as craft rather than as a task to be automated away: "take some pride in your layout. It's an artwork."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup>

## Documentation on the board

[[silkscreen|Silkscreen]] is part of the layout. Functional blocks may be labelled on the reverse where the top side is too crowded to carry them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup> Where bypass capacitors are packed too densely for their designators to sit beside them, a common approach is to place the designators in clear space with silkscreen lines pointing to the parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-720" title="EEVblog #720 - Mailbag">720</a></sup> Parts intentionally left unpopulated can be marked "no load" directly on the silkscreen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup> Row and column coordinates printed around the board edge allow a [[service-manual|service manual]] to identify a failed device by grid reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup> Designators placed inside a component outline instead of outside it, or scattered asymmetrically where symmetrical placement was possible, are treated as poor layout practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup> Untented vias are appreciated because they permit probing without scraping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup>

Layout quality is judged on readable criteria: compact use of area without large wasted regions, sensible placement, short direct traces, no connections looping the long way around the board, consistent alignment, and adequate [[via-stitching|via stitching]] between ground regions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> The converse — crowded [[input-protection|input protection]], flying wires added as afterthoughts, a high-speed interface [[connector]] placed far from the devices it serves, or a real-time clock crystal separated from its [[battery]] by the width of the board — is identified as a layout that was not planned.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup>

## Tools and history

Before CAD, boards were drawn physically. Pre-formed pad shapes were stuck onto clear film and tracks routed by hand with [[adhesive]] tape, or drawn directly onto copper with a resist pen; Rubylith and pad coupons for DIP packages were the professional equivalent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-54" title="EEVblog #54 - Electronics - When I was a boy...">54</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 2 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Magazine layouts were transferred via photocopier onto transparency for home etching.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-142" title="EEVblog #142 - Electronics Magazine Memories">142</a></sup> Hand-taped artwork is recognisable by its curved traces rather than 45° segments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1330" title="EEVblog #1330 - Mailbag with GUEST LABS!">1330</a></sup> Modern practice uses CAD packages including Altium Designer and KiCad, with facilities such as polar coordinates for boards with circular connector patterns.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup> Programmable shortcut overlays speed the work at the cost of dependence on the specific machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1079" title="EEVblog #1079 - Mailbag">1079</a></sup> Tool defects matter at this stage: a schematic that passes an incomplete [[electrical-rules-checking|electrical rules check]] can be carried into layout carrying gross errors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5eJorvyA708" title="Altium Designer Serious ERC bug">•</a></sup>

## Learning the discipline

Layout is described as difficult to teach, because much of it rests on experience and on an innate ability to think about the whole system and how the parts fit together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> The recommended exercise is to take an existing complete design, blank the board, and lay it out from scratch — preferably building the result and confirming that it works.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Breadboards are adequate for confirming that a circuit functions in principle, but only up to roughly a megahertz, and a circuit that is marginal on a [[breadboard]] because of inter-contact [[capacitance]] will usually improve substantially once implemented with tight ground and power loops on a real board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> A circuit that cannot be made even semi-stable on a breadboard, however, is a warning that the design itself is at fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> Re-laying out an existing open-source project to fit a proper enclosure is offered as a way to demonstrate finished engineering ability rather than prototype-level work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KJEmBtvZrPU" title="eevBLAB #78 - Engineering Student Job Advice">•</a></sup>
