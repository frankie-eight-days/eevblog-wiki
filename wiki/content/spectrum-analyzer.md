A spectrum analyzer is an RF test instrument that displays signal amplitude against [[frequency]], operating in the frequency domain where an [[oscilloscope]] operates in the time domain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1661" title="EEVblog 1661 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 5: Time Domain vs Frequency Domain">1661</a></sup> Functionally it is a specialized radio receiver: "a spectrum analyzer is really just a radio receiver" in which "the last detector stage is a logarithmic amplifier instead of a linear voice detector", giving the instrument its wide decibel-scaled dynamic range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-575" title="EEVblog #575 - DIY 1970s Spectrum Analyser">575</a></sup> It is the standard tool for measuring carrier frequencies, harmonics, spurious emissions, modulation, noise floors, and filter responses, and is central to EMC pre-compliance work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup>

## Operating principle

The classic swept-tuned architecture follows a superheterodyne signal flow: a 50-ohm RF input feeds an input attenuator, then filtering, then a mixer driven by a swept local oscillator, producing an intermediate frequency that is filtered by the resolution [[bandwidth]] (RBW) filter, detected, and displayed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup> Modern instruments are "all digital IF": the IF output is digitized by an ADC rather than detected in analog circuitry, in contrast to older fully analog designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> In one representative low-cost design, a 40 MSa/s ADC constrains the IF baseband to below 20 MHz by the Nyquist criterion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup>

Because relative magnitudes span many decades, the vertical scale must be logarithmic; a linear [[voltage]] scale is unusable for typical signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup> Resolution bandwidth trades directly against sweep speed: a narrow RBW such as 300 Hz sharply slows sweep and even front-panel cursor response, while widening to 10 kHz restores near-real-time behavior.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup> The same trade-off applies in RF-scanner instruments modeled on spectrum analyzers, where a wider bandwidth setting yields faster scans.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup>

## Internal construction

Spectrum analyzer main boards are laid out as identifiable functional blocks, typically with distributed-element filters and machined shielding between stages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup> Common construction elements include:

- A 50-ohm input section with [[relay]] or solid-state switching (e.g. SOT23-6 switch packages with internal 50-ohm termination) to insert or bypass a preamplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup>
- [[input-protection]] ratings that vary by instrument; one low-cost model specifies a maximum input of +20 dBm and 50 V DC, unusually tolerant, while others are easily damaged and require care when connecting a device under test that may carry DC or transient energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-323" title="EEVblog #323 - Rigol Scratch&#x27;n&#x27;Sniff">323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup>
- Machined aluminum shielding enclosures in which each circuit block sits in its own milled cavity, with gold-plated contact surfaces and RFI gasketing, so that signals pass between blocks only through machined slots — a technique found in virtually all gigahertz-range RF equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup>
- A physically separate tracking generator board, since crosstalk between the sweep generator and the measurement path must be eliminated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup>
- A local oscillator/VCO, for example a Hittite HMC429 covering 4.45–5 GHz in one design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

## Swept, real-time, and direct-sampling designs

A traditional analyzer sweeps a resolution bandwidth filter across the span sequentially. A real-time spectrum analyzer instead captures contiguous chunks of spectrum — 85 MHz or 110 MHz wide in one product line — performing up to 50,000 discrete Fourier transforms per second and overlaying the results in a persistence display where color indicates spectral intensity, allowing transient signals to be seen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup> Real-time instruments exist up to at least 20 GHz bandwidth with roughly 292,000 captures per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup>

Advancing ADC sample rates have pushed direct RF sampling into the single-digit gigahertz range, eliminating the mixer/LO chain below that frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032  Part 4 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> The Tektronix MDO3000 exploits this by feeding its RF front end straight into the oscilloscope's existing high-sample-rate ADC: high oversampling yields roughly 57 dB of signal-to-noise improvement and a 107 dB [[noise-floor]] from an 8-bit converter, where a conventional analyzer sampling at 20 MSa/s would need a 12.5-bit ADC for equivalent performance. The incremental parts cost of the 3 GHz spectrum analyzer function was estimated at US$25–30, though the shared ADC means the RF channel and analog channels cannot be captured simultaneously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup>

## Tracking generators and network analysis

A tracking generator is a swept source locked to the analyzer's sweep, allowing direct measurement of a filter's [[frequency-response]] — effectively a scalar network analyzer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> This is the easy way to obtain a Bode-style amplitude-versus-frequency plot of an RF filter; equivalent measurements without a tracking generator are possible but considerably harder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> Because the tracking generator in a low-cost instrument costs only a couple of hundred dollars, its output is not ruler-flat across the range — a measured low-pass filter response may show stopband recovery of 10 dB or more at wide spans that is an artifact of the generator rather than the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup> The tracking generator option is cheap enough relative to its utility that omitting it when buying an analyzer is poor economy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-323" title="EEVblog #323 - Rigol Scratch&#x27;n&#x27;Sniff">323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup>

The Siglent SVA1015X extends this idea into a two-port vector network analyzer by multiplexing the existing spectrum analyzer measurement hardware to measure power reflected back from the tracking generator output, adding only directional couplers, switches, and extra [[coaxial-cable|coax]] plus software — a low-cost way to obtain S11 magnitude and phase in a conventional analyzer form factor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope FFT versus dedicated instruments

An oscilloscope's [[fft]] math function converts it into a rudimentary spectrum analyzer, with usefulness determined by the number of FFT points and the quality of the implementation; a 128k-point FFT can give excellent resolution, while small-point-count implementations are little more than toys that show a signal exists but cannot characterize it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup> As engineering judgment, "there's really no substitute for a proper spectrum analyzer" among scope FFT functions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> Nonetheless, a fast, high-point-count FFT combined with a US$10 DIY near-field probe is adequate for basic EMC sniffing without a dedicated analyzer, and an SDR [[usb]] dongle can serve as a "poor man's spectrum analyzer".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup>

Mixed-domain oscilloscopes occupy a middle position. The Tektronix MDO4000 contains a true spectrum analyzer RF front end with an N-connector input — "It is not just an FFT function and that can't be stressed enough" — integrated with the time-domain acquisition so the RF spectrum can be correlated with individual time points and used as a trigger source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> The later MDO3000 achieves a 3 GHz analyzer almost for free in hardware by reusing the scope ADC, with performance comparable to entry-level standalone analyzers, but sacrifices simultaneous RF/analog capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

## EMC pre-compliance testing

A major application of low-cost spectrum analyzers is in-house EMC pre-compliance testing, avoiding repeated multi-thousand-dollar laboratory sessions by allowing before-and-after comparison of design changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> For conducted emissions, a line [[impedance]] stabilization network (LISN) is inserted in series with the supply cable, presenting a standardized 50-ohm impedance as specified by CISPR and coupling the noise to the analyzer's RF input through a BNC port, with gas discharge tube and MOV protection for the analyzer front end; the setup sits on a single large [[ground-plane]], since trailing cables off the plane corrupt the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup> For radiated troubleshooting, near-field probes — H-field loops for magnetic and E-field tips for electric measurements, typically through a 20 dB, 3 MHz–3 GHz preamplifier — locate offending traces and components; H-field probes are orientation-dependent, picking up fields in the loop plane, which can be exploited to isolate sources.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1273" title="EEVblog #1273 - EMC Near Field vs Far Field Explained">1273</a></sup> An industry-standard 120 kHz RBW EMI filter option and quasi-peak detection give ballpark agreement with laboratory measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup>

A cheap general-purpose analyzer will not reproduce the results of a US$50,000 EMC measurement receiver, but gives a reasonable indication sufficient for pre-compliance iteration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> True EMC test receivers span extremely wide ranges (one example covers 20 Hz to 40 GHz) and implement standard-specific detectors — quasi-peak, averaging, and RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup> Related knowledge: spread-spectrum clocking deliberately dithers a system clock to smear a spectral peak — a flat line at, say, 100 MHz becomes a broad hump — lowering peak emitted energy to pass EMC limits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup>

## Operation and measurement practice

- Unlike on oscilloscopes, where auto-set is disdained, the auto button on a spectrum analyzer is a reasonable way to get a first look at an unknown signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>
- Zero-span mode stops the sweep at a fixed center frequency so amplitude versus time can be observed, allowing modulation to be examined.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>
- Burst or intermittent transmitters are captured with single-shot trigger sweep modes; carrier harmonics can be found simply by re-centering on multiples of the fundamental.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>
- Noise-density measurements require selecting a power spectral density measurement type, not merely changing vertical units, to obtain volts-per-root-hertz scaling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup>
- Low-cost portable spectrum analyzers have historically been used with simple antennas — even a dangled wire — to verify transmitter carriers, e.g. a 27.15 MHz CB carrier or a 121.5 MHz EPIRB burst.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-839" title="EEVblog #839 - Mailbag">839</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>
- Sensitivity of a simple homebrew design is modest: roughly 10–20 microvolts, compared with microvolt-level ham receivers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-575" title="EEVblog #575 - DIY 1970s Spectrum Analyser">575</a></sup>

## Frequency coverage

General-purpose benchtop spectrum analyzers are RF instruments; a typical low-cost unit covers 9 kHz to 1.5 GHz and is of no use at audio or other low frequencies, where oscilloscope-based methods or a frequency response analyzer are needed instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> Dynamic signal analyzers — often called FFT analyzers — fill the low-frequency gap, operating down to DC for vibration and acoustic measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> At the upper end, handheld field instruments reach 20 GHz (the Agilent N9344C covers 1 MHz–20 GHz), and real-time and [[calibration]]-lab analyzers extend to 26.5, 40, or 50 GHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1041" title="EEVblog #1041 - Keysight Calibration Lab Tour">1041</a></sup>

## Historical and homebrew instruments

In the late 1970s a capable Hewlett-Packard spectrum analyzer was unaffordable to individuals, prompting homebrew construction: one surviving example is a dead-bug/Manhattan-style receiver design with a 10.7 MHz IF and logarithmic detector, roughly 70 dB of display range, and a top frequency near 200 MHz, used successfully for repeater cavity tuning and for tracking a 160 MHz microprocessor leakage failure in a commercial traffic-light controller down to the offending board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-575" title="EEVblog #575 - DIY 1970s Spectrum Analyser">575</a></sup> Later kit designs repurposed electronically tuned TV tuners as the front end, with the builder supplying the back end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-575" title="EEVblog #575 - DIY 1970s Spectrum Analyser">575</a></sup> A low-cost commercial alternative of the kit era was the Dick Smith VHF/UHF spectrum analyzer, used with an oscilloscope for radio alignment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KKEYAdXEW-M" title="Dick Smith Kits">•</a></sup>
