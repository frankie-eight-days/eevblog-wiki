A differential amplifier is an amplifier that measures the [[voltage]] difference between its two input terminals and multiplies that difference by a gain, without reference to [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> There is essentially no ground reference at the input: a differential amplifier with a gain of 10 fed 1 V of differential signal produces 10 V out regardless of where that pair of inputs sits in the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> It is one of the most-used analogue building blocks in instrumentation, appearing wherever a small signal must be pulled out from on top of a large or noisy reference — [[current]] shunts, thermocouples, strain gauges, [[oscilloscope]] front ends and high voltage probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup>

## Relationship to the operational amplifier

An op-amp is itself a differential amplifier: two inputs, one output, and an internal gain applied to the difference between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> That gain — the open-loop or large DC voltage gain, quoted in data sheets in dB and working out to roughly 100,000 for a common part — is enormous, drifts with temperature and varies between devices, which is exactly why a bare op-amp cannot be used as a practical differential amplifier despite being one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The gain has to be tamed with negative feedback before it becomes useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

Taking the inverting configuration and replacing the fixed bias voltage on the non-inverting input with a second signal input turns the circuit into a differential amplifier whose gain, as in the inverting case, is set by the [[resistor]] ratio −R2/R1: 10 k over 1 k gives a gain of −10, so 1 V and 1.1 V at the inputs — a 0.1 V difference — yield −1 V out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The circuit is modest but serviceable: "It's not a terrific differential amplifier, but it works."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> This single op-amp topology is the default roll-your-own diff amp and turns up throughout commercial designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup>

## Topologies

Beyond the single op-amp version, several arrangements recur:

- **Three op-amp (instrumentation amplifier).** The traditional form used in cold junction compensation ICs, where the three op-amp differential amplifier is packaged alongside a trimmed internal [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]] and a compensation network matched to a particular [[thermocouple]] type such as type K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> An instrumentation amplifier responds to differential variation between two conductors while ignoring common mode changes entirely, so substantial common mode [[noise]] on the pair appears nowhere at the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-616" title="EEVblog #616 - How Microphone Phantom Powering Works">616</a></sup>
- **Discrete [[transistor]].** Discrete differential amplifiers are common in analogue video and RF gear, including vacuum tube implementations in older microwave equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XqakD0dXdjM" title="Guest Video: Kerry Wong - HP493A 8GHz Microwave RF Amplifier Teardown">•</a></sup> Matched dual transistor pairs — parts intended for ultra-low-noise audio work — are used to give a well-matched differential input stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Scope front ends favour a matched JFET pair as a roll-your-own JFET input differential amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup>
- **Dedicated parts.** Off-the-shelf instrumentation amplifiers such as the AD620 buy precision at a price, and are not always worth it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Purpose-built [[current-sensing|current sense]] amplifiers are differential amplifiers with a fixed gain: a MAX4080F measures across a high-side shunt at a fixed gain of five to 0.1% and outputs a ground-referenced voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Some converters integrate them, as with a three-channel 16-bit sigma-delta part carrying built-in differential amplifiers and a 4 ppm reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup>

The choice between an instrumentation amplifier and a rolled-own differential amplifier built from op-amps turns on signal level: for very low-level differential sources such as a strain gauge, the instrumentation amplifier — often with adjustable gain — is the right part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> The choice between a differential and a single-ended front end is a cost and geometry question; where the [[current-shunt|current shunt]] sits on the same board right next to the amplifier chip, the differential input earns less.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Differential amplifiers are also intrinsically noisier than a good single-ended amplifier, which is a real argument against reaching for one by default.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-616" title="EEVblog #616 - How Microphone Phantom Powering Works">616</a></sup>

## Common mode rejection

Any differential amplifier circuit, whether an op-amp or a discrete transistor design, has a [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|common mode rejection ratio]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> CMRR is simply the ratio of the amplifier's differential gain to its common mode gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> It is normally taken on faith from the [[datasheet|data sheet]] rather than verified, though it can be measured directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

In a high voltage differential probe — which is nothing more than a differential amplifier with a gain of ×10 or ×100 chosen to offset the input attenuation — the dominant limit on CMRR is the matching of the input [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] strings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> If one string is unbalanced against the other, CMRR suffers, which is why such probes carry a CMRR trimmer alongside a gain trimmer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> Ordinary component tolerance in the divider and in the amplifier gain is enough to throw a CMRR reading well out unless every element is tweaked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> At higher frequencies the divider resistors become hard to compensate and the response degrades; above 10 MHz, rejection falls off sharply, which is why manufacturers stop quoting figures at 50 or 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Dez9KG6whb0" title="CMRR Followup Micsig DP10007 vs HVP70">•</a></sup>

The rolloff of rejection with [[frequency]] is not a differential-specific pathology — it is ordinary amplifier behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

Measurement wiring matters as much as the amplifier. Untwisted input leads let the common mode signal differ between the two conductors, and the resulting error can wreck a low-level measurement regardless of whether the front end is a probe, an op-amp differential amplifier or a discrete transistor circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

## Current measurement

The most common application is reading the voltage across a current shunt. A very low value shunt — 0.1 Ω or 10 mΩ — is placed in series with the load so that it dissipates little in normal operation, and a differential amplifier across it converts the shunt drop into a usable, ground-referenced signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> With a 1 Ω shunt, 1 A produces 1 V across the resistor and 1 V at the amplifier output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

A single op-amp differential amplifier is often good enough here. In a bench supply design an LM358 was used deliberately in place of a precision low-offset part, because the required current resolution did not justify the precision; the LM358's several millivolts of offset was then compensated by raising the shunt value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> The same output feeds a [[microcontroller]] ADC channel directly, giving 0 to 1 V for 0 to 1 A, or 1 mV per milliamp, off a 1 Ω shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Combined with an INA219, that arrangement produced a five-range [[current-measurement|current measurement]] capability spanning 1 A down to 10 µA resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> A similar µSupply design pairs a 1 Ω shunt with an ordinary rail-to-rail automotive-grade op-amp as the differential amplifier rather than a dedicated high-side current shunt amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

The same pattern recurs across instruments and consumer hardware: a 10 mΩ four-terminal sense resistor feeding a differential amplifier in a [[multimeter]]<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup>, a four-terminal shunt in a 600 V PSU routed back to a diff amp<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>, AMP-03 differential amplifiers reading the output shunt in a source measure unit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup>, an external diff amp with a gain of 50 on a development board<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup>, and integrated shunt-plus-differential-amplifier front ends inside lithium [[battery]] charger ICs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Where the shunt and its amplifier are separated by a [[connector]] and cable rather than sitting together, the arrangement works but is not the preferred practice: the differential sense amplifier belongs right at the shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>

[[battery-capacity|Battery capacity]] measurement needs two differential amplifiers — one measuring the cell voltage directly across the cell, one measuring current — with both logged over time so watt-hours can be accumulated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>

## Signal chains and front ends

Oscilloscope input channels are built around a differential stage. A typical front end AC-couples the input into a JFET amplifier with a low-[[impedance]] emitter-follower output, which then drives the differential amplifier whose differential output goes straight into the ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> [[bandwidth-limit|Bandwidth limiting]] is sometimes implemented inside the differential amplifier itself, by switching series [[resistance]] in the stage — a 200 Ω versus 680 Ω difference between otherwise identical paths marking the 20 MHz [[bandwidth]] filter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Because the differential amplifier drives the downstream ADC board from a low source impedance, running that analogue signal across inner layers to another board is not an interference problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup>

High voltage differential probes follow a fixed architecture: high voltage resistor divider string, FET input buffer, differential amplifier, then an output driver into the scope BNC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1414" title="EEVblog 1414 - MicSig DP10007 High Voltage Probe - Turning it up to 11">1414</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> Because the two halves are symmetric, [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]] one half is sufficient — the other is a duplicate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> Implementations vary in the input devices, from discrete FETs to high-speed FET input op-amps such as the THS4631.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1414" title="EEVblog 1414 - MicSig DP10007 High Voltage Probe - Turning it up to 11">1414</a></sup> A probe lacking that structure — no differential input, no FET input into a differential amplifier configuration — is not a differential probe at all, whatever its other merits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

Elsewhere, differential amplifiers appear as the low-noise input stage of a power meter, shielded inside a metal can, with the amplified low-impedance output driven out to the ADC where interference no longer matters<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>; as the video input stage of an analogue TV IF modulator, where it prevents hum from being superimposed on the video by hum current in the ground line<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup>; in delay line output amplification<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup>; in timer-counter input stages alongside comparators and dividers<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup>; and as the front end of ECG instrumentation, where the useful probing point is the final scaled output rather than the differential amplifier itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1008" title="EEVblog #1008 - Mystery Teardown">1008</a></sup>
