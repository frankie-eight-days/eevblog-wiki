Desoldering is the removal of [[solder]] from a joint in order to free a component, a [[connector]], or an entire sub-board. It is the gating operation in most repair and teardown work: a board that cannot be lifted cannot be probed, and a suspect part that cannot be removed cannot be confirmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> It is also the basis of component salvage, and a routine step in reverse-engineering a board whose traces run underneath its parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup>

## Desoldering as a disassembly step

A great deal of desoldering is forced by mechanical design rather than by any fault. Boards are commonly retained by soldered input jacks, connector tabs, or bare wire leads with no header, so the connector must come off before the board will lift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Yk_T-uCbg10" title="Returned BM036 Clamp Meter Investigation">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup> A single soldered tab on a TRS jack, or final [[soldering]] of BNC connectors on an analogue board, is enough to make a board effectively non-removable without a [[soldering-iron|soldering iron]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> Metal cans over RF and analogue sections are another case: their contents are inaccessible unless the can is desoldered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1275" title="EEVblog #1275 - Uniden UHF Walkie Talkie Teardowns">1275</a></sup> Plastic clips designed for a part that does not pass through the board leave no alternative but to desolder the whole assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1757" title="EEVblog 1757: Sharp GF-7600 Boombox Repair PART 2 Electric Boogaloo">1757</a></sup> Where wires are soldered directly to boards, the specific assembly step implies a matching specific disassembly step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1352" title="EEVblog #1352 - Aircraft Transponder TEARDOWN!">1352</a></sup>

Construction of this kind compounds quickly. A metal plate carrying a bank of switches, with wires soldered directly into it and little clearance, has to be desoldered in its entirety before the board underneath can be reached, and the same work must be repeated on reassembly and on any subsequent visit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1572" title="EEVblog 1572 - Haefely 16kV ESD Tester TEARDOWN + Repair Part 1">1572</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1593" title="EEVblog 1593 - 16kV ESD Tester REPAIR - Part 2">1593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup> Where a switch or contact set is dirty rather than broken, cleaning it properly still means desoldering it and taking it apart, because in-situ cleaning generally cannot reach the contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1757" title="EEVblog 1757: Sharp GF-7600 Boombox Repair PART 2 Electric Boogaloo">1757</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1219" title="EEVblog #1219 - Don&#x27;t Trust Switches - Toy Repair">1219</a></sup>

## Wick, pumps and desoldering stations

[[solder-wick|Solder wick]] is the basic tool for clearing plated through-holes: laid over a filled hole and heated, it draws the solder away and re-opens the hole for a new lead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup> It is also the finishing step after any other method, used to clean pads back to a solderable state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> Its limits are visible in controlled measurement: after tinning a trace with 60/40 solder and then wicking the solder off again, the trace returned to roughly 52 milliohms against an original 51 milliohms, close to the starting value but not identical, and further wicking yields diminishing returns.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> Physically scraping the wick along the surface may remove or leach a small amount of copper as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup>

A hand vacuum solder sucker is the usual complement to wick and is often faster on large pins, where a desoldering gun fitted with a small nozzle is the wrong tool.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup> A desolder pump handles connectors soldered by a wave or dip process reasonably easily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup> A dedicated vacuum desoldering station extends this to large multi-pin parts: a PLCC package was extracted in under three minutes at 300 °C, with a bent pin and awkward working position, with virtually no damage and the part retrieved intact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup>

Hot air is the standard approach for surface-mount rework and desoldering. Cheap hot air rework stations are considered acceptable for rework and desoldering duty even where a cheap soldering station would not be recommended, on the grounds that the tool sees far less use than a soldering iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Comparative removal of identical memory chips from a video card shows that time-to-removal depends heavily on [[airflow]], temperature, nozzle height and movement, none of which has an obvious optimum for an unfamiliar part; every board and every chip differs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1659" title="EEVblog 1659 - Quick 861 Pro Hot Air Station REVIEW">1659</a></sup> Good tweezers matter as much as the air station, since the part must be lifted the moment the joints go liquid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1659" title="EEVblog 1659 - Quick 861 Pro Hot Air Station REVIEW">1659</a></sup>

## Through-hole components and connectors

Multi-pin through-hole connectors are awkward because all the pins must be molten simultaneously before the part will release. An iron with fast thermal recovery, set around 350 °C, can heat all the pins of a connector at once so the part lifts out, though this risks taking some of the through-hole plating with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup>

Where the connector is not worth saving, sacrificing it is the more reliable route: cut or chop the pins off first, then heat and pull each pin out individually, and wick the holes afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-278" title="EEVblog #278 - Elmo Visual Presenter Teardown">278</a></sup> This removes the need to heat three or more pins at once, and eliminates the sideways pressure that is otherwise applied while rocking the part free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-278" title="EEVblog #278 - Elmo Visual Presenter Teardown">278</a></sup> A single awkward pin can be chopped off from the top while the rest are heated together, with the remainder pulled through afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-278" title="EEVblog #278 - Elmo Visual Presenter Teardown">278</a></sup> Fresh solder applied to old joints before removal helps, and is close to mandatory on corroded ones — badly corroded joints on a vintage board resist heat transfer and are extremely difficult to clear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup>

Displays, backlights and similar large-pin-count parts are also through-hole work by another name: an [[lcd|LCD]] [[backlight]] soldered down to the board must be desoldered from underneath to be reached, and a [[vacuum-fluorescent-display|vacuum fluorescent display]] carries a great many pins that all have to be cleared before it will lift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1669" title="EEVblog 1669 - Agilent 53131A VFD to LED Display Upgrade MAILBAG">1669</a></sup>

## Surface-mount packages

Fine-pitch leaded parts can be removed by wiping the pins off with an iron at just enough temperature to melt the joints. The direction of the wipe is critical: wiping sideways across the leads loads the pads laterally and lifts them, so the stroke must run lengthways along the pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup> A second pass picks up stragglers, and the pads are cleaned with wick afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

Power packages with a [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] under the body are a distinct problem, because the pad's [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] and the leads demand different techniques. One approach separates the two: cut the leads with a sharp knife so the body, with its thermal pad, can be attacked on its own from underneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup> Each of these techniques carries its own trade-offs, and cutting leads forecloses reuse of the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>

At the extreme of fine pitch, a flip-chip die can sometimes be pushed straight off its substrate. On a [[processor]] module using 97/3 solder with pads only 180 microns wide, the sheer scarcity of solder meant the die would shear away under sideways pressure alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup> This works only because of the tiny pitch and tiny solder volume; the same push applied to a [[bga|BGA]] will shear off every pad, because the pad area provides almost no adhesion to hold the pads to the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup>

## Low-temperature alloys

Low-melting-point alloy, sold on small reels of around 20 grams, is used specifically for desoldering. Applied around the perimeter of a package, it combines with the existing tin-lead solder and drops the melting point substantially, so that all sides of the part stay molten simultaneously long enough for the part to be lifted or dropped clear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> It works, though the result is messy, and the pads are then cleaned up with wick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> The material is expensive and difficult to manufacture, so it is used sparingly, but a small quantity is worth keeping for desoldering and for occasional ultra-low-temperature work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup>

## Desoldering in fault-finding

Removing parts is a legitimate diagnostic technique. A short on a supply rail can be found by the brute-force method of sucking parts off until the short vanishes, and desoldering is also the way to rule out surface contamination as the cause of a measurement anomaly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup> Where several chips sit shorted across the same rail, removing them one at a time drops the supply [[current]] in proportion, which both confirms the diagnosis and counts the culprits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

The technique is nonetheless expensive in risk. Brute-force removal should start with bypass capacitors and similar low-value parts rather than with an SO-package device, which can be ruined in the process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> The alternative is a high-resolution ohmmeter capable of tracing the short down through the copper, which requires enough resolution in the milliohm region to distinguish points along the path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> Components should not be sucked out until every path has been traced and the conclusion is certain; removing parts on speculation destroys evidence and parts alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup>

## Salvage and reverse engineering

Before cheap bulk components were available, salvaging equipment meant desoldering the parts from it — chips, capacitors, diodes and everything else — and the resulting components stocked the parts drawers for years afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-igqZ77NXpyE" title="eevBLAB #18 - Old Component Drawers">•</a></sup> A collection assembled this way can run to thousands of hours of desoldering, with each part individually bagged and itemised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-737" title="EEVblog #737 - World&#x27;s Biggest Collection Of Electronics Components">737</a></sup> The practice persists selectively: high-power converter modules, LEDs and similar parts worth having are still worth desoldering out of a dead product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-928" title="EEVblog #928 - Jaycar DMX RGB LED Lights Teardown">928</a></sup> The modern shortcut is to keep whole boards rather than stripped parts, pulling a [[capacitor]], choke, [[fuse]] or [[heat-sink|heatsink]] from the board only when one is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup> Equipment with pin headers rather than soldered-in modules can be stripped with no desoldering at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9lIC3ZzIht4" title="Junk box Digital Panel Meter Analysis CX101">•</a></sup>

For [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]], desoldering is a way of seeing. Removing a chip or a socket exposes traces routed underneath it, which is faster and more reliable than buzzing out pins at random, and may reveal parts hidden under other parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup> Single-sided boards largely avoid the problem, since there are no traces running beneath top-side components; double-sided and denser boards generally require parts to come off before a correct [[schematic]] can be extracted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup> Some assemblies make this uneconomic — where a stack of boards is glued and gunked together, cutting the 0.1 inch pins all the way around and prising the boards apart is preferable to desoldering the interconnect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-339" title="EEVblog #339 - Cypres Parachute AAD Teardown">339</a></sup>
