A resistor divider is a pair of resistors in series across an input [[voltage]], with the output tapped from the junction between them; the output is always some voltage less than the input because the circuit drops voltage across the upper [[resistor]] and taps off the voltage across the lower one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> There is no gain in the arrangement at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Despite this simplicity, it is among the most frequently used structures in electronics, appearing in amplifiers, attenuators, every form of voltage regulation, and in many circuits that are not drawn recognizably as dividers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> By drawing convention the input is placed on the left and the output on the right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

## Loading and measurement error

A divider's nominal ratio holds only when nothing else loads the tap. Any additional [[resistance]] across the lower leg — including the measuring instrument — forms a parallel combination that pulls the tap voltage down. A [[digital-multimeter|digital multimeter]]'s nominal 10 MΩ [[input-impedance|input impedance]] measuring a half-rail divider built from 100 kΩ resistors introduces roughly a 1% error; with a 1 MΩ upper resistor the error is about 5%, and measuring a small fraction of the rail with much larger resistances produces errors around 9% — in the worst case approximately an order of magnitude of error attributable to that 10 MΩ instrument resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> A divider of two matched 200 kΩ resistors fed a precise 10 V reads about 4.98 V at the midpoint rather than 5 V on a 10 MΩ meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Analog moving-coil meters load far more heavily: a 20 kΩ-per-volt movement draws 50 µA at full scale regardless of range, and that [[current]] comes from the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

The same parallel-loading principle applies in design. In an LM3914 [[battery]]-gauge circuit, an input divider network sits directly in parallel with the reference-setting resistor R1, so the true value of R1 must be computed from the parallel combination rather than used directly; the adjust-pin current (75 µA typical, 120 µA maximum) must likewise be added to the divider current before calculating the lower resistor, yielding in one worked example 10.91 kΩ, realized exactly as 12 kΩ in parallel with 120 kΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

## References, biasing, and level shifting

Dividers are the standard way to generate reference and bias voltages. Two 1 kΩ resistors from a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] produce a 2.5 V reference for an LM311 [[comparator]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> A divider across a split supply furnishes symmetric ±1.25 V trip references for a window overload detector, and dividing the input signal itself (1 MΩ series with 270 kΩ to [[ground]]) brings the sensed voltage down inside a jellybean comparator's common-mode input range when sensing near the rail, avoiding the need for a rail-to-rail part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Op-amps on a single 5 V supply, such as the LM324, require a divider to bias inputs up to a [[dc-offset|DC offset]] near half the supply, with filtering to keep that bias clean, since [[grounding]] the inputs would clip the [[waveform]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup>

Regulator and reference output voltages are set by feedback dividers: a DC-DC converter with a 1.245 V reference and a 51 kΩ over 10 kΩ divider yields (51/10 + 1) × 1.245 ≈ 7.6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup> The LM4041 reference adds an adjustment pin over the fixed LM4040 specifically so a divider can set the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Conversely, a fixed-[[voltage-regulator|voltage regulator]] can be recognized by the absence of any feedback divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> The [[555-timer|555 timer]] embeds a divider of 5 kΩ resistors to generate its threshold and trigger levels at 2/3 and 1/3 of the supply (6 V and 3 V on a 9 V supply), and this string is popularly — though inaccurately — cited as the origin of the part's name.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The TL431 2.5 V reference paired with dividers is a classic power-good monitoring arrangement in PC power supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup>

As a level shifter, a divider of 1 kΩ and 680 Ω drops a 5 V TTL output to under 2 V for low-voltage logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup> A 220 Ω/47 Ω pair knocks a roughly 3.5 V digital RGB signal down below 1 V to match a monitor chip's 700 mV peak-to-peak input level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1246" title="EEVblog #1246 - Dumpster TV to Retro RGB Monitor Conversion">1246</a></sup> A divider with a [[diode]] clamp also conditions a mains tap into a 50 Hz clock for a digital clock's [[ripple]] counter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup>

## Keypads and configuration encoding

A divider chain lets many switches share a single [[microcontroller]] ADC pin: each key switches a different resistance into the divider, producing a distinct voltage that [[firmware]] maps back to a key.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1282" title="EEVblog #1282 - Design Your Own Membrane Keypad! (µSupply Part 20)">1282</a></sup> Membrane keypads can use per-key printed carbon resistors for this purpose,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1282" title="EEVblog #1282 - Design Your Own Membrane Keypad! (µSupply Part 20)">1282</a></sup> and equipment test points labeled "ADC key" indicate exactly this arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> The technique is fragile in practice: a tactile switch whose [[contact-resistance|contact resistance]] wanders from a couple of ohms to hundreds of ohms corrupts the divider voltage enough to cause major misreads,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1361" title="EEVblog #1361 - Dodgy Tactile Switch TEARDOWN">1361</a></sup> and high-[[impedance]] ladder dividers of this kind are susceptible to [[noise]] pickup as well as dodgy contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> The same encoding trick appears as configuration strapping, where an internal ADC reads set resistors as a divider so that changing the resistor values changes the instrument's configuration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup>

## Instrument front ends

In multimeters, the input divider network sets the voltage ranges, and much of a meter's stability and accuracy comes down to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> Quality meters use laser-trimmed ceramic hybrid thin-film divider networks;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup> the resistors share a ceramic substrate so all taps are thermally matched, with slots routed between taps to prevent creepage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Bottom-end meters substitute discrete passives,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup> while better divider resistors can eliminate [[calibration]] pots entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup> A common calibration strategy is to trim only the millivolt range and rely on the divider ratios for all higher ranges, which is why errors grow on the higher voltage ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup> Because the divider is the highest-impedance part of the front end, contamination or leakage across it loads the network and drifts every range; manufacturers strip [[solder-mask|solder mask]] around the high-impedance node for this reason, and a sealed ceramic divider that drifts points to contamination elsewhere rather than a failed network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup> The divider is also the first thing an unprotected [[input-jack|input jack]] hits, ahead of any PTC or series protection resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W2o8otvVML4" title="ZERO INPUT PROTECTION! Micsig MHO14 Oscilloscope/Multimeter">•</a></sup>

[[oscilloscope|Oscilloscope]] vertical attenuators are tapped divider ladders, and the pulse response can differ with the volts-per-division setting because each tap sits at a different point in the ladder with its own high- and low-[[frequency]] compensation; vintage instruments exposed per-step trimmer capacitors for tweaking each divider step's response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup> Compensation capacitors across divider resistors serve the same AC-flattening role in meter dividers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> A standard ×10 passive probe is itself a divider built around a 9 MΩ resistor working into the scope's 1 MΩ input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup>

## High-voltage and differential probes

High-voltage probes are dividers built from series strings of high-voltage resistors: a homemade 5 kV probe uses five 10 MΩ resistors each rated at 3.5 kV for a 50 MΩ upper leg, against a roughly 50 kΩ lower leg formed by a 56 kΩ resistor in parallel with the oscilloscope's 1 MΩ input and another 1 MΩ resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-85" title="EEVblog #85 - High Voltage Oscilloscope Probe Design">85</a></sup> Dropping most of the voltage across high-value series resistors so that only low-voltage amplification is needed downstream is the standard technique inside high-voltage differential probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup> Such probes use two matched dividers — for example 4 MΩ input legs with compensation capacitors against a low-value lower leg — feeding a differential FET front end,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> and the high leg resistance lets the probe connect anywhere in a circuit without disturbing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup>

Divider matching dominates differential measurement quality. The op-amps in a differential probe may exceed 100 dB of common-mode rejection, but resistor divider mismatch drags the achieved [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] down to 40 dB or less;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup> matching of the divider string and the [[differential-amplifier|differential amplifier]]'s gain together set the figure, and ordinary component tolerance can throw the result out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> The Wheatstone bridge is the canonical ratio-matching application: it is two dividers excited from the same source, and when the ratios are equal the voltage difference between the two tap points is precisely zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup>

## Precision divider construction

Thin-film divider networks achieve very high leg values — around 9.9 MΩ — through serpentine trace geometries, since that is the only practical way to get such values on a substrate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-730" title="EEVblog #730 - Thin Film Resistor Networks">730</a></sup> Mixing geometries (ladders, serpentine sections, coarse and intermediate trims) reduces the sensitivity of the final value to how much material the laser removes, letting trimming converge progressively on the target, and trimming can be done both per-resistor and on a ratio basis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-730" title="EEVblog #730 - Thin Film Resistor Networks">730</a></sup>
