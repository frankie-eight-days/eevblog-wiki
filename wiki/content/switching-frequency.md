Switching frequency is the rate at which the active element of a switch-mode circuit — a [[mosfet]], a bipolar [[transistor]], or a switch array inside a charge-pump chip — is turned on and off to transfer energy. It is the single parameter that sets the size of the magnetics and capacitors a converter needs, the switching losses it burns, and the [[frequency]] at which its noise appears in every other circuit nearby.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> Almost every design decision around a switcher is a trade against it: raising the frequency shrinks the passive components and worsens the losses, and the correct answer is a compromise rather than a maximum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

## Frequency and component size

The higher the switching frequency, the smaller the [[inductor]] a converter requires, and inductors are physically bulky parts — a large [[inductance]] value means a large device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> In a space-constrained design this is often the deciding factor: a [[boost-converter]] selected for a small board was chosen partly because its higher switching frequency permitted a smaller inductor, alongside its 800 mA output capability and 2.05 W maximum output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The same logic applies to transformers, and explains why an offline or isolated stage that would need a large 50 Hz [[transformer]] can get away with a tiny one once it switches at high frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yOJ7xPugsdc" title="Hoymiles Microinverter Reverse Engineering CORRECTION">•</a></sup>

Inductor and [[capacitor]] values are computed for a particular switching frequency, so they are not portable between chips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> Switching frequency can differ vastly from part to part, and substituting a pin-compatible regulator without matching the passives may still work but can produce more output ripple or lower [[efficiency]] than intended.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> Where two candidate parts share a formula and a nominal frequency — both around 1.5 MHz, for example — they can reasonably be treated as equivalents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup>

In classical switch-mode design the frequency enters the calculation directly: the sum of on-time and off-time is simply 1/F, so a device specified for a maximum of 100 kHz yields 10 µs for T on plus T off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

## Losses and the efficiency trade-off

Switching losses rise with frequency. A conventional hard-switching topology drives the transistor essentially digitally, high-low-high-low, and the losses of that transition grow the faster it is repeated — which is awkward, because higher frequency is exactly what is wanted for smaller magnetics and better efficiency elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Resonant topologies such as LLC exist to break this deadlock by reshaping the switching [[waveform]] so that less is lost per transition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup> The trade-off is a standing design question rather than a solved one: for any given converter it is worth asking why a particular frequency was chosen and what changes if it goes higher.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

Overall converter efficiency is a function of the magnetics, the MOSFET, the output [[capacitance]] and the switching frequency taken together, and a converter designed around a specific operating point can reach 90% or better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> Efficiency of that order matters directly as heat: at 100 W output and 90% efficiency, 10 W has to be dissipated by the active semiconductors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

## Device limits on frequency

MOSFETs dominate switching applications because they are faster than bipolar transistors, and remain usable into the megahertz region where high-frequency DC-DC converters operate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> The limit on bipolar transistors is storage time — the base charge is retained for a period on the order of microseconds after drive is removed, keeping the transistor on, and this is what caps their switching frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> Small jellybean MOSFETs are commonly rated around 1 MHz switching frequency; at that level input capacitance is rarely the binding constraint for jellybean use, and package [[power-dissipation]] is more likely to be — a SOT-23 part is limited to roughly 0.2 W absolute maximum at room temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

## Frequencies encountered in practice

Measured switching frequencies span more than four orders of magnitude depending on the era, topology and purpose of the equipment:

- Mains-derived and offline supplies sit at the low end: an arcade machine [[power-supply]] at about 27 kHz,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> a switch-mode supply probed at 40 kHz,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> a VIPER 22A primary-side offline switcher at 60 kHz,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1ngqB4mxZOI" title="Smeg Oven Goes Clunkedy Clunk + Capacitor Go Poop">•</a></sup> a bench supply at around 66 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup>
- [[led]] lighting gear clusters in the tens of kilohertz: an LED fluorescent tube replacement at 64 kHz,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup> one LED panel at 62.7 kHz and another at 47 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup>
- Modern bench and [[usb]] power supplies run higher: 120 kHz,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> about 160 kHz for a USB DC-DC supply,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> 246 kHz for two separate units,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1607" title="EEVblog 1607 - PSU Switching Noise Reduction via Vibration">1607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> and 300 kHz for a boost converter demo board running 19 V in to 24 V out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-oVkrc3gF7ns" title="Conducted Mode EMC Emissions Demo">•</a></sup>
- Integrated converters and chopper-stabilised amplifiers reach the megahertz region: 1.5 MHz for a small buck regulator,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> 1 MHz or more for a tablet's DC-DC converters,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> 2 MHz for a switched-capacitor [[inverter]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> and hundreds of kilohertz — reportedly around 250 kHz — for the internal chopping of an OPA189.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup>

A period tool intended for power work needed only hundreds of kilohertz to several megahertz of [[bandwidth]] to cover the switching frequencies of its day, which is why 7.5 MHz was ample for its market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup>

## Frequency that varies

Switching frequency is not always a fixed number. Many converters deliberately vary it with output power to hold efficiency up at light loads, so a measured value is only valid for the load at which it was taken.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> One PoE hat measured about 24.6 kHz under one condition, and running the load up from a light condition to 2 W doubled the frequency from 9 kHz to 19 kHz — behaviour the [[datasheet|data sheet]] would be expected to describe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup> A supply operating at 120 kHz was seen to add a second, additional switching component once driven to 200 W, a genuine mode change rather than a capture artifact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> In a data sheet, the switching frequency in the typical application circuit is governed by a complex equation with several dependencies, and taking the headline number at face value is a way to come unstuck.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

A frequency that has moved without being asked to is a fault symptom. In a failed combiscope supply, the switching seen across a convenient [[diode]] had simply dropped in frequency, which pulled down every output rail derived from it, including the EHT — evidence against the tripler being at fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup> Conversely, a healthy supply shows a consistent waveform at its nominal frequency with no cycle skipping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup>

## Measuring switching frequency

The usual probe point is the inductor, though the right node depends on the converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> The primary of the main switching transformer works for offline supplies, probed with a high-[[voltage]] differential probe at settings such as 50 V or 100 V per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> A field-sensing [[current]] probe allows the switching waveform at the inductor output to be observed without breaking into the circuit, which is the traditional requirement for current probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> Where automatic measurement is unavailable, the frequency can simply be read off the [[time-base]] and division count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup>

Deep memory helps capture the whole waveform for later zooming, but it also causes trouble: with high-frequency content held in memory, a scope's automatic frequency measurement may lock onto the switching edges' ringing rather than the switching cycle itself. Offsetting the waveform vertically away from the screen centre makes the counter find the peaks elsewhere and track the intended frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

Probe fidelity degrades with frequency, so the measurement setup must be qualified alongside the circuit. At 1 kHz switching, a high-voltage differential probe shows no visible change in waveform even at 300 V; as frequency rises, common-mode rejection falls off and the artifacts appear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> At 1 MHz switching frequency, differences between probes were attributable to the probing arrangement itself rather than the probes' performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> Low-inductance probing — dispensing with the standard ground lead — is required to see the switching component honestly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup>

## Noise, EMC and resonance

Switching frequencies and the fast, sharp edges that accompany them are the primary source of broadband radiated emissions from an electronic product; short of a fully effective sealed metal enclosure, essentially any product containing them will radiate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> On the output side, the ripple that matters on a switching supply is at the switching frequency rather than at low frequency, and manufacturers frequently specify low-frequency ripple in millivolts while saying nothing about the switching noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> A PoE hat exhibited 320 mV peak-to-peak of switching noise at a modest load,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup> and an inexpensive supply module showed roughly 700 mV peak-to-peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> Switching noise also couples in from the environment: an instrument's low-level measurement picked up a component at around 64 kHz that came from studio LED lighting, not the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup>

Filtering a switching component out of a low-level output requires the filter corner to be well below the switching frequency; an RC filter with a 3 dB point at 159 Hz was still not low enough in value to remove the switching noise from a supply attempting to output 100 mV, leaving roughly 10 mV of ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

Resonance makes the choice of frequency consequential in less obvious ways. Fitting many bypass capacitors of different values raises the risk of hitting a resonant point at some switching frequency, where the track inductance and the capacitors' resonances combine and the circuit misbehaves badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Return current also depends on frequency: the higher the switching frequency, the more the return loop hugs the path directly beneath the power traces due to spreading inductance, rather than spreading out across the plane as it tends to at DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

The mechanical consequences are real. In Tesla's Dojo hardware, MEMS oscillators cracked from out-of-plane mechanical resonance excited by capacitor vibration at the switching frequency combined with high currents; among the available fixes were anti-vibration mounting, relocating the oscillator to a separate board, and changing the switching frequency itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> Multi-layer ceramic capacitors are piezoelectric, and choosing a switching frequency of 5 kHz puts the resulting vibration squarely in the audible range — a chipset switching at 20 kHz or 30 kHz instead would have gone unnoticed, making an audible switcher a poor choice of frequency rather than an unavoidable defect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup>

## Charge pumps and voltage multipliers

In switched-capacitor circuits the frequency interacts with capacitor value to determine output droop. Output ripple and sag depend jointly on the capacitor values and the switching frequency, since the capacitors must recharge between cycles and a loaded stage passes a lower peak voltage to the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup> In a Cockcroft-Walton multiplier the practical limit on chain length comes from the AC [[impedance]] of the components together with the switching frequency; a full-wave version doubles the effective frequency, reducing sag and increasing load capability, exactly as it does in a linear supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup>

A [[microcontroller]]-driven voltage doubler illustrates the trade directly. At 1 kHz the ripple and droop are severe; 10 kHz serves as a reasonable rule of thumb.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup> Frequency only starts to matter once the output begins to droop — with no ripple present, raising the frequency buys nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup> Increasing the capacitors to 47 µF allows acceptable operation at frequencies as low as around 100 Hz, down to 81 Hz in testing, with enough DC left to run a load such as an [[lcd]], particularly with a [[linear-regulator]] following.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

The relationship between frequency and ripple in charge pumps is a trap in part selection. A 2 MHz switched-capacitor inverter appears, by analogy with the classic 7660, to promise low output ripple, but the 7660's near-100% efficiency depends on both the switching frequency and the output impedance of its internal switches, and the analogy does not carry over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>
