A short circuit is an unintended low-[[resistance]] connection between two nodes that are meant to be at different potentials — most commonly between a [[power-rail]] and ground, or between adjacent conductors on a board. Because it bypasses the intended load, a short allows a source to deliver essentially its full [[current]] capability: NiMH cells of ordinary AA size can supply many amps into a short circuit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1200" title="EEVblog #1200 - BUSTED! - Energizer &amp; Duracell NiMH">1200</a></sup>, and shorting a load dissipates all available power in the source's own internal resistance, heating or destroying the source unless protection is present<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>. Consequences range from a supply harmlessly shutting down, through overheated components, to open fire<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1037" title="EEVblog #1037 - Solving Ceramic Capacitor Cracking">1037</a></sup>.

## The short circuit in circuit theory

In ideal circuit analysis the short circuit is a defined element, not a fault. An ideal [[voltage-source]] has zero internal resistance, so when applying the superposition theorem, each deactivated voltage source is replaced with a short circuit, while a current source is replaced with an open circuit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup>. The Thévenin equivalent resistance of a network is found by dividing its open-circuit [[voltage]] by its short-circuit current: with the output shorted, the entire source current flows through the short and none through the external resistance, giving the short-circuit current directly<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>. In symmetric networks such as the [[resistor]] cube, nodes that are at equal potential can be deliberately treated as shorted together — or alternatively opened — to simplify the analysis, and both approaches give the same answer<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>.

Component behaviour also invokes the concept: an uncharged [[capacitor]] at time zero behaves as a short circuit, which is why [[capacitance]] near a switch conducts the instant power is applied<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup>, and the zero position of a decade resistance box is implemented by short-circuiting the whole series chain of resistors to give zero ohms<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1586" title="EEVblog 1586 -  Stranger Things Decade Resistor Box + Repair">1586</a></sup>. A ×1 [[oscilloscope-probe]] works by switching in a short across the 9 MΩ resistor and compensation capacitor of a ×10 network, leaving a direct connection from tip to scope input<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup>. At the other extreme, very low-value inductors approach a short circuit closely enough that some [[multimeter]]-based [[inductance]] measurements stop working<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup>.

## As a component failure mode

Many common components fail preferentially to a short. Multilayer ceramic capacitors that crack through their internal plates very often fail short circuit rather than open; an open failure merely kills the product, but a shorted MLCC placed across a power source capable of delivering substantial energy can ignite<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1037" title="EEVblog #1037 - Solving Ceramic Capacitor Cracking">1037</a></sup>. The cracking is driven by mechanical stress — for example, torque from terminal screws rigidly soldered near the capacitor can produce a micro-crack that later fails short and burns the board<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup>. Tantalum capacitors similarly develop internal shorts and can catch fire or explode, particularly with pulsed input<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup>. Film (metallised polyester) capacitors more commonly fail short circuit than by losing capacitance<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-379" title="EEVBlog #379 - Yamaha RX-V557 Receiver Fix">379</a></sup>, and in some constructions an incorrect conductive epoxy can produce a short between terminations<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup>.

Semiconductors fail the same way: a shorted rectifier [[diode]] can dump a power rail into an analogue rail and take out downstream circuitry<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup>, and a spike on a supply rail can short multiple ICs on that rail simultaneously, leaving the rail at fractions of an ohm<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>. A failed lithium cell can measure under an ohm internally, shorting the cell and defeating its protection circuitry<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1613" title="EEVblog 1613 - Audiophile REPAIR: $4500 Chord Hugo DAC">1613</a></sup>. Even powered-down equipment can present a short: a faulty [[electronic-load]] measured under an ohm across its input terminals while switched off, repeatedly tripping the source's resettable overcurrent protection<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--ir61ARd5T4" title="WTF is Wrong with my Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load?">•</a></sup>.

## Causes in manufacture and assembly

Solder bridges between adjacent leads are a routine assembly defect, and automated optical inspection explicitly checks lead banks for shorts between leads<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>; bridges also survive into shipped products<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup>. [[wave-soldering]] of boards carrying tall through-hole parts is arranged to prevent short circuits between long protruding leads<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>. Other typical production causes include metal strands from braided cable bridging nearby pins<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup>, ragged copper left when castellated holes are routed at the board edge<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1681" title="EEVblog 1681 - Mailbag: Nice DIY CC Load,  Amazing Proto Boards, Ian Johnston">1681</a></sup>, copper pours brought out to a routed board edge where they can short against the surrounding metalwork<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup>, and exposed through-hole pins touching a metal chassis when a board flexes<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>. Solder burrs on [[battery]] terminals sitting close to a metal enclosure are enough to short the terminals to the case<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>. Cutting apart panelised PCBs at mouse-bite breakaways with side cutters can accidentally short tracks that pass between the break points<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>.

Design tools are expected to catch the [[schematic]]-level equivalents: electrical rules checking should flag two output pins shorted together or an output shorted to a power net as an error<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5eJorvyA708" title="Altium Designer Serious ERC bug">•</a></sup>, and boundary-scan testing on assembled boards verifies shorts and opens by writing a value to a pin and reading it back — a pin shorted to ground cannot be driven high<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup>.

## Consequences and protection

A short on a rail commonly drags the rail down so that the [[power-supply]] current-limits or shuts down entirely, which is itself a diagnostic symptom<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>. A supply in constant-current mode delivers no more than its set limit even into a dead short, so shorting the output of a current-limited bench supply or a purpose-built [[constant-current-source]] is harmless<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>; consumer designs apply the same principle, with recessed terminals and built-in [[current-limiting]] to tens of milliamps so that shorted output pads do no damage<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-205" title="Amazon Kindle 4 Review &amp; Unboxing - EEVblog #205">205</a></sup>.

Where energy is not limited, shorts start fires. A coolant leak that caused a short circuit in an electronic component was identified as the root cause of a grid-scale battery installation fire<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1422" title="EEVblog 1422 - CAUSE of the Tesla Victoria Big Battery Fire">1422</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1568" title="EEVblog 1568 - Tesla Megapack 2 Battery FIRE! at Bouldercombe QLD">1568</a></sup>, and decoupling capacitors shorting out have ignited [[pcb]] laminates, exploiting the fact that FR-4's flammability rating only limits, rather than prevents, burning<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup>. Charring on a PCB is itself dangerous: carbonised board material is conductive, so a board that has arced once can turn from open to dead short on the next power-up even after apparently being repaired<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup>.

Batteries require particular care because their internal construction places opposite terminals very close together. In alkaline cells the positive terminal connects to the metal can, so the internal lead must be insulated to avoid shorting to the case<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-518" title="EEVblog #518 - 9V Alkaline Battery Construction">518</a></sup>, and cells in a 9 V battery must keep their insulating wrap so the positive cases of adjacent cells do not short together<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2Y1tVvllklc" title="9V Duracell Alkaline Battery Leakage">•</a></sup>. A metal-cased NiMH cell can short if the outer insulation is pierced, releasing many amps from a high-capacity cell<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1200" title="EEVblog #1200 - BUSTED! - Energizer &amp; Duracell NiMH">1200</a></sup>, and clamping thin metal around a cell — relying only on the cell's wrapper to separate cut metal edges from the terminals — is an accident waiting to happen<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>. Shorting can also be a deliberate safety measure: explosive bridgewire assemblies are made safe by twisting the leads together into a dead short and [[grounding]] them, so no stray current can pass through the element<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup>.

## Locating shorts

The standard diagnosis of a shorted rail is a milliohm-level [[resistance-measurement]]: a 3.3 V rail reading 0.11 Ω with either probe polarity — ruling out a semiconductor junction — confirms a solid short<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>, and readings of 0.1–0.2 Ω on a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] similarly confirm one<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup>. Resolution matters: a 6½-digit multimeter with sharp probes can locate a short spatially, because the measured resistance falls as the probes approach the fault, a technique used to find hundreds of shorts on freshly wave-soldered boards where pins short to ground planes<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717ampROkvc" title="PRO TIP: Using a 6.5 Digit Multimeter for finding Shorts">•</a></sup>. Comparing readings at several points on a board triangulates the fault — the point with the lowest resistance is nearest the short, and equidistant points read the same<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup>.

When resistance alone is ambiguous, current-based methods apply. Feeding a current through the shorted net and tracing the [[magnetic-field]] with a current probe follows the fault path even inside a multilayer board, where shorts in vias or on internal planes are otherwise notoriously difficult to find<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>. Cooling the board changes the fault current and can make a marginal short vanish temporarily, confirming the location<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>, and thermal imaging reveals the shorted component as a hot spot when the rail is driven<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>. A cruder last resort is to force a heavy current — at the same voltage, from a high-current supply — across the short to burn it open or produce smoke and heat that identify the failed component; this can restore a board to operation without fixing the underlying cause<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>. On newly assembled boards, the routine precaution is to buzz out each power rail with a continuity check before first power-up<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>.

## Bench hazards

Creating an accidental short is one of the most common ways to damage equipment during measurement and repair. Probes slip on fine-pitch IC pins and bridge adjacent pins, potentially destroying circuitry that was previously intact; probing external passive components instead of the pins themselves, or using hook-type clips, avoids this<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-749" title="EEVblog #749 - Mailbag">749</a></sup>. On flat-flex connectors, probing from the recessed rear of the contact rather than the exposed front prevents the probe slipping across two pins<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup>, and crocodile clips should not be attached to pin headers where they can bridge neighbouring pins<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup>.

Test instruments themselves can be the short. A multimeter on its current ranges is essentially a short across its terminals — about 0.2 Ω on the amps range and a few ohms on the milliamp ranges — so measuring a voltage while still connected for current is potentially destructive<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>; pocket multimeters with fixed leads and current ranges are disfavoured precisely because the range can be selected accidentally<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup>. [[soldering]] carries its own risks: a mains-earth-referenced [[soldering-iron]] touched to the positive terminal of an in-circuit battery shorts the battery straight to earth and can destroy [[calibration]] data<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup>, and cutting both battery wires at once with metal cutters shorts the cell — leads should be cut one at a time<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-930" title="EEVBlog #930 - Mailbag">930</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1613" title="EEVblog 1613 - Audiophile REPAIR: $4500 Chord Hugo DAC">1613</a></sup>. Other documented causes include a screwdriver bridging a terminal tab while tightening connectors on a live board<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup>, a metal temperature probe slipping between a diode and a capacitor and releasing the magic smoke<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup>, exposed soldered-through pads shorting supply rails when the board is placed on a conductive surface<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup>, and probes slipping while measuring a high-capacity battery<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>.
