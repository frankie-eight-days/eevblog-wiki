A current shunt is a low-value [[resistor]] placed in series with a [[current]] path so that the current can be inferred from the [[voltage]] developed across it, by Ohm's law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> It is the dominant method of [[current-measurement]] in bench and handheld instruments: a [[multimeter]] switched to amps is essentially nothing more than a current shunt connected from the input jack to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Because the shunt sits in the circuit under test, every design decision about it is a trade between [[measurement-resolution]], the voltage it steals from the load, and the power it has to dissipate.

## Construction

The classic shunt in a handheld meter is not a packaged resistor at all but a length of [[resistance]] wire, usually nichrome, formed into a loop or a zigzag and soldered directly to the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The wavy or zigzag pattern sets the resistance by path length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup> Wire-wound-on-former construction also appears, with nichrome wound around a ceramic core.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> Because a formed-wire shunt is a large, low-resistance piece of metal, it is effectively a [[short-circuit]] that will never blow, which is why it is not the fusing element in an amps path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

Solder joints on these shunts habitually look poor. The characteristic dull, lumpy fillet where nichrome wire meets the board is normal for the material rather than evidence of a bad joint, and can appear on instruments whose [[soldering]] is otherwise first class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup>

Shunt quality is one of the fastest visual reads on a meter's class. Cheap instruments use a bent bit of copper wire; better ones still use a bent bit of wire, but a large, substantial, shiny one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Notably wimpy 10 A shunts turn up in low-cost meters,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup> and comparisons between meters at different price points often come down to one having a physically beefier shunt with more capacity than the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> Surface-mount shunt resistors are used where the currents are modest, in instrument front ends,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup> soldering stations,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> and [[led]] driver boards where 0.1 Ω parts sense each string.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup> Some designs abandon formed wire entirely for packaged precision parts, which is unusual enough to be conspicuous against the expected nichrome loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup>

## Four-terminal connection

At milliohm values, the resistance of the connecting tracks and solder joints is a significant fraction of the shunt itself, so precision shunts are sensed with separate voltage-sense connections tapped onto the shunt body — a four-terminal, or Kelvin, arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Well-made examples include tapped 10 A shunts with dual sense terminals,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> four-wire 0.1 Ω 0.1% parts in bench instruments,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup> and four-terminal shunts from established resistor makers mounted on the underside of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup> On hand-formed shunts the sense wire is tapped off at a precisely chosen point along the wire and trimmed to trim the effective value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup>

The benefit is easily thrown away in layout. Running a large copper pour and heavy [[via]] stitching between the high-current side and the sense side of a four-terminal shunt largely defeats the purpose of having one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

## Burden voltage

Any shunt in the current path develops a [[voltage-drop]], and that drop is subtracted from the supply reaching the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> The magnitude depends on which range is selected, because each range switches in a different shunt value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> A 10 mΩ shunt on a 5 A range gives 50 mV full scale; the same shunt used for a 50 mA range gives only 500 µV full scale, which is why low ranges conventionally use much larger shunt values and therefore incur much larger burden voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> In practice the high-current range has very low [[burden-voltage]], and switching down to the milliamp range for better resolution costs a substantially higher drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> This is the central difficulty in measuring the current consumption of a product from a fixed supply voltage: the shunt drop means the device is not actually seeing the voltage the supply is set to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup>

## Ranging and switching

Multi-range instruments switch between shunt values, sometimes with relays and sometimes with MOSFETs — for instance a 10 mΩ shunt for the 10 A range and a 500 mΩ shunt for the milliamp range, selected by paralleled MOSFETs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> An alternative is to short out part of a shunt chain with a [[mosfet]] rather than switch between separate resistors; this puts the MOSFET's on-resistance in series with the sense resistor, an error that can be removed in software but which a cleaner topology would avoid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> Simpler meters dispense with switching altogether and use a single fixed shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup> Panel meters are frequently sold with no internal shunt at all, a 100 µA or similar full-scale movement or input being scaled to amps by an external shunt chosen by the user.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-769" title="EEVblog #769 - Mailbag">769</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9lIC3ZzIht4" title="Junk box Digital Panel Meter Analysis CX101">•</a></sup>

## Power dissipation

Above a few amps the limit is thermal. Where a single part cannot handle the dissipation, shunts are paralleled: four in parallel for the main input shunt of an [[electronic-load]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> three in parallel in a 50 A supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> In an electronic load the shunt is bolted to the same [[heat-sink]] as the pass transistors, and the [[airflow]] has to remove the shunt's dissipation as well as the load's.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> The same design at lower current rating uses a different shunt with roughly half the power capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Paralleling is also used to hit an awkward value: ten 10 Ω resistors in parallel give a 1 Ω shunt, which over a 2 A output range with a 10-bit converter works out to 2 mA per step, or 4 mA per step if only 8 bits are available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

## Accuracy, tempco and calibration

Shunt accuracy sets instrument accuracy. In a high-precision instrument specified at 0.05% class voltage and 0.1% class current, the main input shunts are expected to be a very low tempco alloy, either trimmed to value physically or characterised and corrected in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup> Similar reasoning governs meter front ends, where the shunt's [[temperature-coefficient]] is the dominant concern and the value itself is calibrated out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup> When the shunt is off value or inconsistent between batches, the error shows up directly in the reading — a batch problem in a 10 mΩ shunt accounting for a 0.23% error where the rest of the signal chain was good to 0.01%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup> Precision shunts are themselves [[calibration]] artifacts, forming part of the equipment set in an automated calibration rack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-422" title="EEVblog #422 - How To Calibrate A Calibrator">422</a></sup> At the utility scale, shunt error is one plausible source of small residual energy readings, on the order of a couple of hundred watt-hours, in revenue metering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lndRXed2ylk" title="AGL Energy Smart Meter DODGY?">•</a></sup>

## Where shunts appear

Bench power supplies use an output current shunt in the main power path between the power board and the output binding posts, for current readback and [[current-limiting]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-30" title="EEVblog #30 - Jaycar Bench Lab Power Supply Review">30</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup> The same is true of the small DC-DC converter modules sold as cheap adjustable supplies, some of which carry dual shunts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1375" title="EEVblog #1375 - Mailbag: IEEE">1375</a></sup> Electronic loads use large shunts on the input side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> Power analysers use internal shunts on each channel, with provision to program in an external shunt where the internal one is unsuitable, though the internal ones are the recommended configuration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> Soldering stations,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OvGdE5hC1Ro" title="Bakon BK212 Soldering Station - URGH">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup> computer motherboards,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-882" title="EEVblog #882 - Dumpster Dive Apple Xserve Computers">882</a></sup> and telecoms line cards<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1177" title="EEVblog #1177 - Ericsson PABX Teardown">1177</a></sup> all use them for local current monitoring.

Instruments themselves are often built for shunt access during service. Rail-to-rail links on a board, labelled with the rail voltage, can double as current measurement points, letting a technician cut the link and measure the current on that particular rail during troubleshooting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup>

Shunts also make a good diagnostic target. A production test failure confined to the amps range, with all other ranges passing, points at the current shunt, since a passing volts range proves the amplifiers, gains and supply generation are all working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup>

Not everything that looks like a shunt is one. Large low-resistance-looking parts can turn out to be jumper links added purely to raise current-carrying capacity where the copper on the [[pcb]] is inadequate — cheaper than specifying 12 oz copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1610" title="EEVblog 1610 - Deye 5kW Hybrid Solar Inverter">1610</a></sup> Similar parts measured too high in value to be shunts turn out to be something else entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup>

## Layout and safety

Because the amps path carries the full input current to the common jack, layout around the shunt matters. A screw hole placed in the middle of a 10 A trace, leaving very little copper on either side, is a construction defect regardless of what the shunt itself is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup> Good front ends put high-voltage isolation slots around the shunt and the input jacks and route the current path cleanly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-432" title="EEVblog #432 - Brymen Multimeters">432</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup> The shunt sits downstream of the high rupture capacity [[fuse]] in a properly designed amps path,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> and in practice the 10 A shunt is not the part that fails — something else blows first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup>

## Alternatives

The shunt's defining drawback is that it must be inserted into the circuit. Where that is impractical, current transformers or clamp probes are used instead. Revenue meters use current transformers rather than a traditional shunt on each phase, a choice driven by the IEC 62053-21 standard and its relatives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> Hall-effect clamp probes measure current by clamping over a wire without breaking into the circuit, avoiding the need to install a shunt or cut traces, though they cannot be clamped over a PCB track.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

The difficulty is sharpest with mains current. Measuring the mains consumption of a product with a shunt means getting inside the [[power-supply]], fitting the shunt, and reading it with an isolated high-voltage amplifier; a clamp probe simply goes around the active conductor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup> Ordinary [[oscilloscope]] probes across a shunt bring [[grounding]] problems, and differential probes, designed for high voltages, are poorly suited to the small voltages a shunt produces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup>

## Designing for measurement

Where current consumption is a design parameter, the shunt is best planned in rather than retrofitted. Evaluation boards built to compare candidate microcontrollers can be laid out with dedicated current-consumption shunts and jumpers so that in-circuit consumption can be measured directly on each part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> The same thinking applies to test fixtures, where a small current sense shunt lets the fixture read the test current drawn as each board under test is switched on and compare it against an expected figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Multi-output products require a shunt per output to measure delivered power rather than input power; measuring only on the input side gives total consumption including converter losses, which can be approximated by subtracting a nominal [[efficiency]] figure in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup>

Polarity and connection order matter in use. A shunt inserted the wrong way round simply produces negative readings, and the sense terminals must be taken from the shunt itself rather than from further along the wiring.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>
