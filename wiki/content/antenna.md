An antenna is the structure that couples electrical energy between a circuit and free space, and in a radio system it is the component that decides what the rest of the design has to cope with. It is fundamentally simple — in its most basic form "Just a long wire"<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LzAUa_FtuC0" title="Livewave SUPER ANTENNA! SCAM">•</a></sup> — and it is indifferent to what is riding on the signal: "An antenna is an antenna, it'll pick up any signal that is broadcast"<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LzAUa_FtuC0" title="Livewave SUPER ANTENNA! SCAM">•</a></sup>. Because every decibel captured at the antenna is a decibel of gain that does not have to be added later, with its accompanying noise, in television, two-way radio and communications generally "the antenna is everything"<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>.

An antenna only radiates from a changing field: "you can't just put DC on an antenna and have it transmit something"<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup>. The signal has to oscillate, and if it oscillates as a [[sine-wave]] it represents one pure [[frequency]]<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup>. Radiated power falls off with the square of distance, so for an antenna with a 360-degree radiation pattern such as Wi-Fi, the power crossing a square centimetre a metre away is a small fraction of what left the transmitter<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4vHx-UyIM9M" title="eevBLAB #74 - 5G Causes EVERYTHING!">•</a></sup>.

## Size, wavelength and matching

Physical length is tied to wavelength. A UHF walkie-talkie operating at 477 MHz has a wavelength of roughly 0.6 m, so its spiral-wound internal element is a quarter-wave structure rather than a full-length whip<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1275" title="EEVblog #1275 - Uniden UHF Walkie Talkie Teardowns">1275</a></sup>. An FM bug carrying a proper half-wavelength element, sited high with clear line of sight and radiating somewhere between 10 and 30 milliwatts, was received 27 kilometres away on a car radio<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-3 - Talking Electronics - FM Bug Kits - Part 3 of 5">280</a></sup>. Conversely, an early crystal set required a long strung wire before it would produce audible reception at all<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1654" title="EEVblog 1654 - Lee Felsenstein: The Computer Revolution &amp; Counterculture">1654</a></sup>.

A missing or disconnected antenna is not a small degradation. Removing the antenna connection from a 121.5/243 MHz emergency beacon leaves the transmitter running but radiating almost nothing — inefficient by orders of magnitude, enough that the transmission goes nowhere<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>. The same effect appears as a fault: an FM transmitter with an unsoldered antenna wire produced clear audio at almost no signal strength, and reconnecting the single wire restored full strength<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-3 - Talking Electronics - FM Bug Kits - Part 3 of 5">280</a></sup>.

Antenna behaviour also changes with its surroundings. A beacon's characteristics shift depending on whether the element is vertical or laid flat against a grounded bar, and the resulting change in the AC-coupled signal at the feed point is detectable by the on-board circuitry<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>. Reflected power measured at the feed is a direct indicator of this: a bare wire used as an antenna will change its measured characteristics simply from being touched or moved<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup>. Enclosures matter as much as position — a lunar rover built entirely of aluminium needs its Bluetooth antenna mounted outside the body to get any reception<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-885" title="EEVblog #885 - Audi Quattro Lunar Rover Unboxing!">885</a></sup>, and a lock electronics module with the antenna integrated into the chip inside a steel safe works only at around half a metre of range, a limitation accepted rather than designed around with an external element<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7xF1RCVw4Fc" title="How High Security Safe Key Locks Work">•</a></sup>.

## Diversity reception

Multipath is the dominant impairment for mobile receivers. Signals arriving by different paths can land 180 degrees out of phase and cancel, producing a dead spot where reception drops out entirely<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>. The spots are physically small: a location that drops out may be perfectly receivable 30 centimetres away<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>.

Diversity solves this by separating antennas in space so that they genuinely receive different signals, giving the receiver a choice of which to use<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>. In a dual-diversity receiver, two antennas feed an RF switch that selects between them<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>. True diversity goes further, duplicating the entire receive chain — two sets of filtering, two amplifiers, two mixers — so both signals are demodulated simultaneously in real time and the better one can be selected practically instantaneously<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>. The technique extends beyond two antennas<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>. Where a diversity receiver appears to have only one visible antenna, the second is generally hidden elsewhere in the product; in one bodypack design the second diversity feed is taken from a pad associated with the audio jack<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup>.

Feeding DC up the antenna line through an [[inductor]], rather than taking RF out, indicates power being supplied to a remote RF amplifier at the antenna itself<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup>.

## Unintentional antennas

Any conductor of sufficient extent behaves as an antenna, and much practical engineering consists of preventing that. A solderless [[breadboard]] is unsuitable for high-frequency and switching work precisely because its loose wires form large loops that act as antennas<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup>. The same rule governs bypassing: the larger the [[current]] loop area and the higher the frequency, the more strongly it radiates as an antenna, producing EMI and jeopardising CE and FCC compliance — hence the standing instruction to minimise loop area<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup>.

Test equipment suffers from this directly. An [[oscilloscope-probe]] with its earth lead attached is itself an antenna, picking up high-frequency impulses that vanish when the lead is disconnected<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1107" title="EEVblog #1107 - Shocking 4K BENQ Monitor Problem!">1107</a></sup>. Extended metalwork inside an instrument acts as a rather effective antenna, so probe pickup at the front of a CRT is enough to display the noise present<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup>. Probing an RF design under these conditions produces [[waveform|waveforms]] that look like design faults but are artefacts of the pickup, which is why probing technique dominates signal-integrity work in RF<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>.

Circuit inputs are equally exposed. A CMOS 4000-series input has essentially infinite [[input-impedance]], so a long unterminated switch line running alongside mains wiring readily picks up 50 Hz<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup>. Long input leads on a product function as antennas and are a standard reason to fit RF beads at the input alongside ESD and surge protection<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>. In EMC work, ferrite beads are applied along cables for the same reason, since a cable running to a metal chassis is itself an antenna<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup>. Internal transformers and their [[ac-coupling]] can act as effective low-frequency pickup antennas, which is why equipment that is switched off and disconnected from the mains can still show common-mode noise<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup>. Poor RF system design compounds the problem: a transmitter using a tiny coil as an antenna alongside a receiver with no shielding at all raises the broadband [[noise-floor]] rather than producing discrete interference<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup>.

The effect is sometimes deliberately exploited. Non-contact [[voltage]] detection in multimeters and socket testers uses a plain conductive strip along the top edge of the [[pcb]] as the antenna for picking up the electric field<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup>. Regenerative receivers have been built using the tank coil itself as the antenna<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup>.

## Antennas in products

Integration approaches vary widely. Antennas appear as PCB traces and patch structures, as separate daughterboards feeding an RF system-on-chip<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1453" title="EEVblog 1453 - Elgato Key Light TEARDOWN">1453</a></sup>, as modules with the antenna printed on the board and castellated edge connections for surface mounting<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup>, and as inductors on a key fob PCB<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cc_GqtBuP4c" title="Hyundai IONIQ Keyfob Teardown + Battery Replacement">•</a></sup>. Dedicated stand-off antennas — physically separated elements rather than a patch or PCB antenna — represent a materially better implementation where cost allows<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1514" title="EEVblog 1514 - Sonos: The Juicero of Wifi Speakers? (TEARDOWN)">1514</a></sup>. Metalwork can be pressed into service: a tablet with three antennas built into the back of the case connects to them [[via]] leaf spring contacts landing directly on the main PCB, one of them an NFC near-field antenna<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup>. Antennas have also been integrated into the moulded plastic of a device's outer cap<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1210" title="EEVblog #1210 - Sony Waterproof Earphones Teardown">1210</a></sup>, and formed as folded sheet-metal stubbies with the ground on one side and the two antenna feeds on the outer contacts<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup>. In a slim tablet-style enclosure the antennas may sit in dedicated plastic port sections with a matching element on the opposite side, feeding an internal wireless card<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1211" title="EEVblog 1211 - Apple&#x27;s Most Beautiful Failure. The G4 Cube">1211</a></sup>.

Older equipment shows the same functions built at larger scale — a coil concealed inside a plastic moulding on a 1990s handset<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-492" title="EEVBlog #492 - Vintage Motorola MicroTAC Mobile Phone Teardown">492</a></sup>, or an external whip stowed within the [[battery]] pack of a 1986 analogue phone<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-888" title="EEVblog #888 - Mailbag">888</a></sup>. An aircraft transponder rated at roughly 140 watts peak transmit power both receives and transmits on a single antenna<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1352" title="EEVblog #1352 - Aircraft Transponder TEARDOWN!">1352</a></sup>. Satellite handsets use proper [[coaxial-cable|coax]] board interconnects to their antenna connectors rather than wires soldered to the board<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-721" title="EEVblog #721 - Globalstar Satellite Phone Teardown">721</a></sup>.

## Measurement and installations

Radiated emissions testing is performed in an RF anechoic chamber with the equipment on a turntable and the measuring antenna at a distance of one to four metres, against defined upper limits for the relevant FCC requirements; a different antenna type is used for measurements above 1 GHz<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1516" title="EEVblog 1516 - Mailbag Bonanza!">1516</a></sup>. On a spectrum analyser, a simple whip antenna is enough to reveal harmonics radiated by digital equipment against a baseline noise floor around −105 dBm<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup>, and with a demodulator the same instrument can recover an FM signal off an attached antenna<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup>. RF bug sweepers are wideband amplifiers with a detector [[diode]] covering a few hundred kilohertz to 2 GHz, working only in close proximity to the antenna, which is why a room is swept physically rather than swept in frequency<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-956" title="EEVblog #956 - Countersurveillance Monitor Teardown">956</a></sup>.

At the transmitting end, an analogue television facility splits its output through a power divider into two feeders running approximately 180 m up the tower to what are effectively two antennas, an upper and a lower half; if one half is damaged by lightning or otherwise, the other can be switched into full operation at slightly reduced power<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-569" title="EEVblog #569 - Tour of an Analog TV Transmission Facility">569</a></sup>. The kilowatt-level RF from the exciter and power amplifier chain feeding the antenna is the genuinely dangerous part of such a plant<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup>. Deep-space ground antennas are large enough that the structure rides on a film of oil at three pads, each roughly the height of a person, with the main spars carrying the load down from the elevation axis<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1547" title="EEVblog 1547 (Part 1) - Contacting the Voyager 2 Space Probe">1547</a></sup>. Spacecraft antennas are manoeuvred as part of routine [[calibration]] — a magnetometer roll spins up the gyros and rotates the entire structure, a variation observable on the downlink from 15 billion kilometres away<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1547" title="EEVblog 1547 (Part 1) - Contacting the Voyager 2 Space Probe">1547</a></sup>.

Placement has consequences beyond reception. A roof-mounted log-periodic television antenna casting a shadow across a domestic solar array was enough to cost of the order of 20 percent of the array's output, sufficient reason to relocate the antenna entirely<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NoIjTK249D0" title="How a SMALL SHADOW can drop Solar Power Output by 20%!">•</a></sup>.

## Wireless power claims

Antennas feature heavily in wireless-power marketing that does not survive a power budget. Claims of charging a phone at up to 9 metres via a transmitter plugged into a wall socket and a receiving antenna attached to the handset are inconsistent with the inverse-square falloff of radiated power<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WiMe9peQbTQ" title="More Wireless Phone Charging BULLSHIT! - AirVolt">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4vHx-UyIM9M" title="eevBLAB #74 - 5G Causes EVERYTHING!">•</a></sup>. Energy-harvesting modules genuinely exist and will extract power from an antenna across roughly 60 Hz to 6 GHz, performing best between about 100 MHz and 2.5 GHz — but a system pumping one watt into patch antennas and harvesting it into capacitors is a demonstration of harvesting, not of useful wireless charging<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1408" title="EEVblog 1408 - $5M WiGL Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1408</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MCyLO-1grEk" title="WiGL Wireless Power BUSTED AGAIN!">•</a></sup>. The concept is old, having been attempted via air ionisation in the 1890s, and the modern restatement using antennas and RF sources does not change the underlying budget<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MCyLO-1grEk" title="WiGL Wireless Power BUSTED AGAIN!">•</a></sup>.

Antenna-themed claims also recur in consumer scams: a device advertised as turning existing house wiring into a superior antenna trades on the fact that an antenna is only a long wire, while asserting best-in-class capture area and VSWR without measurement<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LzAUa_FtuC0" title="Livewave SUPER ANTENNA! SCAM">•</a></sup>. Claims that an entire metallic object constitutes a complete antenna emitting a 2.3 Hz signal, with the field amplified through motion, likewise have no supporting mechanism<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uI62sAN5JzU" title="The Mysterious Buga Sphere Transmits LF Waves! (Alien Origin?)">•</a></sup>.
