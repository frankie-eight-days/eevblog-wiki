Conformal coating is a thin polymer film applied over a populated [[pcb|printed circuit board]] after assembly, following the contours of the board and its components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-355" title="EEVblog #355 - Dictaphone Dumpster Diving">355</a></sup> Its purpose is environmental: it keeps moisture off the laminate and out from between component leads, where condensation would otherwise combine with dirt and contamination to create low-[[impedance]] leakage paths that eventually short things out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup> It is standard practice in equipment expected to survive climates, weather and thermal cycling that a consumer board would never see — military hardware, automotive safety systems, marine electronics and outdoor power equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-821" title="EEVblog #821 - Mailbag">821</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup>

## Why it is applied

The failure mechanism the coating defends against is progressive rather than sudden. Moisture forms on the board and works its way between components, the film picks up dirt and gunk, and what began as a clean insulator becomes a conductive contaminant bridge until circuit nodes are effectively shorted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> Described in creepage terms, the coating stops moisture spreading across the PCB and so stops leakage tracking across the board surface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup>

The requirement follows the service environment. An airbag controller is coated because the units must work across an enormous range of climates in different countries, and reliability cannot be traded away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup> A hybrid solar [[inverter]] mounted outdoors is subject to sun, cold, thermal cycling and internal dew, so at least a partial coat over the components is expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> A portable [[battery]] station used camping, in a garage, in a trailer or in an RV warrants a coat for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup> A marine navigation instrument gets the underside of its digital [[processor]] board coated specifically for the marine environment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-821" title="EEVblog #821 - Mailbag">821</a></sup> An outdoor intercom board is coated to keep moisture out over the long term.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> A regulator intended for an outdoor RC aircraft carries one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> High-[[voltage]] circuitry is another natural case: a high-voltage multiplier board is coated because moisture and crap on the board would upset its operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1149" title="EEVblog #1149 - How An Electrostatic Whiteboard Works">1149</a></sup>

Coating is not universal even in demanding markets. A high-quality automotive board can be built to a very high standard — good fiberglass laminate, top-quality parts, careful engineering — with no conformal coating finish at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-958" title="EEVblog #958 - Mailbag">958</a></sup> Intrinsically safe instrumentation does not require coating as a condition of that rating, though it is frequently present anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup>

## Identifying a coated board

The usual visual tell is shine: a coated board carries a gloss or sheen that the bare [[solder-mask|solder mask]] does not, most visible on the sides and tops of chips where the coating has pooled over a non-planar surface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-456" title="EEVblog #456 - CSIRO Rubidium Frequency Standard">456</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1489" title="EEVblog 1489 - Mystery Teardown!">1489</a></sup> Raking light held nearly horizontal to the board brings it out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>

This test is not infallible, because some [[solder]] masks are themselves very glossy. The reliable discriminator is coverage: a conformal coating is sprayed onto the board after the components are loaded, so it appears over the chips and passives as well as the laminate, whereas solder mask stops at the bare board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-355" title="EEVblog #355 - Dictaphone Dumpster Diving">355</a></sup> A [[multimeter]] board that looks coated at first glance turns out under a microscope to be high-gloss solder mask with nothing at all over the components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup> The same ambiguity arises with a vintage meter showing a faint luster that resists classification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-930" title="EEVBlog #930 - Mailbag">930</a></sup> Older assemblies can also carry post-solder coatings that are neither ordinary solder mask nor an obvious lacquer, applied over the joints after [[soldering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-481" title="EEVblog #481 - Mailbag">481</a></sup>

## Partial and selective coating

In practice coverage is often incomplete, and the pattern of what was covered reveals how it was applied. A brushed-on coat leaves uneven patches with bare regions between them and the tops of chips and capacitors uncoated — plainly not a dipped board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> Marine gear may have the solder side coated and the component side left entirely bare.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-821" title="EEVblog #821 - Mailbag">821</a></sup> An [[led|LED]] driver may have coating on the primary side only, with the rest masked off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup> In a large battery product one board can be thoroughly coated while an adjacent board is not, the fan controller board coated and the bottom board apparently not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup>

Deliberate selective coating is a separate matter from sloppy coverage. Masking is used to keep coating off contacts and mechanisms that must stay clean: two pins masked off on a coated processor board, and the areas around tactile switches masked on a coated hardware wallet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-821" title="EEVblog #821 - Mailbag">821</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1006" title="EEVblog #1006 - Trezor Bitcoin Hardware Wallet Teardown">1006</a></sup> A power window controller has no coating over most of the board but a deliberate bead of blue coating placed around one [[microcontroller]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-h9V0qJ4p3Aw" title="2010 Nissan Dualis Power Window &quot;REPAIR&quot;">•</a></sup> Gunk applied only around the perimeter of a chip may serve as a local seal against dust or moisture rather than as board-level protection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-52" title="EEVblog #52 - Panasonic Plasma TV&#x27;s Suck (and a Teardown)">52</a></sup> On an active probe, coating over a section that is not the sensitive amplifier suggests a motivation other than moisture ingress.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>

Where a board is coated only partially in an assembly that already has a potting box around it, the engineering judgment is that the half-measure is not worth taking: pot the whole thing in a solid or re-enterable compound, or coat it properly, rather than pouring some coating over the top and calling it done.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-821" title="EEVblog #821 - Mailbag">821</a></sup> The boundary between the two techniques can blur — a very thick conformal coat, with a few components such as a [[current-sense-resistor|current sense resistor]] left exposed, does much of what potting does without turning the assembly into a solid brick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> Full potting, by contrast, ends the discussion: a potted sensor PCB simply cannot be examined.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup>

Coating also appears on things that are not conventional assemblies. The winding of a precision decade [[resistance]] box is coated to protect wire finer than a human hair, most likely by dipping the whole wound element after winding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup> A rubberized pedometer combines a coated board with a potted battery and an outer seal, an arrangement that would make the product completely waterproof.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-412" title="EEVblog #412 - Jawbone UP Pedometer Teardown">412</a></sup> Contract manufacturers offer conformal coating alongside assembly and test as a standard production service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1686" title="EEVblog 1686 - Electronex 2025 Melbourne - Walkaround">1686</a></sup>

Because coating is an additional production process with real cost, it is essentially absent from consumer computing hardware, and partially coating a notebook logic board against known water ingress paths would count as a drastic over-engineering measure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup>

## Consequences for service and repair

Every property that makes conformal coating good for reliability makes it hostile to repair. The coating is an insulator, so a probe tip resting on a coated pad with moderate pressure reads nothing at all; the tip must physically pierce the film, which requires genuinely sharp probes and real force.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1485" title="EEVblog 1485 - PedalCell CadenceX Bike Generator LOL FAIL!">1485</a></sup> Coating and other gunk over [[relay]] pins must be pierced the same way before a coil [[resistance-measurement|resistance measurement]] will read.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> Chemical strippers can remove coating selectively or across the whole board as an alternative to piercing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup>

Anything more invasive than probing means removing coating mechanically. Scraping the coating off a pin before it can be probed is the standard preliminary step on coated military power supplies, and on dense assemblies this turns [[troubleshooting]] into a slow, unpleasant exercise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-821" title="EEVblog #821 - Mailbag">821</a></sup> Repairing coated military equipment means scraping it all off, replacing possibly one part, and replacing it with the exact part rather than a functional equivalent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup> Coating between pins and around traces can be scraped out with a scalpel under a microscope when contamination is the suspected fault — a test that, when it changes nothing, usefully eliminates board leakage as the cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Removing a coated [[eeprom|EEPROM]] is markedly harder than removing an uncoated one, even with low-melting-point alloy, and risks damaging the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup>

Coating also obstructs identification. Part numbers are difficult or impossible to read through it, which is why coated boards are photographed with the illumination almost horizontal and the board turned to catch the marking at the right angle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1489" title="EEVblog 1489 - Mystery Teardown!">1489</a></sup> Failing that, the marking is scraped clear or read under a high-quality stereo microscope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup> Where a manufacturer has already sanded the markings off a controller, coating on top compounds the problem, though adjacent devices such as a [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] may still be readable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Coating over an unknown micro can defeat photography entirely without better lighting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup>

A coated board is also mechanically bonded in a way that is easy to forget. Coating bridges component bodies to the laminate, so lifting a part aside to see the trace beneath it — bending a resonator up roughly thirty degrees — can crack the coating or the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

## Applying coating during repair

Coating is a repair material as well as a manufacturing one. Where corrosion — from a leaking [[alkaline-battery|alkaline cell]], for example — has been cleaned back to bare copper, the exposed copper must not be left open; the rot is stopped and then the area is protected with either conformal coating or a UV-curable liquid solder mask.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup> An aerosol clear conformal coating is the convenient option for this, with the alternative of masking the board off properly first and using photo-imageable solder mask.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qmcSEJehqZM" title="Energizer Battery Leak Fluke 3000 Multimeter &quot;Repair&quot;">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup> Boards that have already been coated once tend to look rough after rework and cleaning, since the coating interferes with wicking and cleanup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup>
