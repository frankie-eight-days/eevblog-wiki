A current limit is the ceiling a power source imposes on the [[current]] it will deliver, enforced by folding back the output [[voltage]] once the load tries to draw more than the set value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OOFoYVQ7kVo" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU FAIL - PLEASE EXPLAIN">•</a></sup> On a bench supply it is the primary safety mechanism protecting an unknown or suspect circuit: a supply set for 24 V that hits a 0.1 A limit will simply sag to 15 V rather than deliver the power that would destroy the board under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> The limit is also the defining half of a bench supply's dual personality — below the threshold it behaves as a constant [[voltage-source]], above it as a [[constant-current-source]] — and a supply that cannot hold that limit under a [[short-circuit]] is considered defective, since surviving a shorted output is the entire point of having the feature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OOFoYVQ7kVo" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU FAIL - PLEASE EXPLAIN">•</a></sup>

## Setting a limit before applying power

Standard practice on unknown hardware is to power it from an adjustable bench supply with the current limit deliberately set close to the expected draw, so that a fault cannot escalate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup> The value is chosen from what the circuit ought to consume: 6 V at 100 mA for a keyboard controller that should never draw more than that, which then measured 4 mA in operation<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup>; 130 mA at 5 V for a prototype whose faults were still unknown, explicitly to protect the circuit if something went wrong<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup>; 0.1 A at 24 V, only 2.4 W, on a repair where the goal was merely to see whether the display lit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>. Where the draw is genuinely unknown the limit is set generously — 7.2 V at 1 A on a camera teardown, 1.7 A on a laptop that turned out to draw 80 mA — on the reasoning that a wrong guess in the loose direction still bounds the damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-625" title="EEVblog #625 - Retro Teardown: Sony&#x27;s First Digital Camera">625</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> For higher-power work the same logic scales: 40 V with a 5 A limit gives a 200 W envelope, and 40 V at half an amp is used to bound the potential damage on a supply already known to have caught fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup>

The limit is also matched to the weakest component in the chain rather than to the supply's capability. An LM317L has a 100 mA internal limit, so the bench supply feeding it is set to the same 100 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>

## Reading the limit as a diagnostic

Because entering current limit is visible on the front panel, the limit doubles as a fault indicator. A circuit sitting at 197 mA against a 200 mA setting, with its LEDs at abnormal brightness, is not working — it is pinned against the limit, which is itself the diagnosis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Unexpected limiting during a bring-up prompted a check that revealed the supply leads were reversed and the board's reverse-protection [[diode]] was conducting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> The inverse error also occurs: apparent hiccupping on a PC repair was initially blamed on an insufficient 3.5 A limit before being traced to the unit's own internal supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup> A controller rated at 1.1 A that overloads and drops out even with a 1.5 A limit available points to a fault rather than a supply shortcoming.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup>

## Choosing the value

The right value depends on what is being tested. For a finished product expected to work, the limit is set to the supply's full capability — 3 A on a converter needing about 1 A — because there is nothing to protect against.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> For a design still under development, a limit just above the expected draw, such as 1.1 A per channel, is the safe choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Setting a low limit is not free, however: power-on surge currents can push a supply into limiting, collapsing the rail and upsetting the converter being powered, so a nominally safe setting can itself create the fault it was meant to prevent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

Adjustable limits are also used as a fixed guard on shared equipment, set once by a pot so that an operator cannot damage an expensive device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-576" title="EEVblog #576 - Advantest R6142 Current Voltage Generator">576</a></sup> Sweeping the limit is a measurement technique in its own right — ramping from 0 A to 1 A in a hundred steps against a 10 V compliance produces a characteristic curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup>

## Implementation in supplies and regulators

In a discrete design the limit is set by comparing the drop across a sense [[resistor]] to an adjustable threshold. In one lab supply design the current-adjust pot develops 250 mV for a 250 mA limit, a direct 1 mV per mA correspondence that can be verified by probing the pot's wiper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> An LT3080-based topology uses two regulators, one for constant current and one for constant voltage, with the current set pin spanning 0 to 1 V to represent 0 to 1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> A two-[[transistor]] arrangement gives the current regulation and voltage regulation separate pass elements; when the supply is not in current limit the current pass transistor is effectively a short and the voltage element does the work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

Some monolithic parts expose the limit externally: a driver with a built-in 4.75 V reference and 31.6 k resistor sets its limit from the total [[resistance]] seen at a pin, or from a DAC, and the same part appears as an adjustable ±500 mA supply with adjustable current limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> Higher up the scale, digital instruments set voltage and current limits per channel from multiplexed DACs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup> [[usb]] Power Delivery specifies an adjustable current limit alongside its 3.3 V to 48 V adjustable output, in 50 mA steps, though implementing it in a power brick is uncommon.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup>

Fixed regulators generally carry an unadjustable internal limit whose purpose is self-preservation rather than load protection — a jelly bean regulator folds back to 30 mA into a short, and the concern is that the regulator survives a shorted load and recovers, not that the load is protected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Linear regulators in older instruments often combine both: an external current limit resistor at the output plus a limit inside the regulator chip, with an LM723-style device providing a dedicated current limit pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup>

## Output capacitance

Output [[capacitance]] directly undermines [[current-limiting]]. When the limit engages, a large output [[capacitor]] dumps its stored charge into the load, delivering current well over the set value before the loop can respond, so a constant-current supply should carry the minimum possible output capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> The requirement conflicts with noise performance and loop stability, since some capacitance is needed both to lower output noise and for the current limit loop itself, which makes the value a deliberate compromise rather than a free choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

## Setting and displaying the limit

Older supplies have no way to preview the setting: the traditional method is to short the output and adjust the pot until the meter reads the desired limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup> Better designs avoid this with a front-panel shorting switch, a dedicated set-current button, or a set-current display mode that shows the threshold without loading the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup> A single-turn pot is workable for current adjustment where a multi-turn is considered essential for voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup> Failing to display the active limit at all is a design fault — a supply set to 100 mA that never shows the figure leaves the operator guessing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Interfaces that bury the setting compound the problem, since a limit left at an odd value by a previous user can be difficult to find and change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 2 of 2 - Review of the BK Precision 1697 Programmable Lab Power Supply">8</a></sup>

## Failure to limit

Supplies do fail at this. Repeatedly shorting a 30 V, 3 A channel with [[relay]] contacts destroyed it: instead of limiting cleanly at 3 A the series pass transistor failed, after which the channel could not drive even 10 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> The expectation is unambiguous — a supply must not exceed its set voltage and must not exceed its set current limit, on power-up or otherwise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OOFoYVQ7kVo" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU FAIL - PLEASE EXPLAIN">•</a></sup> [[firmware]] faults appear too: one supply reported a spurious 30 to 40 mA with no load attached whenever the limit was set anywhere below 10 mA, later corrected in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup>

Limiting behaviour is also tested against active loads, where an [[electronic-load]]'s own control loop can interact with the supply's constant current loop; a well-behaved pair enters limit without oscillation even at 80 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Universal programmers have been observed self-testing the feature by shorting their own programming rails to confirm the limit engages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup>
