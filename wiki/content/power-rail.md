A power rail is a distributed supply net inside a product — a nominal DC [[voltage]] carried by traces, planes or wiring from a regulator to everything that draws from it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Nearly every fault-finding session begins at the rails, because a supply that is absent, sagging, shorted or noisy explains almost any downstream symptom, and because rails are the easiest thing in a circuit to measure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-147" title="EEVblog #147 - Agilent 3000 $12000 Smoke">147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> Modern digital hardware has multiplied them: a design that once needed a single 5-volt supply now routinely carries five, ten or more distinct rails, each with its own regulator, tolerance and sequencing requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1429" title="EEVblog 1429 - Sony RX100 Camera Troubleshooting Part 2">1429</a></sup>

## Rail proliferation

The count of rails in an instrument tracks the complexity of its silicon. A high-end [[fpga|FPGA]] alone can demand core, auxiliary, [[battery]]-backup, I/O pre-driver, I/O, PLL digital and PLL analog supplies, reaching ten separate power rails on one device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Seven or eight rails is a common implementation, and four, five or more is unremarkable for high-end parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> An isolated [[oscilloscope]] design needed roughly five rails clustered at 1.1, 1.15, 1.2 and 1.8 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup>

Teardowns show the same pattern. A Keysight 1000 X-Series scope carries 1.8, 3.3, 1.2, 1 volt and around 2 volts, all feeding the FPGA and other digital cores.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> A Rigol 7000 scope labels 1.2, 1.0, 0.9, 1, 2.0, 1.8, 1.5, 1, two separate 3.3-volt rails and a pair of 5.5-volt rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup> An Agilent 4000X spreads 1, 1.2, 1.4, 1.8, 2.5, 5, 12 and 14 volts across its planes — more voltage rails than you can poke a stick at, but typical of such system designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup> A Sony RX100 camera reaches further down, with regulators at 0.4, 1.1, 1.2, 1.8, 2.5 and 3.3 volts, several distinct 1.1-volt rails among them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1429" title="EEVblog 1429 - Sony RX100 Camera Troubleshooting Part 2">1429</a></sup> One high-cost video board derives 0.9, 1.8 and 3.3-volt rails from a 5-volt input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup>

Analog sections drive the count the other way. A [[signal-generator|signal generator]] using ±20-volt peak-to-peak output stages needs ±12 or ±15-volt rails kept separate and quiet from the digital supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup> Another arbitrary generator runs ±17 volts for the analog sections, −8.5 and +6 volts for the digital, with the +6 volts serving as standby for a soft power button, and the FPGA dropping 3.3 volts down to 1.0 and 1.2-volt core rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Not every design escalates: a camera board whose FPGA needed only one 2.8-volt rail could keep its power distribution simple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

## Checking rails

The first move in [[troubleshooting]] is to measure voltages — thou shalt check your power rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-147" title="EEVblog #147 - Agilent 3000 $12000 Smoke">147</a></sup> The check is fast and it rules out a whole class of cause: a picoammeter showing 5.02 volts on its 5-volt rail plus healthy ±15 volts is simply not a supply problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup> The same reasoning cleared an Amiga 2000 at 4.93 volts on the 5-volt rail, a Compucorp calculator at 4.9 volts with no [[ripple]], and an intercom at 5.21 volts, slightly high but in spec.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Gbn_51IoJiM" title="Amiga 2000 Teardown &amp; Power Up">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Rails can be verified without a [[schematic]] by probing a chip with a known pinout — pins four and eight of an 8-pin [[eeprom|EEPROM]] are its supply pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Where a board silkscreens its rail names, or a test point is labelled, that labelling is worth looking for first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup>

A rail measuring an unexpected but suspiciously round value is usually intentional rather than faulty; 6.5 volts arriving where 3.3 or 5 was expected turned out to be the design value, distributed to a second board as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup> Linear-regulated rails rarely warrant a ripple hunt at all once the DC values check out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup>

Rails also fail together in ways that point upstream: five separate linear rails on the secondary side of a switch-mode supply dropping simultaneously indicates a common cause ahead of the regulators, not five faults.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Conversely, healthy rails do not close the investigation — an Agilent 3000 that emitted smoke had every rail spot on, and a spectrum analyser with good-looking rails still threw the same gate-array error, sending the work back to the systematic troubleshooting guide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-147" title="EEVblog #147 - Agilent 3000 $12000 Smoke">147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup>

## Shorts and resistance

Before applying power to a new or repaired board, the rails are buzzed out against [[ground]] to confirm nothing is shorted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> The check protects the circuitry: replacing a blown [[fuse]] into a still-shorted rail simply destroys something else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> [[flux|Flux]] residue left on pins can corrupt such measurements, so probe placement matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup>

Where a short is suspected but not obvious, the [[resistance]] of the rail itself becomes the measurement. Roughly 0.016 ohms on a rail is an extraordinarily low resistance for a power rail, and correlates with a board drawing double what the manual says the supply can deliver on that rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Comparable rails on the same board measured about 0.11 and 0.16 ohms, and the figures proved repeatable enough to compare against each other — including after a suspect chip was removed pin by pin so the rail could be measured again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

The complementary technique is to measure [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] along the rail rather than resistance across it, walking from the [[connector]] inward: 3.032 volts at the connector, 2.96 volts at a decoupling cap, 2.93 volts at an [[asic|ASIC]], the falls attributable to plane and track resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Powering rails individually also reveals coupling — energising other rails pulled one reading from 10.5 volts down to 8.6, [[current]] finding its way through protection diodes on the unpowered rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Thermal imaging with a single rail energised isolates which circuitry that rail actually feeds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> A current probe that senses the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] of a trace can trace a short running internally through a power plane inside a [[multilayer-pcb|multilayer PCB]], where no surface access exists.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

Excess current draw is a gross indicator worth taking early: a [[power-supply|power supply]] board alone drawing 90 milliamps is a plausible quiescent figure, while the full system pulling nearly 2 amps against a 1.3-amp rating is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Ceramic capacitors sit across power rails, so a cracked ceramic that goes [[short-circuit|short circuit]] sits directly across a low-[[impedance]] supply — in a high-current automotive controller that can dump a thousand amps into the part and set it on fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> A [[capacitor]] failing open often does no harm; failing short is the dangerous case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup>

SCR latch-up is an inherent hazard of CMOS construction: parasitic bipolar transistors formed in the silicon substrate between the positive supply rail and ground form a structure electrically identical to a silicon controlled rectifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16" title="EEVblog #16 - CMOS SCR Latchup Tutorial">16</a></sup> Once triggered it latches, shorting the power rail inside the chip and staying shorted, hence the name.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16" title="EEVblog #16 - CMOS SCR Latchup Tutorial">16</a></sup> Because the parasitic structure lies in the substrate rather than the designed circuit, it is largely independent of what the chip does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16" title="EEVblog #16 - CMOS SCR Latchup Tutorial">16</a></sup>

Rails also fail from the supply side. A brownout — the rail dipping below a device's low-voltage detect threshold and recovering — is a documented [[microcontroller]] hazard on a 5-volt or 3.3-volt rail, addressed by selectable low-voltage-reset levels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup> A shorted protection [[diode]] allowed the full power of one supply to feed into an analog rail, pushing a −15-volt rail to −19 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> Mechanical failure counts too: a mini PC would not start because its main power rail was carried through the [[pcb|PCB]] standoffs, and pressing down to restore that contact booted it immediately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1739" title="EEVblog 1739 - UNUSUAL FAULT! in a Beelink Ryzen 9 Mini PC">1739</a></sup>

Shorting a rail is easy to do by accident. Clipping an earth-referenced oscilloscope's ground lead to a power rail on a mains-earth-referenced product shorts the supply through the scope earth, rebooting or destroying the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Whether the result is an explosion or merely a dead product depends on how much current the supply can deliver, but neither outcome is wanted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Exposed through-hole pins that reach the [[ground-plane|ground plane]] and a 12-volt rail can short the rails together if the board is set down on a conductive surface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup>

## Layout, bypassing and schematics

Rails compete with ground for board area. A power rail routed straight through a region splits the ground plane around it, forcing return currents on a long detour and enlarging the loop area between chips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> On boards with many rails, the usual compromise is one or two full ground planes plus a couple of layers dedicated to power, with individual rails routed as paths on those layers rather than each receiving a plane of its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Power and ground generally belong on inner layers; flood-filled power on the outer layers is an exception for simple power requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

Plane [[capacitance]] between a power plane and ground does not substitute for local bypassing. The capacitance is spread across the whole plane area, but a chip needs it right at the pin being decoupled, and the capacitance available in that small region is very small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Multiple bypass capacitors per rail — a 1 µF, a 100 nF, a 10 nF and perhaps a 1 nF in parallel — are common, and a chip with multiple power rails bypasses each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> Modern FPGAs and processors are stricter still, shipping 50-page documents covering how to power up, bypass and sequence the rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup>

In schematic drafting the convention is power at the top and ground at the bottom, with decoupling capacitors drawn physically close to the pins they decouple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> On breadboards the same distribution problem appears as power strips: a double-width board with extra power strips down the sides, rather than only along the top, accommodates the several different supply voltages a modern build needs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup> The idea is old — 1970s microprocessor development kits already ran power strips down the middle of their prototyping area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup>

## Identifying rails on an unknown board

[[reverse-engineering|Reverse engineering]] a board usually starts by classifying nets as power or signal. Thick traces, especially pairs of them, and pads carrying multiple vias, mark power and ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-849" title="EEVblog #849 - Sony E-Mount Camera Lens Teardown">849</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup> Fat traces bridged by a zero-ohm jumper serve the same role on single-sided boards built down to a price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> Long traces running past a row of bypass capacitors are likely a rail feeding those caps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lDQdA4Ml5GA" title="What do I sound like with my pitch lowered?">•</a></sup> Traces in series with a chip's supply, feeding through resistors and transistors, indicate a switched or sensed power line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup> On an unknown SOIC-8, the standard assumption is that one corner pin is ground and its diagonal opposite is power, leaving the remainder to analyse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup>

An oscilloscope distinguishes rails from logic outputs by [[waveform]] shape: a slow exponential decay over milliseconds at power-down is a classic power rail collapsing, not a logic output driven low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup> When tracing a board manually, printing the layers onto transparencies and highlighting ground in one colour and the positive rail in another keeps the net assignment straight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup>

## Rail noise and probing

Measuring a rail's AC performance rather than its DC value calls for a dedicated power rail probe — a relatively cheap accessory with high [[bandwidth]] and a large built-in [[dc-offset|DC offset]] range, since the task is resolving small [[noise]] signals riding on a high DC voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s6lVvIWWNBw" title="Can VoiceBox AI Clone Dave&#x27;s Voice?">•</a></sup> A 2-GHz power rail probe used on a battery-powered Raspberry Pi's 5-volt rail exceeded the 1-GHz bandwidth of the scope it fed, and compared closely against a far more expensive matched active probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> The offset range varies with the volts-per-division setting, which is a common trap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s6lVvIWWNBw" title="Can VoiceBox AI Clone Dave&#x27;s Voice?">•</a></sup> What the probe shows depends heavily on where on the rail it is connected, on the decoupling present, and on trace and loop [[inductance]] between source and load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup>

Rail noise reaches analog circuitry through the supply rather than the signal path, so its effect on an op-amp is governed by that amplifier's power supply rejection ratio rather than by input coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup> Where a supply proved noisy after the fact, a series [[inductor]] bodged into the rail — effectively breaking the trace to insert it — is a recognisable field fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> Series power resistors on main rails are a normal source of heat, running up to around 70 °C in a television power supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup>

## Design-time estimation

Rail current need not be guessed. FPGA toolchains report [[power-dissipation|power dissipation]] broken down by block type and by hierarchy, and produce an estimated current draw for each individual power rail — including static current on internal supplies — though I/O figures depend on an assumed toggle rate and remain estimates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-636" title="EEVblog #636 - FPGA Demo Boards - DE0 Nano">636</a></sup>
