The watt hour (Wh) is the unit of electrical energy formed by multiplying power in watts by time in hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Energy is the usage of power over time, so its derived units can equally be expressed as kilowatt-hours, watt-hours or watt-seconds, all following from energy equals power times time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Power is instantaneous and cannot be stored; energy can be, and the watt hour is how a stored quantity of it is stated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> For batteries the watt hour is the only measure that captures the true capacity, because it accounts for both [[current]] and [[voltage]] rather than assuming one of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>

The rating is directly usable: a 1 Wh [[battery]] can deliver 1 W for one hour or 0.1 W for ten hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> A 25 Wh cell could potentially deliver 25 W for an hour or 1 W for 25 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> A 5,000 mAh lithium polymer pack at a nominal 3.7 V works out to 18.5 Wh, which corresponds to roughly 1 W continuous for eighteen and a half hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup>

## Why watt hours and not amp hours

Amp hours and milliamp hours measure charge, not energy, and take no account of voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> They are a more simplistic figure that only becomes a capacity number once a nominal voltage is assumed across the discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> [[battery-voltage|Battery voltage]] is not constant — it sags through the discharge and then tapers off before dying — so the watt hour figure is the correct energy capacity precisely because it takes that drop into account.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Quoting energy capacity in milliamp hours is not defensible engineering practice; capacity should always be specified in watt hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

The true capacity is the area under the discharge curve — the integral of the power curve over time — not a point on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> For a graph of voltage versus time, that integral is what the milliamp hour or watt hour capacity actually represents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup>

In practice the two figures often land close together, which is why many batteries are specified in amp hours with a constant current discharge assumed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Under a [[constant-current-load|constant current load]] the errors introduced by the nominal-voltage assumption tend to cancel in terms of area, so the amp hour capacity at a given constant current can be similar to the watt hour capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup>

Where they diverge is with loads that are not constant current. Most real circuits are neither constant [[resistance]] nor constant current but constant power, since a DC-to-DC converter driving a circuit draws essentially constant power from the battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup> For such a design the constant power discharge graph, and with it the watt hour capacity, is the more relevant characteristic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup> Constant resistance testing — literally a 10 ohm or 100 ohm [[resistor]] across the battery — matches almost nothing that gets designed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> With a constant power discharge, remaining capacity can be read directly as a percentage off the x-axis of the discharge curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>

## Measuring watt hours

The traditional method is to discharge the battery at a defined load while logging voltage and current at regular intervals, then computing the total watt hour capacity from that data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Each interval yields its own watt hour increment, and the increments are accumulated to a running total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Doing this properly requires two differential amplifiers and a means of logging — a data acquisition card into a PC, or a pair of logging multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> Amp hour measurement by contrast needs only a battery, a dumb constant current load built from a FET and an [[op-amp|op amp]], a [[multimeter]] and a stopwatch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>

A watt hour total can also be reconstructed after the fact. Multiplying average voltage by capacity in milliamp hours gives around 2.6 Wh for an AA alkaline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> A CR2032 coin cell at 235 mAh over an average of roughly 2.8 V gives its energy capacity closely enough without integrating the whole curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1383" title="EEVblog 1383 - Human Battery Energy Harvesting BUSTED">1383</a></sup> A lithium thionyl chloride cell at 2.6 Ah and 3.6 V nominal comes to 9.36 Wh.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup> Elapsed time against a known constant load works the same way: 68,625 seconds at a continuous 20 W converts to about 381 Wh.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IXVTMUQGN5U" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery Capacity Test #2">•</a></sup>

Measurement setup matters at the [[connector]]. A test taken through a barrel-jack-to-barrel-jack connection with lead and contact losses returns a true minimum figure rather than the highest accurate one; a proper four-terminal measurement at the load would only increase the watt hour result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IXVTMUQGN5U" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery Capacity Test #2">•</a></sup>

## Instrument support

Watt hour capability is not universal on test gear, and its absence is a real limitation for battery work. Electronic loads that offer only amp hours in their battery test mode force the watt hour calculation to be done manually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lYKjScnkeq0" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery: Capacity Test">•</a></sup> The Rigol DL3021 displays voltage, current, milliamp hours, watt hours and a timer in battery mode, but while stop conditions can be set on voltage, current and time, no stop value can be set on watt hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> The Rohde & Schwarz NGP800 performs energy calculation and reads out in watt hours or milliwatt hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> The Uni-T UTE310 provides an integrate mode that integrates power to produce a watt hour figure over an arbitrary logging period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> Bench meters may offer a dedicated energy mode where the units switch from watts to watt hours or milliwatt hours once a timer starts accumulating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> The µSupply adds a watt hour display alongside a joules readout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

Recorded test results illustrate the resolution involved: 2.06 Wh for an AA alkaline discharged at 250 mA<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>; 10.38 Wh alongside 8,333 mAh over 8 hours 21 minutes for an NiMH cell<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9FCzAgJhRdc" title="NiMH 8000mAh Capacity Claim TESTED">•</a></sup>; and 0.024 Wh after 24 seconds at the start of a long alkaline leakage run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RFb3TwWzza0" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Test Setup">•</a></sup>

## Input-referred versus output-referred ratings

[[usb|USB]] battery banks expose the sharpest consequence of unit choice. A pack labelled 20,000 mAh also carries a watt hour figure — 77 Wh, derived from that charge at the internal cell voltage of 3.85 V — and that watt hour figure is the one intended for comparing packs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup> A constant current test at a 5 W load, taking twelve hours, returned 61.8 Wh, well under the 77 Wh spec.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup>

The discrepancy is a referencing problem. The 20,000 mAh figure is input-referred, or battery-referred: it describes the cell inside, before the DC-to-DC converter that drives the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup> The internal battery may genuinely hold 77 Wh and meet its spec, but the input-referred number excludes converter [[efficiency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup> Measured output-referred energy of 61.8 Wh against 77 Wh input-referred implies an 80% efficient DC-to-DC converter, and the same referencing trick appears across many products where efficiency is left out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup>

## Beyond batteries

Domestic electricity is metered in kilowatt hours, the same power-times-time construction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> [[solar-power-generation|Solar production]] is reported the same way: 511 Wh from an eastern-roof panel over a full solar production day, with western-roof panels yielding about 2.5 times more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5x8UVZnwyL8" title="New Enphase Solar Power System - 1st Day">•</a></sup> For solar surface-area analysis, a peak irradiance figure converts to daily energy through the area under the daily curve — a best-case multiplier of seven turning 90 W per square metre peak into 630 Wh per square metre per day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup>

Portable power stations are named for their energy: the Goal Zero Yeti 400 is a 400 Wh battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1707" title="EEVblog 1707 - REPAIR: Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery">1707</a></sup> Capacity tests on one returned figures close to that nameplate — roughly 400 Wh on one run and 381 Wh on another, and 305.6 Wh over 7 hours 4 minutes before a fault forced a power cycle and lost the accumulated total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IXVTMUQGN5U" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery Capacity Test #2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lYKjScnkeq0" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery: Capacity Test">•</a></sup> At household scale the same arithmetic sizes a grid outage: 41% remaining on a 3,600 Wh reserve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JSahPkUjDYA" title="Sydney Power FAIL!">•</a></sup>
