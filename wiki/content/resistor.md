A resistor is a two-terminal passive component whose sole electrical function is to oppose the flow of [[current]], and it is the most ubiquitous part in electronics — the component whose value, tolerance, power rating and placement decide the behaviour of almost every circuit around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> Unlike a [[capacitor]] or an [[inductor]] it stores no energy: it dissipates power as heat while [[voltage]] is applied, and the instant the voltage is removed there is nothing left in it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> That simplicity is deceptive, because nearly every practical design problem — setting a current, dividing a voltage, sensing a load, consolidating a [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]], keeping a part from cooking itself — comes back to choosing a resistor.

## Fundamental behaviour

The [[resistance]] value determines how many electrons can get through, and the voltage across the resistor determines how hard they are pushed; a higher voltage across the same resistor forces more current through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> The voltage across a resistor is a measure of the energy difference between the electrons entering and those leaving, so increasing either the current or the value raises the [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> This is Ohm's law, and it is the relationship on which everything else rests.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> Resistors are not polarity sensitive and can be fitted either way round,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup> although in a simulator a resistor still has an assigned direction, which determines the sign of the current reported through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-516" title="EEVblog #516 - LTSPICE Tutorial - DC Operating Point Analysis">516</a></sup>

An ideal resistor across an AC source produces no phase relationship between voltage and current — the resistive part of an [[impedance]] is its real component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Real resistors are not ideal: a resistor has a small amount of [[inductance]] in it, just as an inductor has [[capacitance]] between its windings, and there is no such thing as a purely resistive or purely reactive circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Resistors also generate thermal Johnson [[noise]], and the higher the resistor value the more thermal noise it contributes — a term that sits outside the input noise of the [[op-amp|op amp]] it surrounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> Resistance is temperature dependent in ways that matter at extremes: a 1.5 kΩ resistor can rise to roughly 20 kΩ at 0.01 K above absolute zero, a property exploited to make simple chip thermometers for dilution refrigerators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup>

## Tolerance and value distribution

A [[datasheet|data sheet]] states a tolerance — ±1% for a typical metal film part — but says nothing about how values are distributed inside that band.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> Measured in bulk, the spread of a nominally 1% batch follows a Gaussian distribution centred on the nominal value: most parts sit within about ±0.1%, a fair number reach ±0.2%, and by ±0.5% there is almost nothing left, with the odds of finding a part out at 0.8% bordering on zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup> A batch of 1,000 resistors plotted against percentage deviation showed a total spread of only about ±0.6% against a ±1% specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup> This behaviour is characteristic of a reputable manufacturer's process and cannot be assumed of cheap unbranded parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup>

A common beginner error is to assume tolerances add arithmetically: putting four 100 Ω ±1% resistors in series does not give 400 Ω ±4%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Because the individual deviations are randomly distributed about the nominal, series and parallel combinations tend to average out rather than accumulate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Precision parts do exist for the cases that need them — 0.05% resistors are ordinary purchasable stock,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1279" title="EEVblog #1279 - Best Dumpster PC Find Yet!">1279</a></sup> and 10 kΩ 0.005% 50 ppm and 1 kΩ 0.01% 100 ppm parts serve as bench references for checking a [[multimeter]]'s absolute accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup>

## Power dissipation and derating

Power in a resistor is I²R, and the resulting temperature rise above ambient is set by the part's [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]], which for a given package size is roughly the same regardless of resistance value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> A resistor cannot be made to dissipate its rated power without its temperature rising.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup>

Every resistor data sheet carries a derating curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> A quarter-watt part is rated at its full nominal power only up to a maximum ambient of 70 °C; above that the permissible dissipation falls linearly to zero at the part's limit temperature, 155 °C in one typical case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> The 70 °C figure is [[ambient-temperature|ambient temperature]], not the resistor's own temperature rise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> Some data sheets additionally give a thermal resistance graph relating dissipated power to temperature rise above ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup>

Lead length matters. The [[pcb|PCB]] itself acts as a [[heat-sink|heat sink]] for a through-hole resistor, drawing heat out of the resistor core through the leads, so trimming the leads close to the board improves dissipation; with 25–30 mm of lead length the effect largely disappears and the heat stays in the core.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> This heating can be used deliberately: passing current through a resistor with a cord tied around it weakens the cord until it breaks, which is enough to trigger a mechanical release.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup>

Getting the wattage wrong is a common and destructive mistake. Discharging a cell through a 10 Ω resistor demands close to 0.9 W, which a quarter-watt part cannot survive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RFb3TwWzza0" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Test Setup">•</a></sup> Choosing a resistor an order of magnitude too low in value can put it far outside its rating, cook it until it cracks, and take it open circuit — after which the same failure repeats on the next power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Spreading the load helps: ten parallel resistors of half a watt each give 5 W of dissipation capability in a [[current-shunt|current shunt]], so the shunt barely warms and its value barely drifts with temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Splitting a shunt into several resistors also gives it a fault-survival margin, so a software error that dumps the full [[output-current|output current]] through it does not immediately blow it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Where higher dissipation or voltage rating is wanted in [[surface-mount-technology|surface mount]], the larger 1206 package is used for exactly that reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup>

## Choosing values

The E12 and E96 series constrain what is actually available. An [[lm317|LM317]] feedback calculation that lands on 360 Ω is served by an E12 330 Ω plus a 33 Ω in series, giving 363 Ω — near enough for the purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> A required gain that produces an awkward resistor ratio is a good reason to restructure a design rather than hunt for exotic E96 values; a gain of ten is easy with E12 parts, an arbitrary ratio is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-572" title="EEVblog #572 - Cascading Opamps For Increased Bandwidth">572</a></sup> A resistive scope probe built around a 1 kΩ resistor gives an inconvenient 21:1 division ratio that no standard scope attenuation setting matches, whereas an E96 value just over 950 Ω yields a usable 20:1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>

Many circuits do not need precision at all. An order-of-magnitude approach is sufficient for an [[led|LED]] dropper: with a 10 V supply and a target current somewhere between 1 mA and 10 mA, any value between 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ will light the LED, so a resistor can be pulled from the parts bin without a calculator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> Where the calculation does matter it is straightforward — 1.6 V across the dropper at 250 mA gives 6.4 Ω, rounded to the nearest E12 value of 6.8 Ω, with the wattage checked by I²R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup>

## Bill-of-materials consolidation

Each distinct resistor value on a board is another reel, another slot in the pick-and-place machine and another BOM line, so it can be cheaper and better to use more resistors of a value already in use than to introduce a new one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Two 10 kΩ resistors in parallel substitute for a 5 kΩ; two in series substitute for a 20 kΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> A 15 kΩ pull-up is rarely worth its own line item if 10 kΩ is already loaded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> The stated ideal of this practice — the holy grail of electronics design — is a board using one resistor value and one capacitor value throughout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The same logic drives jellybean choices: because the LM317 data sheet's nominal fixed leg is 240 Ω, keeping 240 Ω permanently loaded on the machine and reusing it elsewhere is preferable to adding a nearby 220 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The habit extends to hand assembly, where an 820 Ω part can serve as an LED dropper specifically to avoid depleting stock of the more useful 1 kΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> For bench stock, sorting by E12 value into drawers — all the decades of 10, then 12, 15, 18, 22 and so on together — keeps 10 Ω, 100 Ω, 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ in one place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup>

## Roles in circuits

**[[current-limiting|Current limiting]].** An LED is a current-driven device and needs a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] to limit its current, without which it will draw excessive power and be destroyed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> The same applies to seven-segment displays, where omitting the resistor destroys segments outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s2KkgI-kyK0" title="The Signal Path Lab">•</a></sup> A design that drives multiple segments from a [[shift-register|shift register]] without a dropper per segment can exceed the driver chip's maximum current, and the fix costs only five resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> Where LEDs are paralleled, matched droppers share current well: 82 Ω per LED at about 20.8 mA produces the expected 1.7 V across each resistor, confirming even sharing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup>

**Voltage division.** In its most basic form a [[resistor-divider|resistive divider]] is two resistors, with an output always some fraction of the input, and dividers of this kind are everywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Dividers set [[comparator]] thresholds and Schmitt trigger hysteresis, where 9 kΩ and 1 kΩ against ±10 V rails give convenient round threshold numbers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> A Wheatstone bridge is nothing more than four resistors forming two dividers, with a voltmeter or electrometer across the centre.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup>

**Setting a parameter.** Regulator and reference output voltages are set by a resistor ratio,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> and some regulators set [[output-voltage|output voltage]] with a single resistor, lowering parts count relative to the traditional two-resistor approach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1" title="EEVblog #1 - Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope Review">1</a></sup> [[battery|Battery]] charger ICs commonly set charge current with one resistor [[via]] a formula given in the data sheet — 25 mA for a 50 mAh cell charged at half C, for example.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Such values can be genuinely critical: a 1.1 kΩ part calculated from a high-voltage supply's formula to give 160 V cannot be casually substituted with a 1 kΩ, which is the kind of choice that warrants an engineering note on the [[schematic]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup>

**Sensing.** A resistor in series with a supply converts current pulses into a measurable voltage; 10 Ω is chosen to be high enough that CPU current pulses produce tens or hundreds of millivolts rather than microvolts, but low enough not to drop the rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> Multimeter current ranges work the same way, with [[range-switch|range switch]] contacts shorting out shunt sections so that a 1 Ω resistor becomes the shunt on a 50 mA range, giving 50 mV full scale.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Shunt values are trimmed by paralleling — a 1 kΩ across a 1 Ω to bring it slightly under nominal<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> — or built up from parallel parts, as with two 6R2s giving 3.1 Ω to set roughly 400 mA of charge current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> [[burden-voltage|Burden voltage]] is the unavoidable consequence: even a 1 Ω shunt drops 100 mV at 100 mA, and a 100 Ω shunt drops 10 V, which will collapse a 3.3 V rail entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

**Mechanical and layout roles.** Through-hole resistors are 0.4 inches across and can be used to jump four, five or six traces at once, which is a significant routing aid on a double-sided board and something surface mount gives up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Zero-ohm resistors are jumper links, plated straight through, and boards have been seen packed with them purely to avoid the cost of a double-sided PCB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LAbWsqS51Tk" title="When you REALLY don&#x27;t want to pay for a Double Sided PCB">•</a></sup> Optional fit-or-omit resistors combined with [[solder]] bridges give configurable [[microcontroller]] pin routing on a prototyping board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup>

## High-value and high-voltage resistors

High resistance values solve problems that no other component can. A high-voltage differential probe is not isolated; its safety comes from a series resistor chain — two 1 MΩ resistors end to end, then another 1 MΩ, then a further element across the [[isolation-slot|isolation slot]], giving four megohms in each lead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> A multimeter can detect a lead plugged into the amps jack by tapping off a chain of three 3 MΩ resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> Substituting ten SMD resistors in series-parallel for a proper high-voltage [[input-protection|input protection]] resistor is poor practice, since the SMD parts are not rated for the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1734" title="EEVblog 1734 - Uni-T UT892 2000V High Voltage Multimeter TESTED">1734</a></sup> At the extreme, femtoammeter input ranges use resistors of 10 GΩ, 1 GΩ, 100 MΩ, 10 MΩ and 1 MΩ,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> and a condenser microphone capsule may be biased through a 5 GΩ resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup> Very high-value resistors are also relatively expensive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup> An antistatic wrist strap's 1 MΩ safety discharge resistor is normally built into the lead rather than the strap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-768" title="EEVblog #768 - Cordless Anti-Static Wristbands BUSTED!">768</a></sup>

High-value resistors also allow low-power design: a comparator with 25 nA [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] draws effectively nothing from 100 kΩ dividers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Where a time constant is needed, choosing a 10 MΩ resistor and a small capacitor is generally preferable to a low-value resistor and a large capacitor, because resistors are more versatile in a BOM and cheaper than high-value capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup>

## Measurement in circuit

Measuring resistance in circuit is unreliable and is a classic beginner trap: anything in parallel with the resistor corrupts the reading, and a 10 kΩ resistor can read 3.6 kΩ simply because the meter's test current finds another path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Even an unpowered circuit is not safe, because the meter's terminal voltage may turn on semiconductor junctions; some meters offer a low-ohms range that keeps terminal voltage under about 0.6 V for this reason, a feature common on 1980s instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> Two resistors that measure 131 kΩ in circuit against a nominal 539 kΩ each demonstrate the problem rather than a fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> The reading is trustworthy when the surrounding parts are genuinely out of circuit — with the IC removed and the relevant pins floating, a 100 kΩ integration resistor can be measured directly and read spot on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup>

Repeatability of the measurement setup matters too. Probing a nominal 1 kΩ resistor should give the same reading under light pressure at any point along the leads; a variation of six counts between successive readings of the same part indicates a contact problem, not a component problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup> [[contact-resistance|Contact resistance]] is itself measurable — a rotary range switch nominally around 0.9 Ω can climb to about 10 Ω over cycling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Resistors are among the more reliable components, but they do fail. A one megohm carbon film part reading 170 kΩ is remarkable enough to warrant suspecting a missed [[pcb-trace|PCB trace]] before condemning the resistor — and in that case a right-angle trace hidden under the body was the actual explanation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Real failures include corrosion: conductive residue on a board can eat through one lead until the resistor drops out of circuit entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1072" title="EEVblog #1072 - KRK Rokit 6 Studio Monitor Speaker Repair">1072</a></sup> Overheating takes a part open circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Fusible input resistors are fitted deliberately to fail — checking one that measures 10 Ω and is intact rules it out as the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> Interaction failures are subtler: 150 µA of leakage in a [[tantalum-capacitor|tantalum capacitor]] combined with a 1 MΩ resistor is enough to hold a node at mid-rail and break a power-on reset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Older assemblies can fail by losing internal contact altogether, giving no linear response at all across several resistors that should read a few kilohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup>

## Classic problems

Two puzzles recur in electronics education. The infinite resistor problem asks for the resistance measured across one resistor in an infinite two-dimensional grid of identical 1 Ω resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup> The resistor cube asks for the resistance between opposite corners of a cube built from twelve identical resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> The cube yields to symmetry: certain nodes sit at identical potential, so the resistors between them carry no current — measured currents of 197 nA and 51 nA through 4.7 kΩ parts confirm this experimentally, and in an ideal circuit the current is exactly zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> Those resistors can therefore be either shorted or removed, whichever is more convenient, without changing the answer; physically snipping them out makes no difference to the circuit resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> The arrangement is essentially a balanced Wheatstone bridge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> Textbook networks of a few resistors and voltage sources are likewise the standard vehicle for teaching nodal and mesh analysis and Kirchhoff's laws.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>
