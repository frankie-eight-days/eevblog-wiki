The alkaline battery is the dominant disposable primary cell in consumer electronics, built on alkaline manganese dioxide chemistry with a nominal 1.5 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup> Its defining feature for a designer is not its capacity but the shape of its discharge curve: the terminal [[voltage]] slides continuously from around 1.6 V down to 0.8 V, so a product that stops working too early throws away a large fraction of the energy it paid for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Its defining feature for anyone maintaining equipment is that it eventually leaks a caustic [[electrolyte]] that destroys whatever it is sitting in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1274" title="EEVblog #1274 - Long Term Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing">1274</a></sup>

## Chemistry and construction

The electrolyte is potassium hydroxide, and it is the potassium hydroxide that gives the chemistry its name.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-518" title="EEVblog #518 - 9V Alkaline Battery Construction">518</a></sup> The anode material is zinc oxide, soaked in that electrolyte; between anode and [[cathode]] sits a separator, usually a fabric, whose job is to conduct ions across the gap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-518" title="EEVblog #518 - 9V Alkaline Battery Construction">518</a></sup> The manganese dioxide cathode material is a distinctly powdery substance, more so than the zinc oxide paste.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-518" title="EEVblog #518 - 9V Alkaline Battery Construction">518</a></sup>

In a cylindrical cell the outer metal can is the positive terminal, not merely a housing. The only thing separating positive from negative is a small rubber O-ring seal around the base, which means a metal object bridging the can to the negative end will short the cell directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> There is no seal at the positive end at all, which is why leakage is essentially never seen emerging from the positive terminal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-H-yOP-KSMC4" title="4K AA Alkaline Battery Leakage">•</a></sup>

A 9 V alkaline is not a single cell but six 4A-size alkaline cells stacked in series, each stack's bottom plate forming the positive terminal of the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-518" title="EEVblog #518 - 9V Alkaline Battery Construction">518</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2Y1tVvllklc" title="9V Duracell Alkaline Battery Leakage">•</a></sup> More than one internal construction is used for 9 V alkalines, distinguishable by how the pack responds to being physically squeezed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup>

## Terminal voltage and discharge behaviour

A brand-new alkaline cell does not sit at exactly 1.5 V. Open-circuit voltage ranges from the nominal 1.5 V up to about 1.65 V per cell, which for a 9 V [[battery]] means up to about 9.9 V at the terminals; the spread comes from the internal electrochemistry and the purity of the materials used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup> A fresh single cell measured unloaded might read around 1.6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

Open-circuit voltage on its own carries little information about state of charge, because the cell's internal [[resistance]] is inseparable from its electrochemistry — it is not a component that can be factored out of the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Manufacturer discharge curves are therefore given as loaded terminal voltage under a stated constant-[[current]], constant-power or constant-resistance drain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

Alkaline cells share the general shape common to nickel metal hydride and lithium-ion curves — an initial drop, a long sloping middle, then an abrupt collapse at the end — but the alkaline slope is markedly worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Lithium-ion is far flatter, which makes cutoff design easy; the alkaline's non-flat characteristic is what makes wasted capacity a real design problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

Practical end of life is 0.8 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> At 0.8 V roughly 95% of the cell's capacity is already gone, and the residual area under the curve below that point is on the order of 5% even at high discharge currents such as 1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup> Some designers take 0.9 V as the endpoint instead, though 0.8 V is the more common figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup> Below about 1 V lies only the lowest 30% or so of capacity, with the bulk of the energy delivered above 1.2 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

## Capacity

Capacity figures depend on discharge current, and published numbers are not always met. A quality AA alkaline — a Duracell Procell with four years left on its stamped shelf life, straight out of the box — delivered just over 2 Wh at 250 mA continuous discharge, falling short of a specified 9 hours to 0.8 V by more than an hour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> At the low currents typical of instrumentation, a AAA alkaline runs to roughly 1.8 Wh, sustaining 1 mA for around 1,400 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup> A high-end AA is worth about 4 Wh per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1092" title="EEVblog #1092 - Wi-Charge IR Wireless Charging - Fact or Fiction?">1092</a></sup> Nominal AA capacity quoted for battery-life estimation is around 2,800 mAh, though no product uses all of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

Temperature matters more than the room-temperature curves suggest. Taken from 21 °C down to 0 °C, alkaline capacity more than halves, the cause being the sharp rise in the cell's internal series resistance as temperature falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Lithium primary cells such as the Energizer L91 outperform alkalines at room temperature across all discharge currents, and the gap widens dramatically in the cold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup>

## Designing to the curve

A well-designed product has as low a cutout voltage as possible; a superbly designed one runs all the way to 0.8 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Setting the cutout at 1.2 V instead wastes half the battery's capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> The penalty applies across essentially the whole practical current range, from 5 mA to 1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> By contrast, any product designed around rechargeable cells must already tolerate 1.1 V or less, at which point the wasted capacity is negligible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

Single-cell operation is the demanding case. A [[boost-converter|boost converter]] fed from one AA or AAA must accept an input from 0.8 V to 1.5 V, which is what forces the choice of part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Manufacturer [[parametric-search|parametric search]] tools make this explicit — Texas Instruments offers a single-cell alkaline/NiMH input checkbox that narrows ninety step-up regulators to a workable shortlist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Substituting a lithium AA for the alkaline improves converter performance further, because the input voltage stays higher for longer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

The same threshold drives component selection elsewhere. Ultra-low-power microcontrollers operating from 0.9 V to 5.5 V are well matched to single-cell alkaline use, because the usable energy in the cell is essentially exhausted by the time it reaches 0.9 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup> JFETs in electret microphone circuits that hold their gain down to 0.8 V, rather than rolling off at 1.1 to 1.2 V, can practically run from a single alkaline cell until it is dead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-611" title="EEVblog #611 - Electret Microphone Design">611</a></sup> Well-regulated [[led|LED]] torches are judged on the same basis: constant brightness maintained down to about 1 V per cell, with the alkaline cutout at roughly 0.8 V, is the desired behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-78" title="EEVblog #78 - Fenix LD01 LED Keychain Flashlight And Victorinox Minichamp Review">78</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup>

Bench equipment is specified against the same number. A lab [[power-supply|power supply]] that regulates down to 0.8 or 0.9 V is useful precisely because a single D or AA alkaline is effectively dead over that range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> An [[electronic-load|electronic load]] intended for battery discharge curves must operate down to 0.8 V for alkaline work, and lower still for low-power testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup>

Battery-life estimation for a product follows directly from average current and the [[datasheet]] curve. A meter drawing 1.3 mA normally and 20 mA with the [[backlight]] on comfortably meets a 400-hour claim on a roughly 800 mAh alkaline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup> A meter drawing 1.5 mA reaches about 800 hours on a 1,200 mAh lithium 9 V and a few hundred hours less on an alkaline 9 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup> A meter with an always-on display drawing nearly 17 mA yields only about 165 hours best case and realistically closer to 100.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

Not every product is suited to alkalines. Some cameras explicitly recommend nickel metal hydride and warn that alkalines may degrade performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-495" title="EEVblog #495 - World&#x27;s First DSLR Camera - Kodak DCS315 Teardown">495</a></sup> Alkalines nonetheless have a decisive logistical advantage for a shipped product: they can be shipped without the restrictions that apply to lithium cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

## The last few percent

Recovering the residual energy below the normal cutout is a well-understood engineering trade-off, not a novel capability — extracting an extra 5% to 10% is something a designer can already build into a product, and there is no intellectual property in it to commercialise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ixbLmBWUqcQ" title="Popular Mechanics Promotes Batteriser 2.0!">•</a></sup> Marketing claims built on the idea have not withstood examination. The premise that new batteries contain "1.5 volts of energy" confuses voltage with energy, and the accompanying claim that many devices stop functioning around the 1.3 volt mark did not hold up against the devices available to test it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> The capacity that such a sleeve is attempting to reach is the same 5% that lies below 0.8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup>

## Squeezing and dropping

Mechanically deforming a cell measurably changes its behaviour. A squeezed AAA alkaline yielded 261 mAh against 140 mAh for an unsqueezed control from the same pair; both began at the same 1.35 V recovery voltage, but the unsqueezed cell's voltage plummeted immediately while the squeezed one held a flatter response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup> Squeezing a 9 V pack likewise raises its terminal voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup>

The related drop test — determining state of charge by bouncing a cell on end, with a fully charged cell bouncing less than an empty one — is a real phenomenon rooted in the cell's electrochemistry, and works more reliably with AA cells than AAA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-508" title="EEVblog #508 - Can You Test Battery Charge By Dropping It?">508</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup>

## Leakage

Leakage is specific to alkaline chemistry; lithium primaries do not do it, and it happens across brands, engineered leak-proof claims notwithstanding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1350" title="EEVblog #1350 - Fake Energizer Batteries?">1350</a></sup> It is a consequence of the vent design rather than a straightforward defect: alkaline cells are deliberately built to vent, because hydrogen pressure builds up inside from the internal electrochemistry and must be released.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1350" title="EEVblog #1350 - Fake Energizer Batteries?">1350</a></sup> Because there is no seal at the positive end, the electrolyte escapes at the negative seal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-H-yOP-KSMC4" title="4K AA Alkaline Battery Leakage">•</a></sup> Once out, the potassium hydroxide reacts with atmospheric carbon dioxide and forms the familiar white dendrite-like crystalline growths — potassium carbonate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1687" title="EEVblog 1687 - A Pointless Alkaline Battery LEAKAGE Repair">1687</a></sup>

The damage is severe and progressive. Leaked electrolyte creeps along and under a [[pcb|PCB]], gets onto the top side, wicks under lead-mounted parts and heatsinks, and eats through [[solder-mask|solder mask]] down to bare copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p8T4Dtc6OKk" title="The CRUSTIEST bit of electronics EVER!">•</a></sup> [[resistor|Resistor]] markings dissolve until the bodies are transparent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p8T4Dtc6OKk" title="The CRUSTIEST bit of electronics EVER!">•</a></sup> It corrodes battery terminals to the point where a device with visually acceptable contacts fails in a subtle way — for example a voltage detector that gives a single beep at power-on instead of the double beep and LED flashes it should produce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VZuebPVrzI8" title="Fluke 1AC Voltage Detection Stick Repair">•</a></sup> Cleanup involves neutralising the residue, for which white vinegar is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--KSPOPz4VXk" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage AGAIN">•</a></sup>

The resulting bench rule is unambiguous: never leave discharged alkaline cells in equipment long term, and be especially careful about alkalines stored in gear that is used only occasionally, such as a [[multimeter]] kept in a drawer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Carbon zinc cells and rechargeables are preferred for exactly this reason — "None of that alkaline rubbish" is the operative habit when restocking a device that will sit unused.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-18OhbyQslF0" title="2 Minute Dumpster Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1571" title="EEVblog 1571 - Nobel Prize Winning Mailbag. 121GW, Retro calcs, Assembly, and Discrete Logic">1571</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1670" title="EEVblog 1670 - BM786 Multimeter BATTERY LEAKAGE REPAIR + GIVEAWAY">1670</a></sup>

## Controlled leakage testing

Establishing which conditions actually provoke leakage has proven difficult, and no manufacturer documentation or research identifying a discharge regime that makes leakage more likely could be found.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1296" title="EEVblog #1296 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1296</a></sup> The experimental approach taken was to obtain many different brands of standard alkaline cell, drain them under controlled conditions, and observe over months which ones leaked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1274" title="EEVblog #1274 - Long Term Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing">1274</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j_eaXfmRB8Q" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing - Part 1">•</a></sup> An initial run used a resistive load; some cells were left half-drained and others fully drained, in two stacks, then stored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j_eaXfmRB8Q" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing - Part 1">•</a></sup> After a period that included the 2019 flood and long storage on a window sill, none of them had leaked — a negative result, but a result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1274" title="EEVblog #1274 - Long Term Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing">1274</a></sup>

A second attempt changed the method. Resistive discharge was abandoned because the dissipation required 1 W resistors in quantities not available, so thirteen sets of two cells each were wired in series and discharged at a constant 100 mA for 24 hours, or 86,400 seconds, chosen from the Duracell datasheet to land at roughly a 1 V end voltage without needing datasheets for the obscure brands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RFb3TwWzza0" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Test Setup">•</a></sup> Series-string discharge of many dissimilar cells has its own failure mode: after 2,283 mAh had been extracted the entire string collapsed to near zero volts while 50 mA was still flowing, with individual cells reading around 0.46 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hSkaZEgrZkY" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 3">•</a></sup>

Leakage was eventually obtained.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1508" title="EEVblog 1508 - We FINALLY Got Alkaline Battery LEAKAGE!">1508</a></sup> The brand pattern did not match the folklore. Not one of the Duracells in the test failed, and Fujitsu, Eclipse, Panasonic, Coles supermarket-brand, generic eBay cells, Philips and Maxell all came through intact — despite Coles cells being known to leak in ordinary use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1508" title="EEVblog 1508 - We FINALLY Got Alkaline Battery LEAKAGE!">1508</a></sup> The conclusion is that the discharge and storage conditions, not brand, dominate the outcome.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1508" title="EEVblog 1508 - We FINALLY Got Alkaline Battery LEAKAGE!">1508</a></sup> Individual failures observed in the field span every major name, including both Duracell and Energizer, and 9 V alkalines as well as cylindrical cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1350" title="EEVblog #1350 - Fake Energizer Batteries?">1350</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke.
