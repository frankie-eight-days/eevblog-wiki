A ball grid array (BGA) is an integrated circuit package whose connections are [[solder]] balls arranged in a grid on the underside of the device rather than leads around its perimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> The arrangement exists because pin counts outgrew perimeter packages: putting contacts across the whole footprint is the only way to get hundreds — or more than a thousand — connections onto a part of reasonable size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> Everything else about the package follows from that one decision, and almost all of it is a cost: finer [[pcb-design|PCB design]] rules, more layers, mandatory reflow, joints that cannot be seen or probed, and rework that needs dedicated equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup>

## Pin counts and typical parts

BGAs cluster at the high-density end of the parts catalogue. Concrete examples span a 20-pin device measuring five balls by four in an image sensor,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> a 36-pin [[fpga|FPGA]] on a 0.4 mm pitch,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> a 404-pin, 15 × 15 mm camera [[processor]] built on a 45 nm process and rated to 70 °C,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-399" title="EEVblog #399 - GoPro Hero 2 Teardown">399</a></sup> a 672-pin FPGA with 500 I/O pins,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> a 672-pin NEC gate array running at 3.3 V,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-591" title="EEVblog #591 - Agilent 54622D Retro Mixed Signal Osciloscope Review &amp; Teardown">591</a></sup> a 676-pin device,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> a 700-odd-pin Altera Cyclone III,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup> and boards carrying parts described as thousand-pin class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1215" title="EEVblog #1215 - $18,000 4K Monitor Teardown From 2001!">1215</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup> Package designators frequently encode the count directly — the 672 in a Lattice part number is the pin count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> An IBM processor module of an earlier era carried 648 pins on a molybdenum-powder ceramic substrate, a construction predating the term itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup>

FPGAs are the archetypal case. Low-pin-count FPGAs exist but are hard to find and rarely come in friendly packages; because the devices are optimised for high I/O, most arrive as BGAs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-635" title="EEVblog #635 - FPGA&#x27;s Vs Microcontrollers">635</a></sup> The same pressure reaches [[microcontroller]] selection, where a part meeting a peripheral list may only be offered in a BGA that demands an assembler capable of that pitch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-687" title="EEVblog #687 - EFTPOS PIN Pad Terminal Teardown">687</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup>

## Routing and layer count

[[pin-pitch|Pin pitch]] on the package sets the design rules for the whole board. A 0.4 mm pitch part forces the fabricator down to at least 4 thou track and space, plus very small drills if individual vias are to be dropped through to other layers — even if the rest of the board carries through-hole parts that would tolerate 20 thou rules.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> Fanning out a BGA is a direct trade-off between routing density and minimum trace width, in a way a quad flat pack with all its pins on the outside never is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> A 0.5 mm pitch mobile-phone processor is documented by its manufacturer as requiring a minimum of six layers simply to escape the pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-514" title="EEVblog #514 - $5 Mobile Phone Teardown - LG800G">514</a></sup> Boards built around dense BGAs land at six, eight, or ten layers,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-444" title="EEVblog #444 - Car Lane Guidance Camera Teardown">444</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-985" title="EEVblog #985 - Siglent SDS1202X-E Oscilloscope Teardown">985</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1043" title="EEVblog #1043 - Mailbag">1043</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> and the high layer count is often driven by one part while the rest of the board would have been happy with four.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-985" title="EEVblog #985 - Siglent SDS1202X-E Oscilloscope Teardown">985</a></sup>

Where escape routing runs out of room, [[via]]-in-pad filled with resin becomes the answer, and blind and buried vias appear on the densest work — a roughly 1,150-pin package without them requires every pad to be broken out through the full stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Dropping that forest of vias through the board also fragments internal power planes, which complicates the plane design in turn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Design rule sets carry BGA-specific entries — minimum BGA pad dimensions and minimum pad-to-pad distances such as 0.127 mm — which can conflict with the general clearance rules a designer has already set, and the binding constraint may turn out to be [[solder-mask|solder mask]] sliver width rather than copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup> For a critical part like a BGA, [[solder-mask-expansion|solder mask expansion]] is normally set as a local rule on that component rather than globally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> CAD packages with a pin-count limit are consumed by a single such part: a 300-pin BGA exhausts a 300-pin quota on its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup>

## Bypassing

Because the power and [[ground]] pins sit under the body, decoupling capacitors have to sit under it too. Placing them around the outside leaves large [[current]] loops that undermine the capacitors' effectiveness, so on a BGA FPGA the caps go directly on the bottom side, opposite the pins they serve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> A [[capacitor]] moved to the bottom layer sits right next to the via that reaches it, giving a low-[[inductance]] path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> This is why dense BGA boards are double-sided loads: components on the second side add a flip, glue, and extra process steps that manufacturers otherwise avoid, and high-density BGA decoupling is one of the few reasons to accept the penalty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ag-MjKAfATw" title="Rode AI-1 USB Audio Interface Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup>

Where cost pushes a design to a single-sided load, the bypassing must be compromised out to the perimeter, with correspondingly long and inductive connections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup> A four-layer board may leave no room to fan out and bypass properly, forcing the caps outward as a trade-off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> Power pins under a BGA commonly fall into a recognisable star arrangement, and the resulting bypass cap pattern on the underside is a reliable visual signature of an FPGA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> More generally, a dense field of vias with a ring of bypass caps is how a BGA is identified from the far side of a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-444" title="EEVblog #444 - Car Lane Guidance Camera Teardown">444</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup> The absence of that signature is equally informative: no telltale bypass caps means no large BGA underneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-925" title="EEVblog #925 - Panasonic CF-U1 ToughBook Teardown">925</a></sup>

## Board finish, flatness, and warp

Surface finish matters more for BGAs than for anything else on the board. Gold plating can be made extremely flat; hot air levelled tin is lumpier and leaves the chip sitting less flush, so for a high pin count BGA a gold finish is vital rather than cosmetic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> A single pad carrying excess solder is enough to spoil the result once balls are involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> The extra cost is small enough that gold is worth specifying even on simple boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup>

Copper balance and thickness also bite. Leaving heavy copper on one side of a large board and little on the other lets it warp, and a BGA — particularly a large ceramic one — can crack, where an SO package's gull-wing leads provide enough strain relief to absorb the movement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> Two-ounce copper on all layers retains a great deal of heat through the reflow oven, making it much harder to get the vapour phase profile right so that the balls actually reflow, and boards can emerge still very hot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1215" title="EEVblog #1215 - $18,000 4K Monitor Teardown From 2001!">1215</a></sup> Etching 4 oz copper to the fine tolerances a BGA region needs is difficult to impossible, which is a problem on boards that mix high-power sections with dense digital.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> Unusually thick boards behave differently again: one build with a large BGA of several hundred pins, many 0402 parts, and extensive ground planes suffered widespread tombstoning and unsoldered joints because the thermal profile could not be dialled in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup>

## Assembly and inspection

A BGA cannot be hand soldered; it is one of the few genuinely compelling reasons to set up stencil, paste, and reflow rather than reaching for an iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> Placement accuracy tightens too: fine pitch, high pin count devices get local fiducials placed at opposite corners of the package so the machine can correct for local error, which is unnecessary on boards with no high-density parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup> Solder mask alignment on very dense BGA work is critical enough that a test coupon becomes essential on professional boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup>

Once assembled, the joints are hidden. Bed-of-nails testing cannot reach contacts underneath a package, which is precisely the problem JTAG boundary scan was created to solve when packages began moving to BGA in the late 1980s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup> Inspection falls to X-ray, which most competent assembly houses have: the operator fades the image in and out looking for shorts between balls and for colour discrepancies that suggest a dry joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> X-ray is not infallible — a 676-pin device that passed X-ray inspection was found to have a dry joint only when physical pressure was applied to the package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> The consequence generalises: any reflowed connection you cannot see requires X-ray to verify.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup> The occasional escape hatch is a part fanned out to individual vias on the underside with no solder mask over them, which allows a sharp [[multimeter]] probe to buzz out continuity on every pin of a 400- to 500-pin device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup>

For device-level testing without [[soldering]], zero insertion force sockets exist: a Torlon or PTFE-bodied fixture screwed into the board, with individually sprung pogo pins in a crown arrangement that press evenly on each ball without damaging it, the dielectric chosen for the [[frequency]] range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-532" title="EEVblog #532 - Silicon Chip Wafer Fab Mailbag">532</a></sup> These are built for one specific footprint and pin count, and are correspondingly expensive — around 300 dollars in volume for a common type, and over 1,000 dollars for a high-frequency version good to 10 GHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-532" title="EEVblog #532 - Silicon Chip Wafer Fab Mailbag">532</a></sup>

## Failure modes and rework

Large BGAs heat and cool with every power cycle, and the resulting thermal fatigue produces cracked and dry solder joints — the standard suspect behind intermittent faults in televisions and other consumer equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1154" title="EEVblog #1154 - Surprising 4K Dumpster TV Fault">1154</a></sup> The conventional response is to reflow the package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1154" title="EEVblog #1154 - Surprising 4K Dumpster TV Fault">1154</a></sup> It is not reliable: reflowing suspect memory devices on a T-con board changed nothing, leaving an oven cycle as the next escalation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup> Diagnosis is also awkward, because freezer spray on a suspect BGA does not always provoke a dry joint into showing itself, and a chip that stays working while frosted over has not been cleared.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-447" title="EEVblog #447 - Samsung Plasma Followup">447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> It is also worth confirming which parts are actually BGAs before planning a reflow, since in some displays the horizontal driver chips are BGA packages while the vertical ones are not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup>

Removal requires hot air. A conventional iron cannot lift a BGA at all, whereas a hot air station takes one off in seconds, after which the residual solder is wicked from the pads before a new device goes down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup> [[thermal-capacity|Thermal capacity]] is the limiting specification — a large GPU with many balls needs a station that can deliver it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1659" title="EEVblog 1659 - Quick 861 Pro Hot Air Station REVIEW">1659</a></sup> Boards with large [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] are preheated from below, typically to around 200 °C, before hot air is applied from above.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OZb-mB3X-30" title="Abandon Lab! - IBM TCM Part 2">•</a></sup> Even with the right tools the process damages boards: pads and balls come away during removal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup> Reballing a removed device is possible but is a specialised craft with its own machines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup> Mechanical removal is not an option — the sheer number of balls under a main chip resists the force that will shear a chip capacitor off the same board, and attempting to lever a BGA free shears the pads instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2G7Z9IIQoIs" title="IBM BGA Thermal Futility...">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup>

## Design judgement

Where a design has any choice in the matter, avoiding BGA is the recommended course: yields are lower, inspection is harder, pad dimensions are more critical, and the assembly cost and complexity rise sharply, so a quad flat pack at 0.5 mm pitch or coarser is preferred where the part exists.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> The position is held consistently and stated bluntly — a usable package, meaning DIP, SO, or reasonable-pitch QFP, is treated as a basic obligation on semiconductor vendors, with BGA and chip scale packages acceptable only for high-volume work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-123" title="EEVblog #123 - Top 5 Tips for Semiconductor Manufacturers">123</a></sup> Package availability is enough on its own to eliminate an otherwise suitable part during selection,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> and a design that already carries one BGA has paid the assembly penalty, which changes the calculus for every other part on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

The counter-pressure is that packages are simply getting smaller and finer, so density increases whether or not a designer wants it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 2 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Test equipment that once supported repair down to component level is now impractical to service without exotic equipment, precisely because ball grid arrays and leads-underneath packages such as QFN and DFN replaced the parts a technician could reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 2 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Instruments built around a BGA processor still concentrate the pain in one region: the front-end board stays simple while the digital board goes to a much higher layer count for the BGA packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-985" title="EEVblog #985 - Siglent SDS1202X-E Oscilloscope Teardown">985</a></sup>

## Related packages

A wafer level chip scale package is not a BGA despite also having balls underneath. It is a bare semiconductor die flipped over with the balls placed directly on it, and being unencapsulated it is photosensitive — a genuine BGA, plastic or ceramic encapsulated, blocks light from reaching the die, which is why substituting one for a CSP resolves light-sensitivity faults.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-716" title="EEVblog #716 - Raspberry Pi 2 Xenon Flash Problem Explained">716</a></sup>

The pin grid array is the older through-hole equivalent, with pins rather than balls on the underside; it appears in retro computing and legacy instrument hardware and has the practical advantage that its joints are conventional ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-746" title="EEVblog #746 - Sharp X68000 Retro Computer Teardown">746</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup> Land grid arrays and flip chips with reflowed bumps occupy adjacent territory and are easily mistaken for BGAs on inspection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-612" title="EEVblog #612 - Mailbag">612</a></sup>
