A via is a plated hole through a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] that carries a net from one copper layer to another. Structurally it is nothing special: on a bare board a via and a component pad are constructed in exactly the same way, and there is no practical electrical difference between them — the two are simply used for different purposes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> The plating is applied during manufacture by passing a large [[current]] through the panel in an electrolysis bath, coating the inside of every drilled hole with copper so that it connects the top and bottom layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup>

Vias are the currency of [[pcb-routing|PCB routing]]. Every one of them costs board area, splits a plane, adds [[inductance]], and adds a drill hole to the fabrication file, which is why the number of vias on a layout is treated as a proxy for how well that layout was thought through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup>

## Types and finishes

The designer, not the board house, decides what happens to the top of a via.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> A **tented** via has [[solder-mask|solder mask]] flowed over the hole, which is the default on most prototype boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-566" title="EEVblog #566 - Cheap USB Microscope Reviews">566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> Tenting is not the same as plugging: solder mask over the opening leaves the barrel hollow, whereas plugged vias are a separate process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> **Via-in-pad** places the hole inside a component land and is normally filled with resin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> **Blind and buried** vias connect only a subset of layers rather than passing through the whole stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> [[solder|Solder]]-filled vias are not produced at the PCB fabrication stage at all — the fill happens during assembly, either by [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]] so the wave bubbles up into the untented holes, or by opening the [[solder-paste|solder paste]] mask over them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Vias filled with solder carry considerably more current than open ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Tenting control matters most on fine-pitch parts. On a [[bga|BGA]] fan-out where the via sits a fraction of a millimetre from a ball, solder paste that bridges to an untented via produces a short underneath a package that cannot be inspected, and the fault will not be discovered until power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> Forcing tenting on those vias removes the [[solder-mask-expansion|solder mask expansion]] and eliminates the bridging path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> Some tools make this awkward: KiCad's tenting control is board-wide, with no per-via override, which is a real limitation on designs that need some vias tented and others open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup>

## Current and thermal duty

A single via has finite current capacity. A working rule of thumb is roughly one via for every half amp, and any trace carrying serious current should use a minimum of two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Boards built to carry tens of amps show this directly — a [[multimeter]]'s 10 A path may be stitched with a very large number of vias in parallel to keep the path [[impedance]] down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> Vias are also added purely to increase current capacity, sometimes as a retrofit with extra traces on the bottom layer tied through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup> A high-current, low-inductance feed to a large [[processor]] typically appears as a dense field of vias directly beneath the die.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-491" title="EEVblog #491 - Nintendo 64 Game Console Teardown">491</a></sup>

The same hole conducts heat. A power device with its [[heat-sink|heatsink]] on the opposite side of the board relies on a via array underneath the package to move heat through the laminate — nine vias under a [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] is a classic arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup> The thermal path then has to be accounted for as a chain: junction to case, case to PCB, top side to bottom side through the vias, [[contact-resistance|contact resistance]] to the heatsink, and finally the heatsink itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Hundreds of small vias used to conduct power and heat through to the other side of a board is not the most efficient solution, but it is often good enough within a fixed case size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Exposed copper areas dotted with via arrays under a chip are a reliable signature of a thermal pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-443" title="EEVblog #443 - Mailbag">443</a></sup>

## Effect on planes and power integrity

Vias are not free from a power-delivery standpoint. Every via dropped through a [[ground]] or power plane chops that plane up, so a BGA fan-out that pushes a large number of vias through the stack fragments the very plane that was supposed to be one solid low-impedance block.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> The resulting DC drop is measurable: a rail feeding 8 A across a board can easily lose 40 mV before it reaches the load, with a further couple of millivolts of variation across the plane depending on how many vias are breaking it up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Boards without a solid [[ground-plane|ground plane]] end up with split planes and meandering return paths dictated entirely by wherever vias could be dropped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

Via inductance is the dominant term in high-[[frequency]] bypassing, and [[fpga|FPGA]] power-delivery application notes treat it as such alongside plane inductance and [[capacitance]] per square.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> The best achievable bypassing puts the capacitors on the underside of the package, directly beneath the pins, with vias running straight up to the balls, so that individual pins are bypassed rather than the rail as a whole.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Where capacitors sit on the top side, they are via-stitched straight down to the ground and power layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Vias placed for bypass and ground should be committed early — putting them in as part of placing the capacitors reveals exactly how much room is left to route signals out of the area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> A layout that leaves the ground side unstitched while the signal routing proceeds has the order backwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

Stitching also serves RF containment: vias run around the perimeter of a section keep return current local and hold RF impedance down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1315" title="EEVblog #1315 - Ultrasound Probe Extreme Teardown!">1315</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1416" title="EEVblog 1416 - Random TEARDOWN: Sennheiser EW100 True Diversity Receiver">1416</a></sup> Conversely, the deliberate absence of vias along an RF path is itself a design decision, visible on high-frequency interconnects where a signal jumps through a series termination without dropping layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup>

## Routing practice

The governing goal for a surface-mount design is to route as much as possible on the top layer and drop to another layer only when forced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> The target is a single-sided layout every time — the holy grail — with vias treated as an admission that the placement did not quite work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> On a typical review board, several dozen vias might be reduced to three or four once the power traces are moved to the bottom layer and brought up only where needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> The related discipline is directionality: routing traces horizontally on one layer and vertically on the other keeps the board tractable, whereas letting traces run in every direction fills the space and forces a large number of vias to recover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup>

Dropping vias immediately underneath surface-mount parts to reach the inner layers destroys routing density, which is why the traditional four-layer stackup keeps signals on the outer layers and ground and power in the middle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> Jumps to the opposite layer should be kept as short as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Component and pin choices feed back into via count — flipping parts so a bus runs along the correct side avoids a column of vias, and on programmable devices pin swapping can be used specifically to minimise the number of vias on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> A single well-placed via can also be additive rather than costly: taking a trace to the opposite side for a short run frees a clear routing corridor that was previously blocked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Where a copper pour ends up broken into isolated islands, extra vias and jumpers are used to tie the regions back together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup>

Autorouters handle vias poorly. A router will get caught on an existing via and abandon a solution a human would reach by nudging the via a short distance, and rip-up-and-retry algorithms will place a via, route past a constraint, then tear the whole thing out again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup> A board with only a handful of vias in a dense layout is strong evidence of hand routing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup>

## Manufacturing constraints

Via size drives cost and process class. Fanning out a fine-pitch BGA on individual layers forces drill sizes down to around 0.1–0.2 mm, which pushes the board into a much more expensive process class alongside 4 thou trace and space rules.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> More ordinary designs use 0.6 mm plated holes for vias, and because fabricators charge per tool change, drill sizes should be consolidated rather than inherited piecemeal from mixed component libraries.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup> Annular ring is the other constraint: drill wander and drill tolerance mean a hole can skid off centre, so minimum annular rings are specified on both vias and pads, and a via sitting only a fraction of a millimetre from a track risks breaking it if the drill wanders.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup> Where a fabricator's rules forbid routing between pads, the board may simply be rejected unless a via is added to drop the trace out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup>

Fabrication houses occasionally alter a design in ways that break it. Expanding the clearance around pads and vias beyond what the submitted Gerbers specified can sever a ground plane, leaving dead copper and an input ground with no electrical connection to the rest of the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup> Vias also appear in panelisation: several small holes drilled along a mouse bite let a board be broken out of its panel, though cutting through them with side cutters risks shorting adjacent tracks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Plated edge vias are used for mechanical reinforcement of a board edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1336" title="EEVblog #1336 - DT71 LCR Tweezer Destructive Teardown + Lab Update">1336</a></sup> Inspection of a finished board focuses on hole-to-annular-ring registration and the consistency of the barrel plating, both of which can be assessed optically, though a definitive answer requires cross-sectioning.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-817" title="EEVblog #817 - Mantis Elite Cam Inspection Microscope">817</a></sup>

At the extreme end, a ceramic multi-chip module from the early 1990s used 100-micron vias in tens of thousands, and a comparable module packed over two million vias into 63 layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1358" title="EEVblog #1358 - $250,000 IBM Processor X-RAYED!">1358</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Vias fail in service. Alkaline [[electrolyte]] that reaches a board wicks into the barrels and eats them out from the inside; affected vias turn visibly black and the copper is consumed, and the leakage path a via provides is often how electrolyte gets underneath a chip in the first place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1687" title="EEVblog 1687 - A Pointless Alkaline Battery LEAKAGE Repair">1687</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VZuebPVrzI8" title="Fluke 1AC Voltage Detection Stick Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1497" title="EEVblog 1497 - RIP Fluke. Thanks Energizer. NOT.">1497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qmcSEJehqZM" title="Energizer Battery Leak Fluke 3000 Multimeter &quot;Repair&quot;">•</a></sup> Old wave-soldered assemblies present a different problem: where the solder never flowed through the hole, the joint depends entirely on the through-hole plating, and after several decades of thermal cycling on a board with nearly all its copper on one side, that is an intermittent waiting to happen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1572" title="EEVblog 1572 - Haefely 16kV ESD Tester TEARDOWN + Repair Part 1">1572</a></sup> Marginal vias are also a candidate for mechanical intermittents — board flex acting on a via with very little annular ring is a known, if obscure, defect mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup> A short inside a via or pad on an eight- or ten-layer board is notoriously difficult to locate without a current-tracing probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

## Vias in repair and reverse engineering

Vias are the main obstacle to tracing a [[multilayer-pcb|multilayer board]] optically, because a trace that drops into an inner layer disappears from both the top and bottom plots and has to be recovered with a fast continuity tester.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> They are also the main aid: printing each layer onto overhead transparency and using the vias as alignment markers lets the sheets be overlaid so signals can be followed from one side to the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> Where a via must be probed, the annular ring is scraped clear first; tented vias cannot be probed at all without breaking the mask.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1428" title="EEVblog 1428 - Sony RX100 IV Camera Repair and then WTF!">1428</a></sup>

Vias give mod wires somewhere to go. Large pads and large vias make it straightforward to run a wire from a trace on one side of the board to the other, which is a common requirement when modifying a board, and a wire fed down a via rather than tacked to its surface has far more mechanical strength.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1215" title="EEVblog #1215 - $18,000 4K Monitor Teardown From 2001!">1215</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Scope Bandwidth Hack Investigation">879</a></sup> On multilayer boards with blind and buried vias, that option disappears.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1215" title="EEVblog #1215 - $18,000 4K Monitor Teardown From 2001!">1215</a></sup> Vias on membrane-keypad boards are usually covered in conductive carbon ink, which also coats the inside of the hole — [[soldering]] into such a via is a mistake, and the correct approach is to gently scrape the top clear and lay the wire flat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-962" title="EEVblog #962 - Hacking A Calculator Into A Counter">962</a></sup> Single-sided boards may carry via-like holes that do not actually connect to the other side, ruling out a back-side [[solder-joint|solder joint]] entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-505" title="EEVblog #505 - IR Learning Remote Control Hack">505</a></sup> For repairing broken traces or filling vias on home-made boards, conductive ink pens are effective.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup>
