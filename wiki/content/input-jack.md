An input jack is the front-panel banana socket through which a [[multimeter]], [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] or other bench instrument receives its test leads. A typical handheld multimeter carries four of them — [[ground]], 10 amp, milliamp/microamp, and volts/ohms/diode — each fed by a completely separate [[input-protection|input protection]] circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Because the jack is the first metal in the signal path and the last mechanical part a user touches, its construction is one of the most reliable single indicators of whether an instrument was engineered or merely assembled: cheap meters give themselves away at the jacks before anything on the board is visible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup>

## Solid tube versus split jack

The central distinction is between a solid metal tube and a split jack. A solid tube is a full cylindrical barrel that grips the probe evenly around its circumference; a split jack has a slot cut down one side so the two halves act as a spring contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Solid tubes are the preferred construction, and the split type is treated as a defect to be tolerated rather than a design choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup> On analog classics such as the Triplett 630-NA the split jacks — press-fitted into the plastic surround rather than mechanically fastened — are the one part of the instrument that fails to impress.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup> A meter whose jacks are all split reads as a slapped-together design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup>

The worst grade is stamped rather than tubular. In cheap meters the jacks are a bit of stamped metal that does not fit the plastic surround properly and lifts straight out of the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Stamped and folded jacks are simply expected at the bottom of the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> Even where stamped construction is acceptable, the jacks can sit visibly off-centre in their shrouds, relying on the shroud of the probe to self-centre them on insertion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup>

Good construction is unambiguous in appearance: solid input tubes folded over and screwed down on top with a star washer and a self-locking nut,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> or solid metal posts with shake-proof washers screwed directly into the [[pcb|PCB]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup> A generational upgrade from split stamped jacks to solid screw-in jacks is a substantial improvement in a meter's front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup>

## Mechanical retention and board attachment

How the jack reaches the PCB matters as much as the barrel itself. Several distinct approaches appear across instruments:

- Solid metal posts screwed directly into the main PCB, with the [[fuse]] board on a vertical riser soldered to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup>
- A single solid piece with an integrated lead fed through a slot in the board and hand-soldered, the slot itself providing the rigid mechanical constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup>
- Folded metal passed through the board, bent back up on the underside, and soldered at both points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup>
- A dedicated secondary board carrying the jacks, connected to the main board by headers or by brass standoffs running top to bottom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup>
- Solid metal threaded inserts moulded into the case body.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup>

Where jacks are held only by a shake-proof washer, the assembly can work loose in service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> Jacks pressed into place with high-quality contact surfaces can also be effectively unserviceable — in some precision instruments they are not realistically replaceable and are best left undisturbed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup>

## Split jacks and the input alert

Splitting a jack is not always a cost measure. Split contacts are two-sided by design, and the two halves are used to detect whether a probe is actually inserted — the basis of the input alert feature that warns when a lead has been plugged into the wrong terminal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Detection is only needed on the [[current]] terminals, so the volts jack halves are joined together while the amps jacks are kept separate and tapped off to a sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup>

The mechanical cost is real. Because the split halves must flex, the current jacks are noticeably softer than the volts and ground terminals, which are full metal tubes; probes sit almost loose and jiggly in them, and the plastic inserts in the amps jacks can come out entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> The alternative is optical detection: a sensor shines through a clear housing on the terminal jack, so a fully sealed solid jack can still support input alert.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup>

## Sealing and water ingress

Waterproofing is achieved by moulding the jacks fully into the front panel so no water enters through them at all,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> or by fitting an O-ring seal around each jack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup> The absence of O-ring or rubber sealing around the jacks, alongside a smaller [[current-shunt|current shunt]] and a lighter fuse, is a recognisable cost saving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup> Where the whole jack assembly is embedded in one large plastic moulding, the instrument is waterproof through the terminals and the opto sensing has to pass through the clear housing instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup>

Water that does reach the jacks produces a characteristic symptom rather than a failure: the meter reads amps error, or beeps continuously, because the wetted contacts make it think a probe is plugged in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-868" title="EEVblog #868 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Torture Test">868</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-66" title="EEVblog #66 - Death &amp; Destruction of a Fluke Multimeter">66</a></sup> The condition can persist for hours after immersion and survive both blowing out the jacks and banging the meter on the bench, while the [[measurement-accuracy|measurement accuracy]] itself stays bang on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-868" title="EEVblog #868 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Torture Test">868</a></sup>

## Circuitry immediately behind the jack

The jack is the entry point to the protection network. On a volts input the jack feeds a string of five series resistors, used in series specifically to obtain a high withstanding [[voltage]] more cheaply than a single high-voltage part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Other front ends run the jack into a 1 meg high-voltage ceramic [[resistor]] and then into a bank of MOVs, with a tap off to the [[range-switch|range switch]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> or through a fusible resistor, a high-voltage resistor, a PTC and three MOVs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup> In a low-[[impedance]] mode the positive input jack is routed through a 1k resistor into a physically large PTC, chosen for its greater heat dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> On the amps side the jack feeds a [[current-shunt-resistor|current shunt resistor]] and its fuse, an entirely separate path from the volts circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>

[[thermocouple|Thermocouple]] front ends impose a placement constraint on the jack itself: cold junction compensation needs the [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]] to see the temperature of the dissimilar metals at the terminals, so the layout note reads "place near the inputs".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Better implementations physically couple the sensor chip through to the metal of the input jacks; cheaper ones merely place it physically close, which is still preferable to siting it halfway up the meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

## Ratings, arrangement and count

The CAT rating marked at the jacks is not always the rating the instrument holds — jacks printed with CAT 1000 volts on a meter that is in fact CAT II 600 volt rated are a known form of overstatement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup> Terminal count and arrangement vary with function: four-wire [[resistance-measurement|resistance measurement]] adds two sense terminals alongside the basic volts/ohms/diode pair,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> and bench instruments provide both front and rear terminal sets, front for traditional bench use and rear for system measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> Some bench multimeters carry both a 3 amp and a 10 amp input jack, the 3 amp retained for backward compatibility with an earlier model while the 10 amp is the recommended terminal for better accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup>

## Failure modes

The input jack is a common point of failure, and its failures are intermittent rather than absolute. A dodgy jack may not open-circuit outright: it can present a high but finite impedance that forms a [[voltage-divider|voltage divider]] against the meter's own nominal 10 or 11 megohm [[input-impedance|input impedance]], producing a reading that is wrong rather than absent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> The usual cause is tarnishing or oxidisation on the contacts combined with looseness; tightening the jacks lets the probe pierce through the oxide layer and restores the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup>

Mechanical abuse produces the same signature. After a meter was run over by a car, the positive socket required the probe to be physically depressed before it would measure, and wiggling the probe made the reading jump around — with the [[solder-joint|solder joint]], lock nut and star washer all sound, the fault had to be internal to the jack, most likely dirt and grit driven inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-151" title="EEVblog #151 - Extech Multimeter Canyon Trip">151</a></sup> An unreliable input jack is a functional failure of the instrument even when everything downstream is within specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-151" title="EEVblog #151 - Extech Multimeter Canyon Trip">151</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> Consequently, wiggling the leads at the jacks is a standard first check when a meter misbehaves, and ruling the jacks out redirects the diagnosis elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-FgkD8K0Ssdc" title="Fluke 287 Repair - My Arse">•</a></sup>

Jacks also wear out with ordinary use, gradually losing grip on the probe over the life of an otherwise excellent instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>

## As a design and layout concern

Crowding around the jacks is a layout defect in its own right: a resistor touching an input jack, or input components bunched and pushed over, undermines an otherwise sound front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Well-executed designs route high-voltage isolation slots into the board near the terminals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> On schematics, the inputs belong on the left, and a multimeter [[schematic]] drawing the input jack on the right departs from best practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup>

Retrofitting quality is not a practical option — improving the jacks and safety of a cheap DMM by hand is not worth attempting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup> In original design work the jacks are often not specified at all but inherited from the contract manufacturer's standard part used across multiple designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> The distinction between good and bad jacks remains visible without instrumentation: "Not the crap split type" is a complete assessment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup>
