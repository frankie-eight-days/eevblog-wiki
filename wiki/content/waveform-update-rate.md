Waveform update rate is the number of separate acquisitions a digital [[oscilloscope]] captures and presents per second, quoted in [[waveform|waveforms]] per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> It is not the rate at which the screen is redrawn: a scope claiming a million waveforms per second is not refreshing its [[lcd]] a million times a second, it combines many captures into a single displayed image periodically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> The figure matters because catching an infrequent event is a statistical process — a scope capturing a couple of hundred thousand waveforms per second stands a far better chance of landing on a rare glitch than one capturing a couple of hundred.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup> It is a specification that must be compared alongside [[bandwidth]], [[sample-rate]] and [[memory-depth]] rather than treated as a footnote.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup>

## Why it decides whether a fault is found

A scope displaying a 10 microsecond capture window, looking for glitches that recur every 15 milliseconds, will only see one roughly every 1,500 acquisitions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup> At about 720 to 730 waveform updates per second, that works out to one capture every couple of seconds — a number that can be predicted in advance from the update rate and the window length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup> The same arithmetic run against a one-in-50,000 runt pulse shows why 50,000 waveform updates per second catches it about once a second while a scope an order of magnitude slower may take 30 seconds or more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> Against a one-in-a-million glitch, a 50,000 waveform per second instrument would be expected to need around 20 seconds per capture where a million waveform per second instrument sees it roughly once a second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> For a fault that appears once a day, even 50,000 waveforms per second is not enough.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup>

The consequence is that update rate, not analog performance, is often what separates a scope that shows a problem immediately from one that shows nothing at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> It is also what makes a digital scope superior to an analog one for glitch hunting, since the analog instrument's only equivalent control is turning up the intensity to lengthen persistence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup>

## Measuring it

Almost every scope with a trigger output or pass/fail output can be made to report its own update rate: set the auxiliary output to emit a pulse on each trigger, and measure that output's [[frequency]] on a counter or a second scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> The frequency read directly in hertz is the waveform update rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> The input signal must be at or above the anticipated update rate, so a 1 MHz square or [[sine-wave]] is the standard stimulus.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> The de facto bench reference condition used for cross-brand comparison is a 10 MHz input at 10 nanoseconds per division with 250 points of memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Where the update rate falls below the resolution of this method, an alternative is to count acquisitions over a fixed interval — 43,000 acquisitions in 30 seconds works out to about 1,400 waveforms per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Instruments with no pass/fail or trigger output cannot be measured this way and must be compared indirectly against another scope on the same runt-pulse signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> A few scopes, notably the Rohde & Schwarz MXO, report acquisition speed, average blind time and percentage of time captured on screen directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup>

## What the number depends on

The banner specification is a maximum, usually reachable on only one or two optimised [[time-base]] settings for a given sample rate and memory depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup> A full comparison requires a whole table of update rates, not one figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup>

Memory depth is the dominant variable: the update rate falls as the amount of memory the scope must process rises.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> On one instrument 1,000 points of record length gave 250,000 waveforms per second, 100k points gave about 22 kHz, and 1 meg gave 380 to 390 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> Another dropped from 4 kHz to 20 waveforms per second going to 20 megapoints, at which setting a one-in-a-million pulse remained uncaptured after 20 minutes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> The practical rule is to select the lowest memory depth that still covers the time base of interest when hunting infrequent events, rather than defaulting to maximum memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> The high-update-rate fast-acquisition modes of some instruments enforce this themselves by dropping the record length to 1,000 samples, which trades away any detail on zoom-in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup>

Channel count matters on architectures that share acquisition hardware. Turning on a third channel halved the update rate on one four-channel instrument, indicating one chip serving two channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> On others the rate halves with the second channel, and in one case fell by close to an order of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup>

Processing load is where architectures diverge most sharply. Scopes that do decoding, masking and measurement in the acquisition [[asic]] show no drop at all when those functions are enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> Scopes that do not fall apart: enabling digital channels, [[fft]] or serial decode on one instrument collapsed its update rate entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> A single AC RMS measurement took one high-end scope from 400,000 down to 2.5k waveforms per second while a competitor alongside it stayed at a million.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> Turning on a spectrum analyser function dropped another from its fastest rate to 50k.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> Trigger holdoff also throttles the rate, to roughly the reciprocal of the holdoff value, which makes it a deliberate means of slowing a scope down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup>

## Architecture

High update rates come from mapping acquisition data directly to the display in hardware, bypassing the CPU.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> The Keysight MegaZoom 4 ASIC wrote samples and display information straight to the screen to reach a million waveforms per second, a rate that would previously have cost ten to twenty thousand dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> The ASIC's 4 megasample memory is built in, and the technique of filling one 2 meg block while reading out and displaying the other is what produces the speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> The cost is a fixed display layout: because the ASIC writes to a designated screen area, the menu cannot be collapsed to reclaim waveform space.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup> The same silicon was reused for years across the 1000, 2000, 3000 and 4000 series, with capability deliberately disabled on the lower models.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup> Its successor raised the figure to 1.3 million.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> The Rohde & Schwarz MXO4 ASIC is responsible for 4.5 million waveform updates per second alongside the digital trigger system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> Where display data instead passes through a general-purpose DSP or applications processor, that path becomes the bottleneck and intensity grading typically suffers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup>

## Dead time and the limits of the number

A high waveform-per-second figure is not automatically better; what matters is dead time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Segmented acquisition modes inflate the headline number by capturing bursts to memory without updating the screen, and one instrument reaching 400,000 to 446,000 waveforms per second this way still failed to find a one-in-a-million pulse because of the dead time between bursts — switching the mode off, with a far lower nominal rate, found it quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Update rates measured on a burst-mode scope are also visibly bursty rather than continuous.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> A scope with zero dead time acquires continuously and decimates the result for display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup>

Claims frequently fail to survive measurement. One instrument specified at 60,000 waveforms per second delivered 10,000 in the normal vector mode everyone uses, reaching 71 kHz only in dot mode at a single time base setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> A 4.5 million per second claim measured 2.3 million in the automatic memory depth mode most users leave selected, reaching the claim only under manual tweaking — described as marketing wankery, while the same instrument still ran two to three times faster than the reference against which it was compared.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> A 1.5 million figure applies solely to a segmented ultra acquire mode, with normal operation limited to around 50,000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup> Conversely, one instrument was measured at 1.038 MHz against a claimed 1 million.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

Update rate also does not predict how responsive a scope feels. An instrument with a poor waveform update rate can still have a quick, responsive user interface, and the two should not be conflated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup> A perceived difference in display speed between two models can turn out to be screen updating rather than waveform updating, which are different things.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-YkgCcYG29nk" title="Siglent SDS1000X HD Display Update Rate + Web Interface">•</a></sup> There is also a case where the fast scope misleads: a very high update rate paints so many acquisitions into the trace that the waveform appears thick and noisy, and slowing the trigger frequency to a few hundred hertz reveals a clean line with nothing about the signal having changed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

## Anomalies

Tektronix digital scopes drop to an extremely low update rate — on the order of 10 to 20 Hz — when there is no trigger, whether the trigger level is moved off the signal or the input is removed entirely, the result of a timeout of roughly 50 to 60 milliseconds in the acquisition engine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> The behaviour is long-standing and specific to that manufacturer; comparable instruments maintain essentially the same update rate whether or not they are triggering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup> One four-series architecture is specified at 100 waveform updates per second in normal mode, with the greater-than-500,000 figure in the manual applying only to a super acquisition mode that must be switched on deliberately and which leaves very little [[sample-memory]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup>
