Electrical resistance is the property of any material or component to oppose the flow of electrons from one point to another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> It is the quantity that turns a [[voltage]] difference into a finite [[current]] rather than an unbounded one: a wire of zero ohms would offer no opposition at all, and a [[battery]] connected across it would be drained almost instantaneously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> Where current does flow against [[resistance]], the energy is dissipated as heat — the mechanism by which a toaster element toasts bread.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup>

In practice resistance is rarely a design parameter in isolation. It is the thing measured to decide whether a contact is good, whether a trace can carry the current, whether a supposed short is real, and whether a product claim is physically possible.

## Measurement

The direct method is a [[multimeter]] across the two points of interest, and for most bench questions that is sufficient: 4 megohms between the input and the output [[ground]] of a high-voltage differential probe, consistent on both inputs and unchanged when the connections are swapped, establishes that the path is a genuine resistance rather than active circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> Confirming the same 4 megohms with a source capable of 500 V rules out any voltage dependence in the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup>

For low values the meter's own leads and the resistance of the connection dominate, and the accurate approach is to force a known current and measure the resulting [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]], then apply Ohm's law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> This is the technique used to characterise a single [[pcb|PCB]] pad: hold the current constant, measure the voltage across the pad without disturbing the [[solder-joint|solder joint]], and repeat over a range of currents from 0.1 A up to 2 A to build a curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> The effectively infinite [[input-impedance|input impedance]] of the multimeter means the [[voltage-measurement|voltage measurement]] itself does not perturb the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Milliohm-scale work on copper strip uses the same method — just over 1 amp of constant current through bare 1 ounce copper produced 52.86 mV, read directly as 52.86 milliohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-319" title="EEVblog #319 - Lead Free PCB Tinning">319</a></sup> An [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] with its leads nulled out is the alternative for milliohm measurements on assembled hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup>

Two measurement errors recur. Gripping both probes while measuring puts the body across the terminals: a reported 2 megohms between two exposed contacts was the person's own hand resistance, and the correct reading was an open circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-135" title="EEVblog #135 - Kindle Case Mythbusting">135</a></sup> And a continuity function that latches will hold a reading such as 1.7 k that is not a real resistance value at all but an artifact of the meter's own behaviour, reproduced as 1.8 k on a second instrument of the same design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1671" title="EEVblog 1671 - Beware of Multimeter Continuity Latching">1671</a></sup> Resolution matters as well; a resistance display offering a single digit of resolution is of little use for anything beyond a go/no-go check.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>

## Conductors, traces and tinning

The resistance of a [[pcb-trace|PCB trace]] can be reduced substantially by tinning it with [[solder]]. Controlled measurements on Vero board strip, referenced against a confirmed 1 ounce bare copper baseline, put the improvement from 60/40 leaded solder anywhere from a 15% decrease for a very thin coat to a 50% decrease — effectively a halving of the trace resistance — for a thickness comparable to what accumulates during [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> Lead-free solder behaves similarly: a 99.3% tin, 0.7% copper alloy applied as a thin layer brought a 52.86 milliohm baseline down to around 46 milliohms, a 13% decrease against the 15% obtained with leaded solder at equivalent thickness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-319" title="EEVblog #319 - Lead Free PCB Tinning">319</a></sup> The two alloys land in the same ballpark, with thickness rather than composition as the dominant variable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-319" title="EEVblog #319 - Lead Free PCB Tinning">319</a></sup>

The effect is reversible. Wicking the solder back off the strip returned it to roughly 52 milliohms, close enough to baseline to confirm that the change is due to the added conductor cross-section and not to some alteration of the copper — though physically scraping along the strip risks removing or leaching a little copper and introducing error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> Since removing [[solder-mask|solder mask]] lets the wave soldering process do the tinning at no additional cost, the technique is close to free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup>

Conductive inks and paints occupy a different regime entirely and are never shorts. Carbon ink on a membrane keypad measures a couple of hundred ohms across a track.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-505" title="EEVblog #505 - IR Learning Remote Control Hack">505</a></sup> A carbon layer laid down on a single-sided board to route around traces gives 47 and 44 ohms on adjacent runs — not a controlled value, but low enough for its purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1108" title="EEVblog #1108 - Casio CT-X700 Keyboard Teardown">1108</a></sup> Silver paint applied to worn keypad contacts read 15 ohms on one pad and around 4 ohms on another, sufficient for a [[microcontroller]] to register the press; the resistance falls as the carrier evaporates, one pad dropping from 12 ohms to 6 ohms with additional drying time and thinner deposits reaching one or two ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup> Fifteen ohms in that application is, in Dave Jones's phrasing, "good enough for Australia".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup> Printed conductive traces on paper suffer from the substrate's porosity; a single printed layer gives a working pad of 7.6 ohms but may break elsewhere in the loop, whereas roughly ten layers on paper approaches conductivity comparable to copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup>

## Sheet resistance

For a uniform film, sheet resistance is a constant of the material and its thickness, expressed in ohms per square, and it does not change with geometry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-732" title="EEVblog #732 - PCB Sheet Resistance">732</a></sup> An arbitrarily shaped region — however many cuts are made in it — has the same ohms-per-square figure; what the shape alters is the end-to-end electrical resistance for the particular practical purpose at hand, obtained by counting squares.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-732" title="EEVblog #732 - PCB Sheet Resistance">732</a></sup>

## Contacts and connectors

[[contact-resistance|Contact resistance]] is usually the dominant term in a [[connector]] assembly. A mains double adapter measured 9.5 milliohms total, covering both internal wires and all the contacts in each section, with most of that residing in the press-fit plug contacts rather than the conductors — those contacts are held by modest force and are exposed to corrosion and contamination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup> An LCR probe pushed into a single socket with significant force read 8.7 milliohms, doubling to about 20 for the pair, consistent with the total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup> At a 2 kW load this amounts to roughly 1 W of loss per adapter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup> Construction choices matter here: internal wiring with crimped terminations is expected to yield higher resistance per unit than a solid brass strip moulded into the plastic, despite the wiring being more expensive to assemble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup>

The same effect appears on the bench as voltage drop. A supply set to 1 V delivering 5 A showed 0.6 V lost in the connecting leads and terminals — a drop invisible at low current and unmissable at high.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gqzZHbEfWDU" title="KoradFollowup Charles">•</a></sup> Crosspoint switches in a solderless [[breadboard]] product contribute 85 ohms in the signal path, enough to disturb a circuit under test and disqualifying the instrument from precision work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup>

Resistance is equally a diagnostic for contacts that should be open or should be intact. An 18 megohm reading on a printer contact confirms something is connected to the pin rather than nothing at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p5-p8Iu7E1c" title="Repairing a Brother Printer with the threat of violence">•</a></sup> Anti-static bench mats measure in the gigohm range — tens of gigohms in some cases — which is why they do not affect a circuit resting on them outside of extraordinarily critical work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-250" title="EEVblog #250 - Anti-Static Mat Myth">250</a></sup>

## Temperature dependence

Ordinary conductors and heating elements have different resistance values in the cold and hot states.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup> This is not a subtlety confined to heaters. A 3.3 V rail measuring 0.11 ohms with a multimeter — at the milliamp test current the meter uses — drew 11 A rather than the ~30 A that figure would predict, because the shorted path heats up and its resistance rises with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup> A short with a [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] behaving this way is common and expected, and the low-current bench measurement should not be assumed to hold at operating current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> The rail in question was not supposed to be 0.1 ohms at all; the correct value was an order of magnitude greater.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

## Load and source resistance

In DC power analysis, plotting power dissipated in a load resistance against the value of that load resistance — with source voltage and source resistance both held fixed, for instance a 100 ohm source — produces the maximum power transfer relationship.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>

## Resistance in electrochemical cells

The internal resistance of a battery, also called IR or ESR, is not a single quantity but two resistances in series. The electrical resistance comes from the internal metal contacts and construction; the ionic resistance comes from the electrochemical reaction itself, and depends on the conductivity of the [[electrolyte]], the electrode surface area and polarization.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> The two are distinguishable in time. Under a current pulse, the [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] drops sharply and immediately — that steep initial step is the electrical resistance, which is always present and acts instantly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> The ionic component lags, showing up only after some delay and settling to a fixed value over time, which is why it is visible only under pulse conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Electrode materials must themselves be low resistance; the positive electrode of a NiMH cell measures around 1 ohm depending on probe placement, with both surface area and bulk volume contributing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1200" title="EEVblog #1200 - BUSTED! - Energizer &amp; Duracell NiMH">1200</a></sup>

## Resistance in false product claims

Resistance is a favoured ingredient in pseudo-technical marketing, mixed with heat loss, harmonic distortion, power quality, reactive power and [[electromagnetic-interference|electromagnetic interference]] to produce claims that sound plausible without meaning anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-870" title="EEVblog #870 - Shonky Z-Energeia Energy Saving Wipes">870</a></sup> Wipes sold on the premise that cleaning a [[fuse]] box aligns the electrons and lowers resistance fall into this category and would require controlled experiments and proper engineering measurements to substantiate — measurements the vendor does not supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-870" title="EEVblog #870 - Shonky Z-Energeia Energy Saving Wipes">870</a></sup>

A related claim is that a heating element's resistivity is identical in the cold and hot states, offered as an [[efficiency]] advantage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup> It is irrelevant: a resistive radiant heater converts everything into heat and is 100% efficient regardless of internal losses, so the figure of merit is the energy delivered, not the constancy of the element's resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup>

## The infinite resistor grid

A recurring puzzle asks for the resistance measured across one [[resistor]] in an infinite two-dimensional grid of identical resistors, conventionally 1 ohm each although the value does not matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup> The answer is 0.5·R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup> The result is verifiable on a finite physical grid: with R = 10 k, the expected 5 k appears as measurements of 5.034 k, 5.017 k and 5.02 k at various points, all within the tolerance such a grid would be expected to show.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup> The harder variant asks for the resistance between diagonally opposite nodes, which resists intuitive solution and often drives people to write a program; the accepted answer is 2/π times R, about 0.636·R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup>
