Battery voltage is the terminal [[voltage]] a cell or pack presents to the circuit it powers, and it is the single most important thing about a [[battery]] that a designer cannot treat as fixed. It is not constant: over a discharge it falls along a characteristic curve whose shape depends on chemistry, load [[current]] and temperature, and it sags further under load because of the cell's internal [[resistance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> Almost every practical consequence of battery-powered design — where a product cuts out, how much of the stored energy is actually usable, how efficient the converter running off the pack will be — follows from that decline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

## The discharge curve

Plotting cell voltage against discharge time, with current held constant, produces the characteristic curve that defines a battery's usable life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> The subject of the plot need not be a single cell — it can equally be several cells in series, in parallel, or a combination of both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> For a lithium-ion cell being charged the same curve runs the other way: from a flat cell at around 2.8 V the terminal voltage rises fairly linearly under constant current, then tapers as it approaches 4.2 V, the float charge voltage threshold at which the charger switches from constant current to constant voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> That threshold has to be held to within about 1% or better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup>

Alkaline cells start higher than their nominal rating — a fresh AAA straight from the pack measures 1.6174 V — and then decline steadily under load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup> Below about 0.8 V the voltage collapses rapidly rather than continuing to taper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup> Cells discharged at half an amp down to 0.8 V are effectively finished; drawing half an amp from one afterwards pulls the terminal voltage down to around half a volt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-508" title="EEVblog #508 - Can You Test Battery Charge By Dropping It?">508</a></sup> Even after a capacity meter has declared a pack exhausted, the open-circuit reading may still be well above the cut-off, so the terminal voltage measured at the battery itself rather than at the end of the test leads is the meaningful figure — 1.21 V at the cell when the tester had already stopped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup>

## Voltage under load and internal resistance

The gap between an open-circuit reading and a loaded one is the product of the cell's ESR and the current drawn, and it is directly visible: connecting a load to a battery-powered instrument makes the displayed [[voltage-drop]] immediately in response to the internal ESR and the current draw.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> Under a pulse load the drop has two components with different time constants. The very steep initial step is the electrical resistance, which acts instantly; after some time the ionic resistance kicks in and drags the voltage down further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Pulse characteristic graphs in coin-cell data sheets are the method by which these two resistances are separated and measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup>

Because voltage sags under current, capacity claims that ignore load conditions are meaningless. A capacity figure is only interpretable alongside whether it assumes constant current or constant power, what happens under pulse loads, and what happens as the battery voltage drops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> For the same reason capacity is properly expressed in watt-hours rather than amp-hours: amp-hours is the more simplistic figure precisely because it discards the falling voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>

Measuring the true terminal voltage during a discharge test requires four-terminal connection, with sense wires taken to the sense terminals so the reading reflects the battery and not the drop in the load leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-865" title="EEVblog #865 - How To Increase Alkaline Battery Capacity">865</a></sup> Lead drop is not always negligible — a pack under a 4 A load shows a battery voltage that has dropped substantially, part of which belongs to the leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lYKjScnkeq0" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery: Capacity Test">•</a></sup>

## Cut-off voltage and wasted capacity

Where a product decides the battery is flat determines how much of the battery it actually uses. Designing a low-battery warning or cut-out at 1.1 V per cell throws away all the capacity that lies under the remaining part of the discharge curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Instruments vary widely in how well they do this. An Agilent U1272A specified to start flashing its battery symbol at 4.4 V in fact held on to 4.3 V, and the engineering preference is for a warning that waits until about 1 V per cell so that more of the battery's capacity is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> At the other extreme, a meter whose display becomes unreadable at 2.5 V across two cells has failed well before 1.25 V per cell, and its low-battery indicator is useless because the display carrying that indicator has already gone dark.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

The same reasoning applies to regulators rather than displays. In a constant-current [[led]] driver the voltage at which the regulator drops out of regulation is the figure that matters as the battery ages, and is found by winding the supply down while watching the LED current — one such headlamp was still regulating at around 1.1 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup>

Where a chip sets the floor, the cut-out follows from its [[datasheet|data sheet]]. An op-amp rated for single-supply operation from 2.7 V to 5.5 V, powered from a nominal 3 V battery, must not be allowed below 2.7 V as the battery voltage drops, which means the product needs a low-voltage battery detection circuit cutting out at about 2.7 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> The choice of battery type, the battery voltage and its compatibility with the device being driven are among the first decisions in a battery-powered design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

## Effect on converters and regulators

A [[boost-converter]]'s [[efficiency]] is a function of its input voltage as well as its output current, so the efficiency of a battery-powered supply changes over the discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> At 0.7 V input the efficiency of one such converter is a good 10% below what it achieves at 1.5 V, and efficiency also falls off at low output currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Data-sheet efficiency curves are given for specific values of Vbat — 0.9 V, for example — and only apply at that input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

This voltage dependence is exactly what makes claims of recovering large amounts of "unused energy" from a battery by boosting its declining voltage back to 1.5 V misleading rather than impossible. Boosting low battery voltage up to 1.5 V violates no law of physics, and the underlying problem — that devices stop working while charge remains — is real and well known.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> What fails is the arithmetic: describing new batteries as containing "1.5 volts of energy" confuses voltage with energy, and the claim that many devices stop functioning around the 1.3 V mark is demonstrably not true of the majority of devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> In a passive load such as a toy motor the boost converter makes matters worse: as the battery voltage drops the converter draws more and more current from the battery to hold its output, consuming more power for little gain in performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-963" title="EEVblog #963 - Batteriser / Batteroo Passive Toy Test!">963</a></sup> Measured directly, a cell reading 1.5 V at the sleeve output was down to 1.18 V at the battery underneath it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-963" title="EEVblog #963 - Batteriser / Batteroo Passive Toy Test!">963</a></sup>

Where the converter's behaviour is known, it can be compensated in [[firmware]]. In a battery-powered bench supply the input battery voltage is fed to a second ADC channel of the [[microcontroller]], and the converter's efficiency and output-current curves are programmed in so that the software can compute the maximum output capability from the present battery voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Where no regulator is present at all, the output simply is the battery voltage: the 12 V output of one portable power station is connected directly across an internal pack nominally at 12.8 V, so the output tracks the cell voltage down through the discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lYKjScnkeq0" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery: Capacity Test">•</a></sup>

## Voltage as a state-of-charge indicator

Inferring remaining capacity from terminal voltage is a guess, and a poor one near the ends of the curve. A power station without a coulomb counter can only measure the battery voltage and estimate what capacity is left, which produces a very non-linear indication as the pack nears empty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uxm3qeKcg3w" title="EcoFlow Delta Pro Battery Capacity TESTED">•</a></sup> The alternative is a proper coulomb charge counter, which logs the charge going into the battery and the charge coming back out, giving an accurate indication of when the battery is actually dead rather than relying on voltage and chemistry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uxm3qeKcg3w" title="EcoFlow Delta Pro Battery Capacity TESTED">•</a></sup> Dedicated parts exist for this, and their absence in a top-end product is a real shortcoming.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uxm3qeKcg3w" title="EcoFlow Delta Pro Battery Capacity TESTED">•</a></sup>

Voltage-referenced capacity ratings carry the same weakness. A power bank rated 20,000 mAh quotes that figure against the internal battery voltage of 3.85 V held constant — "input referred capacity", which multiplies the two numbers into a watt-hour figure that excludes the losses of the DC-to-DC converter feeding the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

Where a voltage-based gauge is built anyway, the interface constraint is the chip. Driving a bar-graph gauge from the pack requires a [[voltage-divider]] to halve the battery voltage first, because the [[comparator]] inputs will not swing all the way to the supply rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

## Voltage as a diagnostic

In repair, the battery voltage is a reference value against which unknown nodes on a board are identified — a probe reading of 4.26 V on an unknown pin is recognisable as the battery voltage, as distinct from 5 V [[usb]] minus a [[diode]] drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup> The same reading taken at the battery itself confirms whether a charging path is working: 3.03 V on the pack with the charger connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup>

Insufficient battery voltage produces characteristic symptoms rather than a dead device. A backup cell too low to hold the supply causes the system to reset continually as the low-power-detect circuit repeatedly switches power off, heard as a reset hiccup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup> A keyfob cell at 2.69 V is on its way out and no longer has enough voltage for the in-car proximity function, even though the fob is not entirely dead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cc_GqtBuP4c" title="Hyundai IONIQ Keyfob Teardown + Battery Replacement">•</a></sup> A watch cell at 0.7 V will not accept a charge at all, against 1.42 V for a good one of the same nominal 1.5 V lithium rechargeable type, and 1.1 to 1.21 V for one that is flat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JWr5E2ej5cg" title="Part 2: Citizen EcoDrive Watch Battery Replacement">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0018phUFjuQ" title="Citizen EcoDrive Watch Battery Replacement">•</a></sup>

Packs can be dead well past the point of recovery: a NiCd-era instrument pack reading 0.4 V is flat enough that recharging it means bypassing the internal charging circuitry and applying current directly to the contacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XpZVIWdXliY" title="Fluke OneTouch Network Tester Battery Restoration Attempt">•</a></sup> Conversely, a normal-looking voltage rules a battery out as the fault — two cells in a portable DAC both reading 4 V are fully charged, which points instead at capacity or ESR as the complaint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1613" title="EEVblog 1613 - Audiophile REPAIR: $4500 Chord Hugo DAC">1613</a></sup> A pack in a 15 kWh home storage installation measuring around 52 V is consistent with the roughly 40% pre-charged state it ships in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1634" title="EEVblog 1634 - 15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery Install!">1634</a></sup>

Series strings deserve individual attention, since the terminal voltage of the string hides the state of its members. Cells in a discharged multi-cell assembly that read around 2 V per pack have discharged evenly, but in a string where one cell drains first that cell can be reverse charged by the rest — one measured at negative half a volt, another at 0.23 V, while its neighbours still held 1.3 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2Y1tVvllklc" title="9V Duracell Alkaline Battery Leakage">•</a></sup>

Automotive and vehicle systems report battery voltage as a primary health indicator: 13.8 V read over an OBD link indicates the alternator is working and charging the battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup> A small electric vehicle designed to warn below about 12.5 V is telling the truth when it starts beeping at that point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-501" title="EEVblog #501 - Sinclair C5 Electric Car Teardown &amp; Test Drive">501</a></sup>

## Instrument self-measurement

Battery-powered test equipment commonly measures and displays its own supply. An LCR tweezer reports 4.4 V through a service menu;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> a bench [[multimeter]] reports 3.07 V through an info screen;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup> a pocket dosimeter's statistics page reports both the internal battery voltage and the 360 V high-voltage rail for its Geiger-Müller tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-776" title="EEVblog #776 - World&#x27;s Smallest Digital Dosimeter">776</a></sup> Radio nodes and wearables report it as telemetry alongside their payload data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1062" title="EEVblog1062 - The Things Network - Progress Edit">1062</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup> Larger installations expose battery voltage in cloud dashboards alongside battery temperature, [[inverter]] temperature and grid parameters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aANZNwmnC_4" title="AERL Lithium Home Storage Battery - Cloud Data">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5C_IT9F4ZkA" title="eevBLAB 128 - Why Dont I Go OFF-GRID Solar/Battery?">•</a></sup>

Characterising an instrument's own [[battery-life]] is done by measuring supply voltage and supply current simultaneously with two meters while the unit is powered from an external supply at the nominal battery voltage — 9 V, for instance — which reveals figures such as an 18 µA standby draw that are frequently absent from the manual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup>

## Design consequences of a moving supply

Because the supply moves, anything whose output depends on it must either regulate or be designed to tolerate the range. A series dropper [[resistor]] for an LED string is sized against the nominal battery voltage: 7.2 V nominal, less 5.6 V of LED string voltage, leaves 1.6 V across the resistor, giving 6.4 Ω at 250 mA and a quarter-watt dissipation — with the acknowledgement that the actual pack voltage will wander between roughly 7 and 8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> A tape transport takes the opposite approach: a servo amplifier holds the motor at constant speed regardless of the battery voltage and belt tension, because an unregulated motor would let playback speed follow the discharge curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup> Where a processor runs practically directly from the battery voltage with no 5 V regulation in between, the entire logic supply moves with the pack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup>
