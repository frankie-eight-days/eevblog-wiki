Solar irradiance is the amount of [[solar-power|solar power]] falling on a unit of area, expressed in watts per square meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup> It is the single input that sets the ceiling on what any [[solar-power-system|photovoltaic]] installation can produce: a capture area of one square meter under nominal conditions intercepts roughly 1,000 W, and no amount of optical or electrical cleverness extracts more energy than arrives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 - WAVJA Solar Balls, 60x Better Than A Solar Panel? - Part 1">1633</a></sup> Because the figure varies with season, latitude, cloud, time of day and even minute to minute, the gap between a panel's nameplate rating and its real output is largely a story about irradiance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup>

## Terminology

The quantity is also called solar insolation, and loosely solar radiation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5C_IT9F4ZkA" title="eevBLAB 128 - Why Dont I Go OFF-GRID Solar/Battery?">•</a></sup> Insolation and irradiance are technically distinct, though the terms are used interchangeably in casual system discussion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5C_IT9F4ZkA" title="eevBLAB 128 - Why Dont I Go OFF-GRID Solar/Battery?">•</a></sup> Irradiance is measured directly by an instrument called a pyranometer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup>

## The 1,000 W/m² standard

One thousand watts per square meter is the industry standard test condition, and it appears on every [[solar-cell|solar cell]] [[datasheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1553" title="EEVblog 1553 - World&#x27;s First Commercial Solar Power Station">1553</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 - WAVJA Solar Balls, 60x Better Than A Solar Panel? - Part 1">1633</a></sup> Standard test conditions (STC) specify 1,000 W/m² at a nominal module temperature of 25 °C, and the STC number is what appears in marketing material and on the panel's nameplate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup> Datasheets typically carry a second set of electrical properties at NMOT, the nominal module operating temperature — for one 370 W panel, 42 °C at an irradiance of 800 W/m².<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup> That same panel produces 277 W under the 800 W/m² NMOT condition rather than its 370 W STC figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup>

Panel [[efficiency]] is the fraction of that incident 1,000 W/m² a module converts. A high-end residential panel of 1.76 m² rated at 400 W is 22.6% efficient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 EXTENDED VERSION - LightYear Zero + Aptera Solar EV">1480</a></sup> A 370 W panel of about 1.7 m² works out to roughly 217 W per square meter of panel area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1467" title="EEVblog 1467 - Stanford Solar Power at Nightime!  BUSTED">1467</a></sup> Photovoltaic cells are doing well at 25%, which stands as a practical maximum, and the Shockley limit bounds what any single-junction cell can reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1591" title="EEVblog 1591 - MORE Colas Wattway Solar Cycleways in the Netherlands!">1591</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 - WAVJA Solar Balls, 60x Better Than A Solar Panel? - Part 1">1633</a></sup>

## Variability

Irradiance changes not just daily but hourly and minute by minute; even under a perfectly clear sky with the sun in the same position, output differs from one year's date to the same date the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup> Day-to-day swings of around 20% between otherwise perfect days are ordinary, and a heavily overcast day may deliver only 500 W/m² or a couple of hundred.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup> Cloud passing over a fixed array produces sharp step changes in output — a curve that jumps from 2.25 kW to 2.32 kW in one step is an irradiance change, not a thermal one, since panel heating cannot act that fast.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup> A day full of deep cloud dips can nonetheless total the same energy as a smooth one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup>

Geography and season dominate the annual picture. Irradiance maps show Australia reaching the 1,000 W/m² band across much of the country in December, while the same maps for January in the northern hemisphere show southern Spain falling well short of the summer peak it reaches in June.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 EXTENDED VERSION - LightYear Zero + Aptera Solar EV">1480</a></sup> Sydney exceeds 1,000 W/m² on some days in summer, and output there can roughly halve in midwinter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 EXTENDED VERSION - LightYear Zero + Aptera Solar EV">1480</a></sup> A north-facing array in the southern hemisphere typically runs 30 to 40% down in winter even on a high-irradiance day, and worse when panels are not ideally oriented.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BXVgk-uoxn8" title="Hoymiles Solar Microinverter Install + Deye Gen Connection">•</a></sup> The same seasonal shortfall is why a household [[battery]] may fail to charge from excess generation in winter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5C_IT9F4ZkA" title="eevBLAB 128 - Why Dont I Go OFF-GRID Solar/Battery?">•</a></sup>

Above the atmosphere the figure is higher and far more stable: about 1,357 to 1,360 W/m² at Earth's distance from the sun, which is why spacecraft solar arrays outperform equivalent terrestrial ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1637" title="EEVblog 1637: Solar Freakin&#x27; Space Mirrors! - Reflect Orbital DEBUNKED">1637</a></sup> Atmospheric losses account for the drop to the terrestrial nominal of 1,000 W/m².<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1637" title="EEVblog 1637: Solar Freakin&#x27; Space Mirrors! - Reflect Orbital DEBUNKED">1637</a></sup>

Soiling is a comparatively minor effect. On a clear morning, an array reading 1,852 W before washing read 1,967 W afterwards, but the sun was still rising through the measurement, which confounds the comparison; the practical conclusion is that cleaning makes little difference unless panels are genuinely filthy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0KX5F7Si-TY" title="Does Cleaning Solar Panels Make a Difference?">•</a></sup>

## Consequences for system design

Because irradiance is a hard ceiling rather than a design variable, sizing decisions turn on how often the ceiling is actually approached. Panels rated at 370 W paired with 295 W peak microinverters will clip on roughly 38% of days per year in Sydney.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup> Under the 800 W/m² NMOT condition the same combination never clips at all, so paying for higher-rated microinverters buys nothing where that level of irradiance is not reached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup> Even the highest-output [[microinverter]] in that family, a 349 W peak unit, cannot match a 370 W panel at 1,000 W/m².<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup>

The engineering judgment that follows is to fit the highest-output panels available and let the [[inverter]] clip, rather than letting inverter selection dictate panel choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup> Two panels of identical physical size see identical irradiance, so the lower-rated one simply converts less of it; under poor conditions the larger-rated panel still yields more, and the inverter absorbs the surplus.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup> Deliberate array oversizing on this reasoning is common practice in Europe, where irradiance is lower than in Australia.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1386" title="EEVblog 1386 - 295W Inverter vs 370W Solar Panel - WTF?">1386</a></sup>

Monitoring platforms exploit the same relationship in reverse: knowing panel area, orientation, tilt and the irradiance incident at a given moment, they compute the expected yield and report installation efficiency against it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup> Relative-efficiency figures reported this way move with irradiance and can be distorted by an overstated system rating or by panels that are not honestly rated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup>

## Irradiance as a physical bound

The fixed energy density arriving at a capture area is the standard test against extraordinary solar claims. A one square meter panel intercepts 1,000 W under nominal irradiance and that is the whole budget — "You can't get more than that, Captain."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 - WAVJA Solar Balls, 60x Better Than A Solar Panel? - Part 1">1633</a></sup>

Applied to a solar-roadway installation, 2,800 panels of about 120 W each assumed a nominal 1,000 W/m² under regular test conditions across a 336 kW system, while real yield estimates for flat-laid panels at that location — derived from measured local irradiation over a full year — fall well short of what a conventionally tilted commercial array achieves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1047" title="EEVblog #1047 - Solar Roadways FINALLY BUSTED! (Colas Wattway)">1047</a></sup> Two installations 30 km apart experience essentially the same irradiation over an eleven-month span, which makes their output ratio a fair size-adjusted comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1047" title="EEVblog #1047 - Solar Roadways FINALLY BUSTED! (Colas Wattway)">1047</a></sup>

Applied to a solar-bodied car, 5 m² of the best available residential-grade cells at 1,000 W/m² gives about 1,185 W under ideal conditions — a figure that additionally demands summer, cloudless skies, and no shading whatsoever from trees, buildings or lampposts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 EXTENDED VERSION - LightYear Zero + Aptera Solar EV">1480</a></sup>

Applied to an orbital reflector, a 10 m by 10 m mirror redirects at most the 100 m² of sunlight it intercepts, over a few minutes per satellite pass; a [[ground]]-level claim of 200 W/m² across a 5 km spot is inconsistent with that geometry by orders of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1637" title="EEVblog 1637: Solar Freakin&#x27; Space Mirrors! - Reflect Orbital DEBUNKED">1637</a></sup> A balloon-mounted mirror test measuring 516 W/m² at 242 m does not scale to orbital distances.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1637" title="EEVblog 1637: Solar Freakin&#x27; Space Mirrors! - Reflect Orbital DEBUNKED">1637</a></sup>

Applied to nighttime photovoltaics, 50 mW/m² recovered after dark against a peak of about 217 W/m² is on the order of 0.02% of the panel's daytime output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1467" title="EEVblog 1467 - Stanford Solar Power at Nightime!  BUSTED">1467</a></sup>

Historical concentrating systems face the same accounting. A commercial solar power station using a 5 m diameter dish under a nominal 1,000 W/m² lost about 14% to mirror reflection alone, ending at 10 to 12% total system efficiency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1553" title="EEVblog 1553 - World&#x27;s First Commercial Solar Power Station">1553</a></sup>
