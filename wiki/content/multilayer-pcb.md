A multilayer PCB is a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] carrying copper on internal layers as well as the two outer faces, built by laminating several thin double-sided cores together into a single stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup> Layer counts of four, six, eight and upward are routine, with twenty or more available from ordinary board houses,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup> and extreme examples run far beyond that: a 24-layer board 8 mm thick,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-532" title="EEVblog #532 - Silicon Chip Wafer Fab Mailbag">532</a></sup> and an IBM thermal conduction module from the early 1990s built as a 63-layer ceramic substrate carrying over a hundred individual dies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1358" title="EEVblog #1358 - $250,000 IBM Processor X-RAYED!">1358</a></sup> The internal layers buy routing space, controlled [[impedance]] and continuous [[ground]] and power planes — none of which are available on a double-sided board — at the cost of manufacturing complexity, price, and near-total loss of visual access to the circuit.

## Construction

Manufacture is essentially the double-sided process repeated and stacked. The inner cores are made much thinner than a normal board, on material called prepreg, and are processed as double-sided PCBs but with the [[solder-mask|solder mask]] and [[silkscreen|silk screen]] omitted; the finished inner layers are then sandwiched and glued together, and only the outermost copper receives solder mask and silk screen at the end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup> The result is the same sequence of steps as a two-layer board, simply many more of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup> Prepreg layers are thin enough to be used as boards in their own right — a calculator board on the order of 0.1 to 0.2 mm appears to be nothing more than the prepreg that would normally sit inside a multilayer stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1078" title="EEVblog #1078 - World&#x27;s Thinnest Calculator Teardown!">1078</a></sup>

Because the inner planes sit on very thin prepreg, copper must be pulled back from the board outline. Copper taken right to the edge can be smeared or burred when the board is sawn or routed out of the panel, shorting the planes together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup> The exception is deliberate: a test stack brings the internal copper layers out to the edge of the tooling strip so the stack-up can be inspected on the finished bare board, alongside an impedance test coupon.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup>

## Stack-up and copper weight

Every piece of copper in the stack can be specified independently — an eight-layer board has eight copper layers, each of which can be ordered at a chosen thickness, so a layer expected to carry heavy [[current]] can be made thicker than the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> One ounce copper is 35 microns; half-ounce is not usually offered as a general option, but on four-layer and other multilayer boards the internal layers are commonly half-ounce while the outer layers are one ounce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Anything unusual — a custom stack-up, or internal structures such as blind vias reaching only part way through — is a chargeable option and applies only to multilayer boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup>

Current rating differs between internal and external copper because the outer layers can shed heat to free air while the inner ones cannot, so an internal trace must be roughly three times as wide for the same temperature rise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup> On a four-layer board carrying 1 A with a 10 °C rise, an internal trace works out near 30 thou wide against about 31 thou at the limit; holding the rise to 1 °C instead pushes the requirement to roughly 50 thou on the outer layer and 125 thou on the inner.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup> Extra layers also allow a wide conductor to be split: a trace requiring 3 in of width for 80 A at 4 oz copper can be built as roughly 1.5 in on each of two layers, or spread across four layers of a four-layer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup>

Plane ordering matters as much as thickness. Ground and power planes should be sandwiched directly against each other across the thin internal prepreg rather than pushed to the outside of the stack; this both keeps the signal traces accessible on the outer layers and preserves the distributed [[capacitance]] between the two plates, which is destroyed if the planes are separated by the signal layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Why designers go multilayer

Pin escape from fine-pitch BGAs is the most common forcing function. Routing out a [[bga|BGA]] with peripheral and centre balls pushes a design to at least six or eight layers, and boards are seen at ten where a ground layer is placed between every signal layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-444" title="EEVblog #444 - Car Lane Guidance Camera Teardown">444</a></sup> A large BGA on an [[oscilloscope]] board must be multilayer simply to fan out, likely six layers or more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Dense [[processor]] interconnects show the same signature — a mass of vias fanning out under a board-to-board [[connector]] into a large LSI chipset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1211" title="EEVblog 1211 - Apple&#x27;s Most Beautiful Failure. The G4 Cube">1211</a></sup> High-density designs with tightly packed passives may reach ten or twelve layers purely to fit the nets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1019" title="EEVblog #1019 - Mailbag">1019</a></sup>

Beyond routing, internal layers serve [[signal-integrity|signal integrity]] and EMC. A full internal [[ground-plane|ground plane]] flood-filled across the entire board, including under a mains section, provides continuous return.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup> [[via-stitching|Via stitching]] around the perimeter ties the internal planes together, lowering the impedance and [[inductance]] of the [[grounding]] system — common practice in RF systems — and a dense ring of vias at the board edge blocks RF leakage out the side of the stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Tight bypass loops on high-performance designs are a reason to move to a six- or eight-layer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-464" title="EEVblog #464 - Mailbag">464</a></sup> Length-matched differential pairs and controlled impedance likewise imply a multilayer stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup> Compliance can force the change outright: an early portable computer's double-sided board, which had no large ground plane, was re-laid out as a multilayer version in 1982 to meet FCC requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup>

Multilayer construction also helps very high impedance analog nodes. Guard traces prevent surface leakage into sensitive nodes, and on a multilayer board they are also placed on internal layers to catch leakage within the stack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup>

Cost is the counterweight. A large share of a product's cost is the bare board, especially when it is multilayer and high density.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-335" title="EEVblog #335 - Carbon Printed Resistors">335</a></sup> In the early 1980s four-layer and higher boards were available but prohibitively expensive for a consumer item, making a single double-sided board with all components on one side a hard design requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup> On simple designs the layers are simply unnecessary — a board with no bypassing and nothing on the bottom does not need to be multilayer at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup> Where the layers are present in a piece of test gear, the judgement is favourable: one meter is described as multilayer, "none of this two-layer rubbish".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup>

## Identifying and counting layers

Internal copper is often visible through the substrate as darker traces or a darker green flood under strong light, which is the usual way a multilayer board is recognised on sight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1256" title="EEVblog #1256 - GORGEOUS Mystery Test Gear Teardown!">1256</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup> A board dark all over under the surface routing indicates internal planes — one such case being a four-layer board carrying 5 V and ground internally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup> Backlighting a board with an even light source can reveal inner traces, though a well flood-filled internal ground plane defeats the technique entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup>

A well-designed board removes the guesswork by including layer designators: physical copper numerals placed one per layer, so that "1" sits on the top layer, "2" on the layer beneath it, and so on down to the bottom layer, allowing the layer count to be read directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Partially obscured identifiers still bound the answer, since the count cannot be odd.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup> Failing that, the copper layers can sometimes be counted directly at an exposed board edge — eight layers of alternating ground and power visible in a spectrum analyser's module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup>

Routing style is another tell. Traditional multilayer boards route predominantly vertically on one side and predominantly horizontally on the other, with the remaining connections handled on the layers in between — the classic top-bottom alternate routing seen on processor boards of the DIP era and on later instrument boards alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup>

## Working on a multilayer board

[[reverse-engineering|Reverse engineering]] is the clearest penalty. Nets disappear into the stack, and tracing them becomes a systematic, tedious search rather than visual inspection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup> A pin with no track on either the top or the bottom can only be resolved by probing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup> The Apollo Saturn V LVDC board is an extreme case with no traces on the bottom whatsoever, all interconnect being internal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup> Faults inside the stack are equally awkward: a short inside a [[via]] or a pad on an eight- or ten-layer board, or an internally shorted power plane, is notoriously difficult to locate, and one approach is to inject a reasonable current and follow the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] of the conductor with a current probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

Rework is harder because the inner copper acts as a [[heat-sink|heat sink]]. [[desoldering|Desoldering]] a Molex power connector from a multilayer motherboard is slower than from a two-layer board owing to the [[thermal-capacity|thermal capacity]] of the inner layers, and depends heavily on how the designer detailed the thermal reliefs on the pads; raising the iron to around 350 °C and dwelling longer lets the heat penetrate to the inner layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup> The same reasoning applies to salvage: a connector tied into large ground planes needs a decent [[solder]] sucker, and probably has thermal reliefs on its pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-946" title="EEVblog #946 - Apple (Raspberry) Pi Cluster - PART 2">946</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1019" title="EEVblog #1019 - Mailbag">1019</a></sup> Beefy multilayer boards also slow hot-air rework of power packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup> At the extreme, a 60-plus-layer substrate absorbs so much heat that a BGA will not release.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2G7Z9IIQoIs" title="IBM BGA Thermal Futility...">•</a></sup> The stored heat persists after assembly — boards with internal ground planes trap heat in the inner copper and stay warm long after leaving a reflow oven.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup>

Modifications carry a specific hazard. Getting a wire from a bottom-side trace to the top sometimes means drilling through the board, which on a multilayer stack risks shorting the inner layers — serious when those layers are 5 V and ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup> Large pads and vias are valued partly because they allow mod wires to pass through without drilling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup>

## Advanced structures

Several manufacturing options exist only on multilayer boards. Blind vias formed by laser drilling reach a specified internal layer rather than passing through the stack, and are the sort of structure that accompanies a dense mobile-phone-class custom stack-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> An open cavity board is made by routing the opening in the upper stack only, then bonding it to a lower stack that is left solid, leaving a recess — achievable without controlled-depth Z-axis milling, because the material is removed before lamination rather than after.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Chips can be embedded within the layers themselves so that the finished assembly is as flat as the bare board, which requires multilayer construction together with controlled-depth routing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup> Micro vias on multilayer boards are handled by the same via-property calculators used for ordinary through-hole vias on a standard 1.6 mm two-layer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Fine-pitch BGA escape can also force very small drill sizes when individual vias must be dropped through to a lower layer on a four-layer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

A planar [[transformer]] is a direct application of the technology: the windings are etched turns on a multilayer PCB, stacked as layers with copper thickness selected per turn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> The approach yields a very low-profile component and better process control than mechanically wound wire, along with [[efficiency]] advantages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup>

Additive manufacturing of multilayer boards exists at the prototype end, in the form of a 3D printer aimed at rapid-turnaround prototype boards rather than production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aLcdm6gB6eE" title="DragonFly $300,000 3D PCB Printer Prototype">•</a></sup>

## Design tools

CAD support for multilayer work is uneven. Single-layer display modes, which grey out all layers except the one being edited and highlight it above the rest, are essentially only meaningful on a multilayer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup> Three-dimensional board viewers that let the user fly inside the stack are most useful on multilayer designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-968" title="EEVblog #968 - Mystery EDA Package">968</a></sup> Autorouters ship with named strategies including a default multilayer algorithm alongside the two-layer ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup> Autorouted multilayer results are recognisable by right-angled traces, which no careful designer would produce by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> Routing quality remains the limiting factor: a board with all traces running one direction on one side and the perpendicular direction on the other is a golden rule for double-sided and multilayer boards alike, and machine routers still fail it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup>
