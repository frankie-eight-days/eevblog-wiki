Static [[ram|RAM]] is volatile read/write memory that holds each bit in a latch rather than a [[capacitor]], so it needs no refresh circuitry and no controller to keep it alive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> Two consequences follow, and between them they account for nearly every appearance of the part: it is fast, which makes it the memory of choice wherever an [[fpga|FPGA]] or a sampling front end has to write at full rate,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> and its standby [[current]] is so small that a coin cell can hold its contents for the shelf life of the cell, which made it the storage medium of every pre-flash pocket computer, organiser and calibrated instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup> The penalty is cost and density: SRAM has always been the expensive way to buy a kilobyte, which is why early home computers used dynamic RAM instead and could afford 16 K when SRAM of that size was out of reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup>

## Speed grades and part marking

Old SRAM is marked with a speed suffix, and reading it is a routine part of identifying a board. A dash and a number on the end of the part number is an access time: `-70` is 70 nanoseconds, while `-15` may mean 15 nanoseconds or 150 depending on the process the device was built on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> The range encountered in practice is wide. A Pyramid Semiconductor device of 256 bits by 4 ran at 15 nanoseconds, small enough that its only plausible role is a buffer or fast counter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Fairchild 81C78A parts in a signal averager were 45 nanoseconds, sitting alongside Sony devices in the same instrument at 100 nanoseconds for the slower, already-serialised data path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup> The same 45-nanosecond MB81C78A, 64 kbit organised as 8 K bytes, served as video memory in the Sharp X68000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-746" title="EEVblog #746 - Sharp X68000 Retro Computer Teardown">746</a></sup> At the other end, a home-built logic analyser used 650-nanosecond SRAM purely as a capture buffer behind a binary address counter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup> IBM's 1991 multi-chip [[processor]] module carried 128 kbit SRAMs with 10-nanosecond access time, a large and fast part for its date.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup>

## Acquisition and display memory in instruments

Digital oscilloscopes divide their memory into two kinds, and the division is visible on any teardown. Bulk [[sample-memory|sample memory]] is DDR or SDRAM because it is cheap per bit; the fast working memory bolted to the acquisition or display FPGA is SRAM because it must accept writes at full [[sample-rate|sample rate]] with low latency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> The Rigol DS1054Z pairs a 512 Mbit DDR2 sample buffer with a Cypress 9-megabit SRAM in a 256 K by 32 configuration, used to accumulate the frames that produce intensity-graded display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup> The Uni-T UPO2104CS uses a Cypress CY7C1360, a 9-megabit 256 K by 32/36 pipelined SRAM, for the same job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup> The Siglent SDS2000X carries an 18-megabit NetSol part, 1 meg by 18, feeding the 256-level intensity graded display,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> and the Siglent 1000X a 9-megabit synchronous pipeline burst SRAM on the acquisition FPGA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup> The Rigol DS2000 uses two Cypress CY73 1380C 18-megabit pipelined SRAMs on the display side alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> The Rohde & Schwarz HMO1202 uses three ISSI 9-megabit synchronous SRAMs running around 200 MHz, just over a megabyte per chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup>

Where the whole sample memory is small enough, SRAM does that job too. A Micsig handheld with 240 K per channel puts two ISSI 256 K by 18-bit, 4-megabit SRAMs next to the Cyclone FPGA, because SRAM is easy to drive directly from an FPGA and fast; the parts are wide enough that the memory could have been doubled at no layout cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Fluke's PM3370B Combiscope builds its sample memory from eight 2 K high-speed SRAMs, four per side, with an extra two pads per footprint and 32K/2K jumper links so that 8 K parts can be fitted in place of the 2 K ones for 32 K of sample memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup> Logic analysers follow the same rule, using SRAM rather than DRAM for the trace buffer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup>

## Battery backup and retained data

Before flash was cheap, the working memory of a pocket organiser, diary or handheld computer was also its only storage, so the SRAM was never allowed to lose power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> These machines were built with one or two operational cells plus a separate backup cell, and removing all of them at once destroys the data, since the only remaining charge is what the main capacitor holds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup> The current involved is minuscule — retention life is effectively the shelf life of the [[battery]] — which is why such machines are often still holding their contents decades later.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> Cambridge's Z88 backs its 32 K internal SRAM and RAM packs with a [[supercapacitor]] so cells can be swapped without loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> The Casio FX730P steers its batteries with diodes so that during backup only the 8 K 6264 SRAM is powered, not the whole machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1102" title="EEVblog #1102 - Casio FX730P 1980&#x27;s BASIC Scientific Computer">1102</a></sup> The Apple Newton stores everything in five Mitsubishi 128 K by 8 SRAMs — 640 K in total — with no flash at all, which is why its backup cell is critical; later Newtons moved to flash.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-418" title="EEVblog #418 - Mailbag Apple Newton Teardown">418</a></sup> Tandy's portables expose the arrangement as a front-panel memory protect switch that gates the DC-DC converter feeding the memory board while leaving the trickle-charged backup path intact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup>

Parts intended for this duty are specified for it: Samsung KM68-series SRAMs are nominally 5 V devices but retain data down to 2 V, which is what allows a 3 V lithium cell to hold them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup>

## Non-volatile RAM modules

The battery can also be inside the package. Dallas non-volatile RAM and real-time-clock parts are pin-for-pin compatible with an ordinary SRAM of the same size but contain a potted lithium primary cell — not rechargeable — with a guaranteed ten-year factory life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-969" title="EEVblog #969 - Vintage $80k NLE Video Editor Teardown">969</a></sup> Because the cell is internal and encapsulated, its [[voltage]] cannot be measured; the device is simply replaced as preventive maintenance, after which the instrument must be recalibrated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> Failures announce themselves through self-test: a Prema 6047 reports error 8 when the checksum stored in the Dallas SRAM no longer verifies, and the part can be substituted with a standard A6116 2 K by 8 SRAM of the same pinout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> Prema also wrote the original factory [[calibration]] constants into [[eeprom|EEPROM]], so a flat [[battery-backup|backup battery]] does not cost the instrument its calibration — a switch on the rear panel copies the factory values into the replacement SRAM at power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> Working practice with any such instrument is to read the contents out and archive them before the cell fails, along with the program [[rom|ROM]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> Device programmers of the era handle SRAM and non-volatile RAM as distinct families, and the SRAM entries are test routines that write to the part, so reading a battery-backed device intact requires selecting it as non-volatile RAM rather than as generic SRAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup>

## Security keys

Volatility is occasionally the point. Secure microcontrollers built for payment terminals keep encryption keys in SRAM and expose a self-destruct pin, so that breaking the tamper mesh removes the retention supply and the keys evaporate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-687" title="EEVblog #687 - EFTPOS PIN Pad Terminal Teardown">687</a></sup> The same result is achieved discretely: a terminal with two soldered-in cells for redundancy routes their supply through traces that a drill will sever, erasing the key-holding SRAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup> The SRAMs used for this are not always the hardened cryptographic parts that were available; in at least one such design they are ordinary commercial devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup>

## On-chip SRAM

In microcontrollers, SRAM is the working memory quoted alongside flash, and its size largely determines what can be attempted. Figures span from 300 bytes on small PICs — enough for practical C given a good compiler<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-63" title="EEVblog #63 - Microchip PIC vs Atmel AVR">63</a></sup> — through 2 K on a CH32V003 RISC-V part,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1524" title="EEVblog 1524 - The 10 CENT RISC V Processor! CH32V003">1524</a></sup> 8 K on an 8051-cored measurement chip,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup> 16 K on an STM32F103<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup> and on a PIC24F used in a pocket calculator design,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-130" title="EEVblog #130 - The µCalc Credit Card Scientific Calculator / Computer">130</a></sup> 32 K on a Cortex-M0,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> 64 K on a Cortex-M4 tweezer,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1336" title="EEVblog #1336 - DT71 LCR Tweezer Destructive Teardown + Lab Update">1336</a></sup> 96 K in an automotive vision controller,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-444" title="EEVblog #444 - Car Lane Guidance Camera Teardown">444</a></sup> 256 K on a Tiva Connected LaunchPad,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup> and 512 K embedded in a Cypress EZ-[[usb|USB]] FX3 to buffer USB 3.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-592" title="EEVblog #592 - Mailbag">592</a></sup> DSPs carry their own: a TMS320C5504 has 256 K of internal SRAM alongside 128 K of ROM even where external SRAM is also fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup> Modern low-power design leans on the same memory: a [[microcontroller]] in storage mode does nothing but retain its internal SRAM and can do so at around 100 nA while remaining wakeable on interrupt or watchdog.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Selecting a part therefore means budgeting SRAM explicitly against flash rather than taking the largest available, since memory drives package size and price — 64 K SRAM [[lcd|LCD]] controllers are both expensive and physically large, with a 44- or 64-pin QFN as the smallest option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup>

On-chip SRAM is not exempt from silicon errata. The PIC24F series shipped with a long list of documented bugs that included the SRAM, where the reported size was simply wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3" title="EEVblog #3 - Static Myths, PIC Micro, Pocket Multimeter">3</a></sup>

Structurally, SRAM arrays are recognisable on a decapsulated die as large regular blocks of repeated cells with routing running straight over the top of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1380" title="EEVblog 1380 - BBQ IBM Chips">1380</a></sup> At the extreme, a Tesla training tile aggregates 1.3 terabytes of high-speed SRAM against 13 terabytes of high-[[bandwidth]] DRAM at 1.1 exaflops, the same two-tier arrangement that appears on an [[oscilloscope]] board scaled up by six orders of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup>

## Practical notes

SRAM is forgiving of supply decoupling in a way that is easy to underestimate: an entire vintage computer board had every [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] removed except the main input filter, including the ones adjacent to the SRAM, and continued to run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> It is not forgiving of overvoltage. Finding 7 V on the backup-battery SRAM of a Toshiba T1000LE — a 5 V rated part with an absolute maximum in the region of 6 V — identifies a fault serious enough to explain a dead machine, and is the kind of measurement worth taking before chasing the [[power-supply|power supply]] further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> Common vintage part numbers recur often enough to be recognised on sight: the 6116 2 K by 8,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup> the 6264 8 K by 8,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1102" title="EEVblog #1102 - Casio FX730P 1980&#x27;s BASIC Scientific Computer">1102</a></sup> and the 62256 32 K by 8, which was sold in volume as PC cache memory and fitted into sockets beside the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup>
