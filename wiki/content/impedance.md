Impedance, designated by the symbol Z and measured in ohms, is the AC equivalent of [[resistance]]: it is the ratio of AC [[voltage]] to AC [[current]] and can be thought of as "AC resistance," with the familiar Ohm's law relation Z = V/I still applying.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Unlike DC resistance, impedance is a complex quantity combining a real (resistive) component and an imaginary (reactive) component, the imaginary part representing the phase relationship between voltage and current across the element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1470" title="EEVblog 1470 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 3 - Complex Numbers are EASY!">1470</a></sup>

## Definition and complex form

Impedance is defined as Z = R + jX, where R is the real resistive component and X is the reactance, the imaginary reactive component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Because reactance can be inductive (+jX_L) or capacitive (−jX_C), the sign of the imaginary part depends on whether the circuit is dominated by [[inductance]] or [[capacitance]], and both X_L and X_C are expressed in ohms so they add directly to resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Impedance can be written in rectangular form (real plus imaginary) or polar form (magnitude and [[phase-angle|phase angle]]), the two representations being interchangeable; polar form is preferred when multiplying or dividing, for instance when calculating current as I = V/Z.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1661" title="EEVblog 1661 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 5: Time Domain vs Frequency Domain">1661</a></sup>

The AC form of Ohm's law replaces R with Z: V = I·Z, where V, I, and Z are all vectors carrying real and imaginary components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1469" title="EEVblog 1469 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 2 - Phasors">1469</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup> Solving AC circuits is otherwise identical to DC analysis — Kirchhoff's laws, nodal and mesh analysis, Thevenin and Norton equivalents, and superposition all work unchanged once impedances replace resistances.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup> Series impedances sum directly (Z_T = Z1 + Z2 + Z3), and parallel impedances combine reciprocally, exactly as resistors do in DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup>

## Impedance versus reactance

Impedance and reactance are distinct terms even though both are measured in ohms. Reactance is purely the complex, [[frequency]]-dependent part — the inductive or capacitive opposition alone, with no resistive element — whereas impedance encompasses both the real resistance and the imaginary reactance of a complete circuit or component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1661" title="EEVblog 1661 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 5: Time Domain vs Frequency Domain">1661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> For a physical [[capacitor]], for example, the [[capacitive-reactance|capacitive reactance]] X_C = 1/(2πfC) is the contribution of the capacitance itself, while the total impedance adds the [[equivalent-series-resistance|equivalent series resistance]] (ESR) and the lead/package inductance at the frequency of interest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> The terms are often used interchangeably in casual speech, but the distinction matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup>

Impedance should also not be confused with the characteristic impedance of a [[transmission-line|transmission line]]: both are expressed in ohms, but a component's impedance changes with every incremental change in frequency, whereas the distributed characteristic impedance of a transmission line is essentially constant with frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup>

## Related quantities

Admittance, symbol Y, is the reciprocal of impedance, Y = 1/Z, measured in siemens, and decomposes as Y = G + jB, where the real part G is conductance (the inverse of resistance) and the imaginary part B is susceptance (the inverse of reactance).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Impedance is by far the quantity used in everyday practice; conductance finds use in fields such as electrochemistry, while susceptance and admittance appear mainly in more complex circuit analysis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> In AC power calculations, [[apparent-power|apparent power]] uses impedance rather than resistance: S = I²Z and S = V²/Z, since Z includes both the real resistive and imaginary reactive components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

## Frequency behaviour of components

Capacitors and inductors have no fixed resistance; instead they exhibit a frequency-dependent reactance, with capacitive reactance falling as frequency rises and inductive reactance rising with frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> The total impedance of a real capacitor is the sum of its ESR, its capacitive reactance, and its inductive reactance at the frequency of interest, producing the characteristic V-shaped impedance-versus-frequency curve with a minimum at self-resonance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> Electrolytic ESR is conventionally specified at 100 kHz because the capacitor is essentially resistive around that frequency; above resonance, package inductance dominates and impedance climbs again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> Capacitors of different values and packages have different impedance characteristics, which is why bypass networks commonly place multiple values — for example 10 µF, 1 µF, and 0.1 µF — in parallel near a chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> Care is needed with many parallel values, since combinations can create a resonant impedance peak at some [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] and destabilise the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup>

## Measurement

An [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] fundamentally measures impedance: it drives the device under test through a range [[resistor]] at a selectable test frequency (for example 100 Hz, 120 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz) and derives impedance from the voltage-to-current ratio; every other parameter — capacitance, inductance, quality factor, dissipation factor — is then calculated mathematically from the measured impedance, [[via]] relationships such as |Z| = √(R_S² + X_S²).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> Dedicated impedance measurement can also be integrated into a circuit: the Analog Devices AD5933 combines a DDS [[signal-generator|signal generator]], a 12-bit 1 MS/s ADC, and DSP performing Fourier transforms to report impedance over an I²C interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup>

Measuring capacitors in circuit works surprisingly often, but surrounding circuitry with very low impedance swamps the measurement — readings become meaningless when the parallel impedance around the part falls to tens of ohms, and below roughly 10 ohms in-circuit measurement fails entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup>

## Practical consequences in circuits

Currents take the lowest-impedance path through a circuit and back to the source; at high frequencies this is the path of lowest impedance, not lowest DC resistance, which is why [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] placement and [[ground]] return routing govern where switching currents actually flow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> A low-impedance, low-inductance [[ground-plane|ground plane]] — often implemented as a flood-filled plane — is the default good practice, with star-point [[grounding]] reserved for cases with a specific justification; a solid plane behaves as a distributed capacitor with very low impedance and is essential for fast, high-current logic such as ECL, which will not work on a two-layer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup> Power traces are likewise made fatter than signal traces specifically to lower their impedance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

Termination matters once cables behave as transmission lines. An unterminated [[coaxial-cable|coax]] run can produce drastic amplitude and phase variations with frequency — at around 35 MHz the phase between two similar cables can even swap over — because reflections interact with the cable's impedance and length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> The standard RF system impedance is 50 ohms, as used in dummy loads and terminations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1003" title="EEVblog #1003 - Mailbag">1003</a></sup> Instrument [[input-impedance|input impedance]] also loads the circuit under test: a 10 MΩ [[multimeter]] input can significantly disturb a precision [[voltage-divider|voltage divider]] even when it is orders of magnitude larger than the circuit impedance being measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> A rough rule of thumb for back-of-envelope work is that any parallel element an order of magnitude larger than the relevant impedance can be ignored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup>

Output versus input impedance determines [[signal-integrity|signal integrity]] in probing and coupling: a low-impedance output will not develop significant amplitude from stray [[capacitive-coupling|capacitive coupling]], whereas a high-impedance input node can show hundreds of millivolts of coupled signal from a nearby trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> Conversely, a high-impedance node loaded by any significant impedance is swamped, while driving it from a low-impedance output — or shorting the input — suppresses the effect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - PART 2: Piezoelectric Oscilloscope Issue Followup">983</a></sup> Fault-finding exploits the same principle: an unexpected 1 kΩ path to [[mains-earth|mains earth]] is classified as quite low impedance and a hazard when probing, since it is far from an open circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Ferrite chip beads are characterised by their impedance at 100 MHz for filtering applications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup> Outside electronics proper, transthoracic impedance — the human body's opposition to current flow, varying from about 25 to 180 ohms depending on body mass, skin resistance, and tissue — is a determining factor in successful defibrillation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1008" title="EEVblog #1008 - Mystery Teardown">1008</a></sup>
