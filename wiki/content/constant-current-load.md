A constant current load is a load that draws a fixed [[current]] from a source regardless of the source's terminal [[voltage]], in contrast to a [[resistor]], whose current falls as the voltage across it falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> It is the default load type used to characterise batteries and to test power supplies, because holding current fixed removes one variable from the measurement and reduces capacity accounting to current multiplied by time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> In instrument form it is normally realised as a low-side current sink: a power [[mosfet|MOSFET]] regulated by an op-amp against the voltage developed across a sense resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup>

## Circuit principle

The canonical implementation is an op-amp driving the gate of a MOSFET, with the source returned to [[ground]] through a sense resistor and the op-amp's inverting input tapped off that resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> The op-amp forces the sense-resistor voltage to equal the control voltage on its non-inverting input, so with a 1 ohm sense resistor a 1 V input sets 1 A of load current, giving an adjustable constant current load referred directly to an input voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> The whole thing needs only a MOSFET, an op-amp, and some means of setting and reading that voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> Because the current is set by feedback rather than by [[resistance]], the load is an active element, not a resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

Although the term speaks of a load, the same topology is a constant current sink, and it can be thought of as a resistor from the perspective of the source under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> High-side constant current sources and low-side sinks are two arrangements of the same idea.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

Scaling up is a matter of paralleling pass devices. A commercial source measure unit uses four FQB8N60C MOSFETs — 600 V, 12 A, 1.2 ohm on-resistance parts — with drains commoned to the tab and each device given its own [[current-shunt|current shunt]], so that each dissipates a quarter of the total power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Range switching is done by selecting among sense resistors, including a 1 ohm resistor and two separate 1.0 ohm resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>

Adding a [[microcontroller]] converts a fixed-function current sink into a general programmable load. A [[pwm|PWM]] output generating the control voltage, in place of the manual pot, plus an ADC reading the sense-resistor voltage, allows constant current, constant power, constant resistance, or arbitrary pulse profiles to be synthesised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> Without that intelligence the same hardware is limited to constant current alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> Off-the-shelf hardware is sometimes repurposed this way: a hobby [[battery]] charger with custom [[firmware]] can be turned into a modest constant current, constant power, constant resistance dummy load, limited mainly by how much heat its pass element and heatsinking can shed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup>

## Why linear-regulated products present a constant current load

A product powered through a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] is, seen from the battery, close to a pure constant current load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> The reasoning is a basic property of linear regulators: essentially no current flows in the adjust or set pin, so the input current equals the [[output-current|output current]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> The regulator holds a fixed voltage across a load whose behaviour is steady on average — even a complex load such as a [[processor]] running the same task — so a fixed voltage produces a fixed current, and that fixed current is pulled from the battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> A [[multimeter]] drawing roughly 5 mA is a worked example: 5 mA at the load means 5 mA out of the battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Raising the supply voltage does not change it; the draw stays at 5 mA because the load is constant current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> The regulator's [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]] adds to this, but for a milliamp-region load a microamp-region IQ can be neglected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup>

Switching regulators behave completely differently, and any load that changes state differently again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> A switch-mode front end draws roughly constant power, so its input current rises as the [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

## Battery capacity and the load-type problem

Battery data sheets typically assume a constant current discharge, which is why capacity is so often quoted in amp hours or milliamp hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> That specification is not strictly correct, because it ignores the voltage change over the discharge and assumes the load is constant current when not all loads are.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Under those assumptions the measurement captures an area under the discharge curve that includes some region it should not and excludes some it should, the two errors partially cancelling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> True capacity is the full area under the discharge curve and is properly expressed in watt hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup>

Three load types appear on battery data sheets: constant current, which is the most common; constant resistance, tested literally by strapping a 10 ohm or 100 ohm resistor across the cell; and constant power, which is the most representative of most designed products and underlies the watt-hour rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Few real circuits behave as constant resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Data sheets may additionally give pulse-load curves to simulate something like a toy being switched on and off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup>

The practical consequence is that a constant current capacity figure is accurate but narrow. Measuring an AA cell at 250 mA constant current for about 7.5 hours yields 1875 mAh, a number known accurately for a 250 mA constant current load and largely inapplicable to a product that does not draw 250 mA constant current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> The most accurate approach is to log the discharge curve using the real product, which may switch between modes and so present neither constant current nor constant power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Where a [[datasheet|data sheet]] lacks a curve at the current of interest — a 9 V alkaline characterised at other currents but not at 5 mA — the value must be interpolated or measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup>

Measuring amp hours needs only a constant current load, a stopwatch, and a multimeter across the battery to catch the [[cutoff-voltage|cutoff voltage]]; measuring watt hours is harder, requiring a data logger that captures both voltage and current over time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> Discharge curves are also better understood in terms of loaded terminal voltage: the curve a data sheet gives for a constant power, constant current, or constant resistance drain is the loaded voltage, quite distinct from the open-circuit voltage seen by pulling the cell out and metering it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

## Bench practice

Electronic loads used for battery work generally provide a cutoff: a constant current is drawn until the terminal voltage reaches a set threshold, at which point the test stops automatically, protecting the cell from over-discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup> A capacity-based stop is the complementary mechanism — set a stop capacity of 1.25 Ah at 100 mA and the load ends the run once half a nominal 2500 mAh alkaline has been removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1274" title="EEVblog #1274 - Long Term Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing">1274</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j_eaXfmRB8Q" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing - Part 1">•</a></sup> Discharge data is typically plotted against capacity on the x-axis, though time is often the more useful axis and can be recovered by exporting the data; a lithium cell test giving 59 minutes 11 seconds of depletion from 4.2 V corresponded to almost exactly 5 Ah.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup>

Applying a constant current load also exposes internal resistance directly. Switching a half-amp load onto a fresh 1.58 V alkaline drops the terminal voltage immediately, the step being the drop across the cell's ESR; removing the load lets the voltage recover, but that recovery represents chemistry and ESR rather than recovered energy, and reapplying the load collapses it again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> A nominally flat cell sitting at 0.56 V can still deliver a continuous 10 mA under constant current, whereas a constant power setting on the same load will not start, because constant power requires the instrument to compute and compensate while constant current is handled more directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Not every load offers both: some battery test modes support constant current only, forcing constant power tests to be run outside the built-in mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Some instruments do support constant power in battery mode, which better represents modern DC-DC-fronted products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IXVTMUQGN5U" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery Capacity Test #2">•</a></sup>

Cell chemistry and cell size limit how hard a constant current test can be pushed. A AAA is not designed for 1 A constant current, and a 1 A discharge of a good alkaline AAA returns only about 263 mAh or 0.268 Wh.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

Anomalous readings during long discharges are worth chasing rather than accepting: a string reading 0 V at the pack terminals while the load still reports tens of milliamps flowing, or a load delivering 50 mA when set to 100 mA, indicates a connection or contact problem rather than a battery result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup> [[voltage-drop|Voltage drop]] in the test leads matters too, and remote sensing may be required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup>

For long-term storage or leakage experiments, a single small constant current load — on the order of 10 microamps — placed across a whole series string is a practical substitute for [[soldering]] individual resistors to each cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RFb3TwWzza0" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Test Setup">•</a></sup> An earlier leakage attempt using a fixed 1K resistive load across cells produced no leakage in any brand tested, and no strong evidence existed as to which load condition best provokes leakage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup>

## Testing power supplies

For supply characterisation the constant current load is the standard instrument. [[ripple|Ripple]] and [[noise]] rise with load, so measurement is done at maximum rated output current or at the intended operating current — a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] tested into 2 A, for example.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Load-step and turn-on behaviour is captured by switching a set current onto the output and single-shot capturing the transient: a 10 V output into 3 A showed no [[overshoot]] at all;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> a 15 V output into 5 A produced a visible kink in the rising edge without overshooting;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup> a 2000 W supply started into a 19.9 A constant current load exhibited a step lasting around 150 ms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Electronic loads are dynamic and some supplies cannot start into them at all, which is why a plain resistive load is sometimes used for the startup comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Because a constant current load and a purely resistive load do not stress a regulator identically, comparing the two is a legitimate diagnostic step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup>

The same load doubles as a current-reading cross-check: a 1 A constant current load read as 1.008 A on one instrument and 0.999 A on another indicates agreement within specification,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> whereas a 0.6 percent discrepancy on a 1 A setting was traced to the load being left on its 40 A range, and ranges on such loads cannot be changed with the load switched on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

A constant current load is also a fault-finding tool. Applying a modest 0.1 A load to a suspect bench supply channel establishes whether the pass element can source any current at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup> Applying 50 mA to a device battery and watching the terminal voltage collapse to 3.03 V identifies a cell that cannot sustain even that draw,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1370" title="EEVblog #1370 - Kindle Paperwhite REPAIR">1370</a></sup> as does a pack that goes completely dead at a 200 mA draw.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XpZVIWdXliY" title="Fluke OneTouch Network Tester Battery Restoration Attempt">•</a></sup> Recovery from [[short-circuit|short circuit]] can be characterised by switching a regulator from a short back to a defined constant current — 250 mA, then 50 mA — and observing whether it re-establishes regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

Beyond supplies, the constant current load serves as a controlled sink wherever a defined current is needed: two of them in parallel across a rectifier output to develop a comparable ripple voltage on two different 100 microfarad [[capacitor]] arrangements,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> a 1 A load at 5 V as a clean example of 5 W instantaneous power in an energy-versus-power demonstration,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> and a 1 A current sink built from the same dummy-load circuit when a precision [[current-source|current source]] was needed and a ground-referenced output was not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> It is also the natural bench test for a mains-side or alternator-side unit rated for a continuous 4 A output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-720" title="EEVblog #720 - Mailbag">720</a></sup>

[[efficiency|Efficiency]] characterisation is the case that most rewards automation: with an LXI-capable supply and load on the network, a script can set the input voltage, read back input current and hence input power, command the load's constant current setpoint, and sweep it, gathering points at the load's current resolution — around 1 mA — across the full range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> Simulation is a further step removed; a constant current load can be swept over temperature in SPICE to expose the tempco of the arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup>

## Practical judgement

The DIY constant current dummy load is regarded as an instrument every bench should have, and the circuit is trivial enough to be built from junk-box parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> Where more capable hardware is available, a precision [[electronic-load|electronic load]] with programmable setpoint and readback of output power and voltage is preferred over cobbled-together arrangements, particularly at higher currents such as 8 A where lead resistance becomes significant enough to need attention.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

On the specific question of how much energy a constant current cutoff leaves in an [[alkaline-battery|alkaline cell]], the difference between constant current and constant power framing is small: with a cutoff around 1 to 1.1 V, roughly 10 to 20 percent of the battery's energy is left stranded as an absolute best case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> A claim of eight times the usable life depends on the product being poorly designed, with a loaded cutoff as high as 1.4 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> A separate claim — that because a cell takes longer to fall from 1.2 V to 1.1 V than from 1.5 V to 1.4 V under a constant current draw, the recoverable life must be larger still — is true as an observation about the slope of the discharge curve but does not carry the conclusion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>
