Ringing is the damped [[oscillation]] that appears on the edges of a fast signal when parasitic [[inductance]] in the [[current]] path resonates with the [[capacitance]] around it, typically riding on top of [[overshoot]] at a rising edge and undershoot at a falling edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> It is the visible symptom of a [[signal-integrity|signal integrity]] problem rather than a problem in itself: the same parasitics that produce it also produce [[ground]] bounce, broadband radiated emissions, and false logic transitions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup> Because it lives entirely in the high-[[frequency]] content of an edge, ringing has nothing to do with the nominal frequency of the signal — a slow [[square-wave|square wave]] with a fast [[slew-rate|slew rate]] rings just as readily as a fast one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Origin

Any current loop with inductance and capacitance in it has a resonance, and a step of current excites it. The inductance need not be a component: bond wires inside a package contribute inductance between the die and the pins, and that alone is enough to put ringing on a signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> On a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] the contributors are trace inductance, loop inductance, and the position of the source and load relative to each other — the loop area of the path the transient current has to take.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> The rule that governs all of it is blunt: "Any piece of wire of any length is an [[inductor]]."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

In switching converters the same mechanism produces high-frequency [[noise]]: parasitic inductances distributed through the layout ring when subjected to large rates of change of current during the charging and discharging of the storage element. Because these parasitics are of much lower inductance than the intentional ones, they ring and generate noise at correspondingly higher frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

Ringing on a [[power-rail|power rail]] is fast. Probed at 10 ns per division, the transients on a rail can carry content in the hundreds of megahertz, and the frequency follows the slew rate of the driving circuit — even ordinary [[microcontroller]] switching gets there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> Where the rail is probed matters as much as what is on it, since the observed ringing depends on the decoupling present at that point and on the inductance of the loop between the point of measurement and the source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> Physical layout of the circuit under test can dominate: a switching board held down against a ground surface rings visibly more than the same board raised on a stand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

## Consequences

The most direct failure is in logic. A clock line with enough ringing on it re-enters the indeterminate input region — for a 74HC-family part, the band between 1.5 V and 3.15 V — and if the excursion dwells there, the receiving gate can register multiple clock pulses from a single edge, with metastability in downstream flip-flops as a further consequence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup> Logic datasheets state the requirement plainly: routing and load conditions must be considered to prevent ringing, which is a statement about [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]] and loading rather than about the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> The risk is worst where drive is strong and the load is light, since high drive creates fast edges into light loads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

The second consequence is emissions. Ringing on a poorly bypassed rail appears in the frequency domain as broad, high-amplitude content across a wide band; adding bypass capacitors knocks it down markedly between roughly 200 MHz and 500 MHz, and removing them brings it straight back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> A circuit radiating that much broadband energy is unlikely to pass CE or FCC emissions compliance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup>

The third is rail integrity. Ringing driven by insufficient bypassing on a 5 V supply can amount to a substantial fraction of a 100 mV per division trace, which is a large amount of [[ripple]] to place on a supply feeding digital logic, with knock-on glitches and ground bounce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> On a board with several chips and inadequate bulk capacitance, the supply [[impedance]] resonates and the result is large ringing rather than a small droop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

## Mitigation

Bypassing is the primary control. A local [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] placed as close to the pins as the package allows cleans up an edge that otherwise rings and undershoots, and a supply that rings on a poorly bypassed [[oscillator]] output can be made to look like a commercially built part by adding one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> The limit is the inductance that cannot be removed — the bond wires inside the package, and the leads and series inductance introduced when the [[capacitor]] is not mounted directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Adding leads and series inductors between the capacitor and the pin complicates the picture and undoes part of the benefit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup>

Splitting bulk and high-frequency decoupling between two capacitors of different value is a long-standing practice justified by the overlaid impedance curves giving a broader, lower-impedance path across frequency, and controlling ESR is one of the ways ringing is deliberately damped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Low-inductance capacitor constructions change the calculus, potentially allowing a bulk capacitor plus one low-inductance part in place of a conventional pair; three-terminal feedthrough capacitors shift it further still, since such a part can offer 10 µF at very low inductance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup>

Termination is the other control. An unterminated line rings on every edge; a length of [[coaxial-cable|coax]] driven without proper termination shows clear overshoot and ringing that a matched termination removes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-381" title="EEVblog #381 - How Do Ultrasonic Delay Lines Work?">381</a></sup> Even with a nominal 50 Ω termination, small mismatches remain visible — around 55 Ω against 50 Ω is enough to put noticeable ringing on the input of a fast digitiser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> Whether the termination is a separate 50 Ω through-terminator or an in-line one can make a measurable difference at high frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup> For an existing supply already built, ferrite beads placed across switching components such as the [[mosfet|MOSFET]] are one route to taking the edge off ringing already present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup>

Construction technique matters as much as component selection at the extremes. A pulse generator built for the lowest possible inductance and plugged directly into the [[oscilloscope]] input, with no coaxial cable at all, produces almost no ringing; the same class of pulse routed through a long coax picks up ringing attributable to the cable's inductance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> A coaxial line works as a [[transmission-line|transmission line]], but only within limits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup>

## Measurement artefact versus real ringing

A large share of observed ringing is created by the probe, not by the circuit. The ground lead supplied with a standard passive probe is a loop of wire and therefore an inductor, and it will put ringing on the displayed [[waveform]] that is not present in the circuit; removing it is the first step in any signal integrity measurement, ahead of everything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The same lead is what defeats high-frequency [[probe-compensation|probe compensation]]: after adjusting the low-frequency trim at the probe tip and the high-frequency trim at the [[connector]] end, ringing remains on the displayed step until the long earth lead is replaced with a short ground spring or hook.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Traces annotated as showing ringing "due to our [[antenna]] earth lead", or ringing attributed to a long ground lead on an active FET probe, are of this class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup>

The distinction is testable. When high-fidelity probing technique is used and the probe's own contribution is negligible, the ringing and undershoot on a clock edge are real events in the circuit and should be treated as such.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> Conversely, ringing seen with a [[bandwidth]] limiter engaged and casual probing on a retro machine's [[processor]] clock is not evidence about the clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-788" title="EEVblog #788 - Apple IIC Teardown">788</a></sup> Probe quality itself shows up the same way: a benchmark step at 5 ns per division through a good probe shows a little overshoot and a little ringing before settling, and a probe under test is judged against that reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup>

Oscilloscope front ends have their own pulse response. A 100 MHz instrument fed a roughly 700 ps pulse through an in-line 50 Ω termination can be distinctly ringy, with pronounced overshoot and undershoot, while another instrument on the same signal is clean.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> Some scopes show an almost ringy response to a step from a [[signal-generator|signal generator]] where others show only mild overshoot, so the signal source must be accounted for before blaming the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup> Turning off a [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]] is what reveals the overshoot, ringing, and transmission-line and termination effects that were being hidden.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup> Ringing severe enough to make a waveform untriggerable forces the trigger to be taken from a cleaner source elsewhere in the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

## Ringing as a diagnostic

Because ringing frequency is set by the resonance of a real physical loop, it carries information. Cursor measurement from peak to peak on the higher-frequency content within a ringing burst yields a specific frequency — on one oscillator output, about 29.4 MHz — distinguishing deliberate high-frequency components in the waveform from the settling tail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Ringing on a rail captured in single shot is sinusoidal in character and, correlated on a mixed-signal instrument against the digital lines, can be tied back to the switching transient that excited it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup> Changes in ringing on the coils of a driven load track loading conditions, the loaded case showing ringing on both the rising and falling edges with flattened top and bottom compared with the unloaded case at just over 13 V peak to peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup>

Ringing also appears deliberately as a test stimulus: a ringing clock is one of the standard training and demonstration waveforms built into oscilloscopes alongside sine with glitch, RF burst, and burst with infrequent glitch, precisely because it is a realistic hard case for triggering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>

## Judgement

Some ringing is unavoidable and tolerable; the engineering question is how much and where. On a linear supply's control loop, a small amount of ring and undershoot on the op-amp output as the circuit hits a limit, with a clean overall switch-on, is acceptable, though a long decaying ring on the op-amp is not desirable even when the output itself is well behaved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> High-frequency content on a supply rail traced to probing rather than to the circuit still points at something worth eliminating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> On a fast pulse output, ringing following overshoot degrades an otherwise respectable 250 ps average [[rise-time|rise time]], and imperfect signal integrity of that kind — a dip before the edge and undershoot and ringing at the tail — is usually traceable to the interconnect, a coaxial cable or a socket-type connector, rather than to the source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-316" title="EEVblog #316 - More PFANG, More 13GHz Scope, &amp; More Pulser">316</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup>

At high [[voltage]] and fast switching the tolerance narrows sharply. Winding up the switching voltage on a wide-bandgap experiment makes the ringing on the switch node progressively and severely worse, to the point of being a major problem rather than a cosmetic one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>
