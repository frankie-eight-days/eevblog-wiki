A PCB footprint is the copper land pattern, [[solder-mask|solder mask]] openings, hole sizes and [[silkscreen]] outline that a component physically occupies on a board, held in a CAD library and linked to the [[schematic]] symbol that represents that part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> It is the point where an abstract netlist becomes a manufacturable object: the footprint determines whether the part can be soldered, whether it can be tested, whether the traces around it can be routed, and whether the assembled board works at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Getting a footprint wrong is one of the most common and most expensive prototype errors, because unlike a schematic mistake it cannot be patched without physically abusing the part or spinning the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup>

## Land pattern standards and pad sizing

The IPC publishes standard land patterns, and they are issued in three density levels rather than as a single recommended geometry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> The three sizes are described as normal, least, and most pad size, and the choice between them is driven by the density of the board — a dense layout takes the smaller pads, a sparse one can afford the larger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> The difference is essentially how far the pad overhangs the end cap of the component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

For boards that will be hand soldered, the larger pad options are preferable, because they leave exposed copper beyond the component body where a chisel tip can make thermal contact with the pad and the lead simultaneously; with [[flux]], the joint then forms on its own through surface tension.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> The same extended pad doubles as a flying probe test point, which makes generous pads worth the board area on any design headed for flying probe test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> There is a limit: pads long enough to suit a bare prototype board are longer than is sensible in a production footprint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup>

[[solder-mask-expansion|Solder mask expansion]] between adjacent pads is part of the footprint, not an afterthought. A pad that is too wide leaves too little mask between pins, the mask breaks through, and [[solder]] bridges during [[hand-soldering|hand soldering]] or [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]]; a gap of four or five thou of mask between pads is a workable target, and narrowing the pad is the usual fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

Where a manufacturer publishes a recommended pattern — tactile domes and similar mechanical parts are typical — that pattern should be used as given.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1291" title="EEVblog #1291 - Mailbag">1291</a></sup> The patterns are fairly tolerant of small deviation, but departing from them without a specific reason is not worth the risk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1291" title="EEVblog #1291 - Mailbag">1291</a></sup> Recommended footprints are also printed in device datasheets alongside the package dimensions, and those dimensions are exactly the inputs an IPC footprint wizard in a layout package asks for when generating the land pattern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> A correctly followed manufacturer footprint shows up in the reflow result: a [[battery]] holder pad barely larger than the tab itself still reflows cleanly on the edges at a peak of just over 230 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup>

## Library discipline and failure modes

Having every schematic component carry a correct, verified footprint before layout begins is the first step of a board design, and time spent sorting the libraries out beforehand is recovered during layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> A board built entirely from existing, proven parts goes much faster than one that needs new footprints, precisely because the footprints are already known good.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Creating a footprint from scratch is not the bottleneck — a schematic symbol and a land pattern each take a minute or two, and dimensions can be reconstructed from photographs and partial data when a mechanical drawing is incomplete.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup>

Pinout errors are the classic footprint failure, and they are worse than a reversed silkscreen because they cannot be worked around by orientation alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Transistors are the worst offenders: "Always double-check your [[transistor]] footprints" is a standing rule, since the same three-lead package is issued with different pinouts by different manufacturers and part types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup> Recovery from a wrong three-pin footprint means rotating the part on the pads, flipping it upside down and bending the leads, or some combination — workable on a rush prototype, but it must be repeated on every affected device on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup>

Dimensional errors are subtler and scale with package size. A footprint whose [[pin-pitch|pin pitch]] is slightly off from the specified 0.6 mm places the first pins acceptably and then accumulates error along the row, until the outer pins sit visibly off-centre; on a small part it is survivable, on a large chip it becomes a real assembly problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Because errors of this kind are invisible in the schematic, checking a suspect footprint against the [[datasheet]] mid-layout is routine practice, and doubling as a pass to consolidate hole sizes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

Jellybean parts warrant the opposite of minimalism in the library: a general-purpose transistor such as a 2N3904 is available in DFN, SC-70, SOT-23, SOT-223 and TO-92 among others, down to packages of roughly 0.6 mm by 0.8 mm, and building the same part into the library in all of those footprints means a design constraint on size never forces a change of part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup>

## Footprints as a manufacturing variable

The footprint set is a design-for-manufacture lever in its own right, distinct from consolidating the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] or substituting cheaper parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Consolidating footprints — reducing the number of distinct land patterns a board uses — matters because the pick-and-place machine, not the schematic, pays the cost, and ignoring this can make a device more expensive to build than it needs to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The specific case is package size taken too far: 0201 capacitors are half the size of an already small 0402, and specifying 0201 for a 1 µF part with no space pressure to justify it is a poor trade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

The footprint name is carried through to the assembler in the pick-and-place file, alongside the reference designator and the component's X-Y location relative to a datum that is usually but not necessarily the bottom left corner of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> The file can be CSV or effectively any format the manufacturer accepts, and it is supplied together with the overlay diagram showing where each designator goes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup>

Company-standard footprints and approved spacings, enforced through DRC constraints, are a real force on layout at established firms, and constraints carried forward from a decade earlier can still be shaping boards being designed today.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> In contract layout work, the schematics handed over generally include most footprints already, sometimes with alternatives for the layout engineer to consider, along with mechanical requirements and placement information for connectors and critical parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup>

## Placement, routing and optional footprints

Footprint geometry constrains routing directly. Where four traces must pass between unused pads and a 15 thou clearance rule leaves no room, the options are to relax the clearance or to edit the footprint — narrowing the pads vertically, or converting rectangular pads to rectangles with rounded corners, to open the channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Modifying the land pattern to serve the route is a normal part of layout rather than a last resort.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

Pad size also affects serviceability. Large footprints on a board that is not densely populated make it easy to get a probe onto a node, which is a deliberate advantage of not shrinking packages further than the layout requires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> Board space is finite, though, and a discrete [[crystal-oscillator|crystal oscillator]] circuit costs three components' worth of area plus the clearance a high-[[impedance]] node needs around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup>

Footprints need not be populated at all. Laying down multiple [[capacitor]] footprints and fitting only as many as a given model requires lets one [[pcb|PCB]] serve a 50 W and a 100 W variant of the same product, with one or two capacitors in the low-power build and four in the high-power one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Unpopulated footprints are also a visible tell in commercial products: an empty footprint identical to a fitted one next to it, positioned behind an unused front-panel cutout, indicates a planned option that the particular model does not carry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> Kits and collectible boards are sometimes shipped with the footprints bare and the parts left to the builder to source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup>

Where two boards share an identical footprint and mounting hole pattern, mechanical options follow: the same board can be mounted to the bottom of an enclosure or flipped and mounted to the top, and stacked assemblies become possible without a second layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-258" title="EEVblog #258 - PSU Housing Design - Part 11">258</a></sup>

## Footprints in CAD packages

The mechanism for assigning a footprint to a schematic symbol differs sharply between packages and is a frequent source of friction. In Altium the footprint is a model attached to the component, and building a custom part — schematic symbol, footprint, simulation model, 3D model — is done from a single panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-754" title="EEVblog #754 - Altium Circuit Maker First Impressions">754</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> KiCad historically split the job across a separate association program that read the netlist and matched footprints to symbols after the schematic was finished, rather than offering a footprint picker inside the schematic editor itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup> The expectation from an Altium background is a button in the schematic that opens the PCB library, chooses the land pattern and pushes it through to the board; typing a footprint name into a text field is a poor substitute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup>

Terminology compounds the problem: KiCad at the time called a footprint a module in some dialogs and a footprint in others, including a footprint editor labelled the module editor, on the grounds that "nobody calls a PCB footprint a module".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup> Filtered footprint lists — offering only the plausible patterns for a capacitor or a DB9 [[connector]] — are useful to a beginner, and the full unfiltered list remains reachable, but an unfiltered list can run to hundreds of entries for a single 8-pin DIP.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup> Automatic footprint association exists and may serve as a decent first pass, though automated assignment in a CAD program invites distrust.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup> Missing library entries surface on netlist import as footprints not found, which for demonstration projects shipping with the tool is a poor first impression.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup>

Other tool behaviours around footprints include selection filters that let footprints be excluded while tracks and vias are retained, which is what makes bulk layer swapping on a four-layer board possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> Some open-source layout editors can generate footprints on the fly from a scripting language or a dynamically generated element definition, though the scripting support is weaker on Windows than on Linux.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-125" title="EEVblog #125 - Soup to Nuts with Free Software">125</a></sup> Data-exchange viewers built around standards such as IPC-2581 expose footprints as a browsable category alongside components, highlighting every instance where a given pattern appears, in the manner of a CAD system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-349" title="EEVblog #349 - SMCBA Lecture IPC-2581 Open Standards for PCB Design Data">349</a></sup> Board-level tools also vary in how they carry footprint metadata; an Eagle design may hold device footprints without any link to distributor part numbers, which limits how much of a BOM can be recovered from the file.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup>
