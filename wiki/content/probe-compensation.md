Probe compensation is the adjustment of a passive [[oscilloscope-probe|oscilloscope probe]]'s trimmer [[capacitor]] so that the probe's divider stays flat across [[frequency]] when connected to a particular [[oscilloscope]] input. It is performed by hooking the probe to the scope's compensation output — a [[square-wave|square wave]], conventionally 1 kHz — and trimming until the displayed edge is square rather than rounded or overshooting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> An uncompensated probe misreports edges, and on a scope or probe with no adjustment available at all the instrument becomes unusable for high-frequency detail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup>

## The adjustment

Compensation applies to the times 10 setting only: the trimmer works against the 9 megaohm [[series-resistor|series resistor]] that forms the divider in times 10 mode, so nothing happens in times 1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> The probe is hooked to the compensation output and trimmed with a small adjustment tool until the 1 kHz square wave is square.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

The trimmer's range is a specified quantity. One 10:1 probe specifies a compensation range of 10 to 35 pF, whose midpoint of 22.5 pF is the value used to represent the compensated probe in simulation; a series resistor of around 68 ohms is also typical in that network, though its exact value has very little effect on the response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> Because the range is finite, unusually high scope [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]] is a real concern: a 1 megaohm input at 24 pF, against the 14 pF of an earlier generation of the same family, raises the question of whether the probes supplied with it can still be brought into range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup>

Compensation is specific to the scope input, not to the probe alone. Different oscilloscopes have different input capacitance, so each probe must be re-compensated on each scope it is moved to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup>

## Where the trimmer lives

Passive probes come in two styles: the compensation adjustment sits at the probe head, or it sits at the [[connector]] end where the cable plugs into the BNC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> Both do the same job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> Higher-[[bandwidth]] probes are most commonly compensated at the connector end rather than at the tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> Within a single vendor's range the two conventions can sit side by side — of two Rigol probes with essentially the same input capacitance, the RP2200 is compensated at the probe tip and the RP3300 at the end of the cable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup> The 500 MHz probes shipped with a 1 GHz scope are likewise end-compensated, times 10 only, which is what a passive probe at that bandwidth is expected to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup>

Some probes carry both a low-frequency adjustment on the probe body and a separate high-frequency compensation at the cable's connector end, adjusted in sequence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

Moving all the compensation to the termination end rather than the probe head is what allows some hands-free probe systems to be usable at times 1: with the adjustment made at the termination, there is nothing left to trim at the tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup>

## Automatic compensation

Probes with an identification interface — a one-wire chip in the head that reports a serial number — dispense with trimmer caps entirely. There are no compensation adjustments at either the tip or the scope end; the probe is hooked to the [[calibration]] port and an auto-calibration routine is run, and the scope stores the resulting compensation against that probe's serial number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> The result is that the same probe moved to another channel is recognised as not yet compensated for that channel, because the stored setting is per channel as well as per probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

Active probes are not exempt. They still have to be compensated; the correction is simply stored internally against the probe's serial number rather than dialled in by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>

A scope may also report whether a connected probe needs alignment at all, and allow the adjustment to be made live with the [[waveform]] on screen while a screwdriver turns the trimmer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup>

## Symptoms of poor compensation

The visible signature of a mis-set trimmer is a square wave that is not square: rounded corners when undercompensated, [[overshoot]] and [[ringing]] when overcompensated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup> Overshoot on a step is the expected consequence of using a probe on a scope it has not been compensated for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup> Compensation state can also plausibly explain triggering trouble on a badly shaped edge, though a poorly compensated probe on one instrument may still trigger reliably where a properly working setup on another does not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup>

Probes with no adjustment at all cannot be corrected. A pocket scope supplied with a probe having no compensation adjustment on either the probe or the instrument shows severe overshoot and undershoot on a 1 MHz square wave, making it useless for high-frequency detail regardless of the claimed 40 MHz [[analog-bandwidth|analog bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup> Shipping an otherwise proper scope probe uncompensated and unadjustable counts as a design failure in itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup>

Stray hand [[capacitance]] acts as an uncontrolled compensation change: touching an active probe's tip structure with the fingers alters its characteristic response directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>

## The compensation output

The compensation signal is a low-frequency square wave, typically 1 kHz, brought out to a pin or pad on the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> Its presence is treated as a basic expectation of a bench oscilloscope, including in tablet-format and very low-cost instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1492" title="EEVblog 1492 - $5 Oscilloscope Repaired! + Oz GIVEAWAY">1492</a></sup> On some designs it is generated by dedicated chips rather than by a general-purpose output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> Its absence is a defect, though not a fatal one on an otherwise working secondhand instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-559" title="EEVblog #559 - Auction Score">559</a></sup> Conversely, seeing a clean square wave on the compensation output through a channel is a quick confirmation that a repaired or salvaged scope's front end is alive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-690" title="EEVblog #690 - TDS220 Oscilloscope Autopsy">690</a></sup>

## Distinct from the attenuation setting

Compensating a probe is separate from telling the scope what attenuation the probe is set to. The volts-per-division reading is only correct if the scope's probe setting matches the switch position on the probe itself: switching the probe to times 10 requires switching the channel's probe factor to times 10 as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup> Scopes with probe identification handle this automatically, detecting the times 10 probe and setting the vertical scale to match.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> On scopes without it, a front-panel or menu probe-attenuation control that is quick to reach is a usability point in its own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup>
