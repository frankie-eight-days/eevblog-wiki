Solder wick is a [[flux]]-impregnated copper braid used to draw molten [[solder]] away from a joint, a pad, or a plated through-hole.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Held against the work and heated with a [[soldering-iron|soldering iron]], the braid pulls the solder up into itself by capillary action, leaving the pad clean and tinned rather than blobbed or bridged — "It goes up the wick, hence the name solder wick."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> It is treated as a non-negotiable bench item rather than an optional accessory: a basic [[soldering]] kit is not complete without it, and a $300 starter lab budget allocates for it alongside flux and a tip cleaner.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup>

## What it does

The primary job is removing excess solder. A joint that has been overfed becomes a ball with dags of solder trailing off the iron; laying wick over it and heating recovers a normal fillet without the joint having to be redone from scratch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Applying too much solder is therefore not a fault that has to be avoided at all costs, because it can simply be wicked back off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

The same action clears through-holes. Once a component has been pulled, the hole is usually still plugged with solder and will not accept a new lead; wick laid over the pad and heated opens it up, which is the standard way of preparing a board for replacement electrolytics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> For component removal generally it competes with a solder sucker or a dedicated [[desoldering]] station, and either can be used on through-hole capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup>

In surface-mount work it does two distinct jobs. It removes solder bridges between adjacent pins — a drag-soldered fine-pitch package that comes out with two pins shorted is cleaned up in one pass, including at 0.65 mm pitch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup> And it cleans up pads after a chip has been lifted with hot air, with ChipQuik, or by cutting and dragging the pins off, leaving a flat tinned footprint ready for the replacement part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> Where a chip has been removed by cutting each pin individually, wick then takes off the stubs left behind.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Cleaning pads down to bare metal matters most when a new device is going back on; if the board is only being tested and the chip is scrap, the pads can be left as-is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

## Technique

The dominant failure mode is lifting pads. Placing the iron on the wick and dragging the assembly across a row of pins applies lateral force to pads whose [[adhesive]] has been softened by heat, and on a mediocre [[pcb|PCB]] the pads come off with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> The correct motion is to dab: press down on one pad, let the solder transfer, lift, move along, repeat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> This is slower but does not tear the footprint off the laminate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup> Scraping should be reserved for cases where nothing else will work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

Where the wick must be stroked, it is stroked longitudinally — along the axis of the pad, in the direction the pin runs — never sideways across the row.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup> A feather-light touch is required regardless of direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

Iron temperature is the other half of the equation. A low tip temperature protects the pads, and dropping from 370 °C to 330 °C is a reasonable response to a board that has already shown it will lift pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup> Excessive heat combined with lateral force is what destroys footprints, so the two precautions are applied together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup>

## Flux

The braid is sold with flux already impregnated in it, and that built-in flux is what makes it work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup> It is also the first thing to fail: flux dries out and ages in storage, so old stock underperforms, and the practical remedy is to add liquid flux from a pen before wicking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> On corroded or 20-year-old joints, on conformally coated boards, and on mixed alloys — regular lead-free solder contaminated with a low-melting-point removal alloy — supplementary flux is what makes the difference between the wick grabbing the solder and merely smearing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> On ordinary joints in good condition the built-in flux alone is generally sufficient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

Burning flux produces visible smoke and a distinct sizzle during fine-pitch cleanup; the fumes should not be inhaled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

## Choosing and stocking

Brand quality is not cosmetic. Cheap braid has poor or insufficient flux and works badly; a good professional-grade multicore braid is the recommended default, and the cheapies are to be avoided.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

Width should be matched to the work, which means keeping more than one roll. A general-purpose braid of around 2.2 mm covers most tasks, with a narrow roll of roughly 1 mm for fine-pitch pins and a wide roll for bulk removal and for use with a wide chisel tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> Very thick braid on a 0.65 mm pitch package is the wrong tool, and this is precisely where the superfine stock earns its place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> A roll of each width is regarded as the minimum sensible holding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup>

## Limits and alternatives

Wick is not universally superior to suction. A desoldering station clears multi-lead through-hole parts far faster, though its nozzles block frequently and need continuous cleaning; wick plus a cheap manual sucker and a wide iron tip is a workable substitute for occasional repair work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup> Dave Jones ranks a second soldering iron above a desoldering station in purchasing priority, on the grounds that a desoldering station is of no help with surface-mount parts, where heat must be applied to both ends of the component at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup>

The reverse also holds: a round desoldering nozzle cannot seal against irregularly shaped pads or oddly formed legs, and cannot reach solder close to a board edge, so wick — or simply heating and wiggling the lead free — is the better option there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup> Some parts resist wick entirely and have to be removed by heating all leads simultaneously and lifting the package off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup>

Used braid is worth keeping rather than discarding. Solder-loaded offcuts make excellent very low [[impedance]] straps and [[grounding]] links, a use Dave Jones keeps a parts-drawer stash for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup>
