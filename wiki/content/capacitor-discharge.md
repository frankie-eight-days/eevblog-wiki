Capacitor discharge is the removal of stored charge from a [[capacitor]], either as a deliberate bench safety step before working on a circuit or as the natural exponential decay that governs any RC network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> The two meanings sit close together in practice: the same exponential law that describes a timing circuit's fall curve also decides how long a mains filter capacitor stays lethal after the power is switched off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> In equipment with a rectifier and bulk filter capacitors, the stored energy remains at hundreds of volts long after the supply is disconnected, and discharging it deliberately is the difference between safe servicing and a serious shock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup>

## The exponential decay

In a basic RC circuit, a [[voltage-source|voltage source]] charges the capacitor through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]]; switching the source out and shorting the top of the [[resistor]] to [[ground]] discharges the capacitor through that same resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> The discharge curve is the mirror of the charge curve: the instantaneous capacitor [[voltage]] starts at whatever value it was charged to and falls exponentially from there, reaching 36.8% of the starting value after one time constant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> The [[current]] follows the same shape but starts at its maximum, set by the applied voltage divided by the series [[resistance]], and decays toward zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> In theory the voltage never quite reaches zero; after roughly ten time constants — about an order of magnitude beyond the time constant — it can be treated as fully discharged for any practical purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup>

The same curve appears wherever a charged capacitor sees a resistive path. Driving a capacitor from a [[signal-generator|signal generator]] through a random resistor produces a clean linear ramp on charge and a recognisable capacitor discharge curve as it dumps into the resistive load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup> In a [[555-timer|555 timer]] circuit, the discharge pin's internal [[transistor]] is switched on at the 6 V threshold and drains the 1 µF timing capacitor through the 100 K resistor to ground, taking half the time the charge took because the charge path went through 200 K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> A peak detector shows the unwanted version of the same behaviour: leakage prevents the held value from staying put, so the stored voltage droops away toward zero until the next peak tops it back up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>

## Bleeder resistors

Well-designed equipment discharges its own capacitors. A mains capacitor-dropper supply places bleeder resistors across the 220 nF mains capacitors so that a unit pulled from the outlet does not present a charged capacitor at the exposed pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup> The implementation detail matters: two 750 K resistors in series, 1.5 MΩ total, are used rather than one, because the SMD resistors in question are rated to only about 200 volts each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup> Large high-voltage electrolytics in consumer gear usually carry a [[bleeder-resistor|bleed resistor]] as well, but the presence of one should never be assumed — the capacitor should be measured before it is touched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup>

Many supplies simply omit them. A mains supply with a full-wave bridge and bulk filter capacitors sitting at 400 volts DC can hold that charge for a long time after the mains is switched off precisely because no discharge resistors were fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> The same is true of large [[lcd|LCD]] television supplies, where the main capacitor retains a couple of hundred volts with no bleed resistor across it and can take tens of minutes to decay on its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup> Photographic flash capacitors are a compact version of the same hazard, typically charged to 300-something volts with substantial stored energy behind them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-816" title="EEVblog #816 - Chinon Film Camera Teardown">816</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1440" title="EEVblog 1440 - Pentax Film Camera TEARDOWN">1440</a></sup>

## Discharging with a multimeter

The low-[[impedance]] or low-Z voltage mode found on many multimeters — nominally provided to kill ghost and phantom voltages — doubles as a controlled capacitor discharger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1429" title="EEVblog 1429 - Sony RX100 Camera Troubleshooting Part 2">1429</a></sup> Selecting it places a low impedance directly across the input, so the meter both drains the capacitor and reads the voltage while it does so.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-816" title="EEVblog #816 - Chinon Film Camera Teardown">816</a></sup> On one implementation, the mode switches in a 1 K series resistor together with a [[ptc-thermistor|PTC resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> The effect is fast: a camera flash capacitor at 265–267 volts drops through 70, 34, 19 and 12 volts within seconds on an auto-V mode that inserts a couple of kilohms of load,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1440" title="EEVblog 1440 - Pentax Film Camera TEARDOWN">1440</a></sup> and a television main filter capacitor can go from an overload indication to zero volts almost immediately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup> A 0.5 µF capacitor operating at 128 volts is emptied essentially at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup>

Meters without a low-impedance mode can still do the job. Switching to the ohms range for a couple of seconds and switching back discharges the capacitor safely, and this works with practically any [[multimeter]] of reasonable quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> The reason is visible in the [[input-protection|input protection]]: the ohms range presents a clamping circuit and PTC resistors across the input, functionally similar to what the dedicated low-Z mode does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> In the [[121gw|121GW]], two 1.5 K PTC resistors sit across the input in ohms mode — a value high enough to limit the current and low enough to discharge quickly, and safe against 240 volts mains because the PTCs increase their resistance sharply as current flow and internal [[power-dissipation|power dissipation]] heat them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> Ordinary 1.5 K resistors of the same value would burn up quickly under the same conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup>

The recommended procedure is to disconnect the circuit from the mains before switching the meter to the ohms range, rather than switching ranges with high DC voltage present on the leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> A separate approach for larger capacitors is a discharge lead with a resistor built into it, placed across the terminals,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> or a suitably rated dummy load resistor calculated for the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup>

## Dielectric absorption and recovery

A capacitor that has just been discharged does not necessarily stay at zero. The voltage creeps back up after the discharge path is removed, an effect caused by dielectric absorption in the capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> The recovery is routinely visible when a discharged capacitor is re-probed with a high-impedance voltage range,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1440" title="EEVblog 1440 - Pentax Film Camera TEARDOWN">1440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1429" title="EEVblog 1429 - Sony RX100 Camera Troubleshooting Part 2">1429</a></sup> and on supercapacitors it can be pronounced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> The recovered voltage is not dangerous, but it means a single discharge should not be taken as permanent, and it is one more reason to measure rather than assume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup>

## Bench practice

The standing rules are to measure before touching, and to assume nothing about bleed resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> Equipment with vacuum tubes deserves particular caution, since anode voltages are high and travelling wave tube supplies can leave a couple of thousand volts floating around inside; the capacitors must be confirmed discharged before poking around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XqakD0dXdjM" title="Guest Video: Kerry Wong - HP493A 8GHz Microwave RF Amplifier Teardown">•</a></sup> Large capacitors in vintage computers and [[crt|CRT]]-based gear hold enough energy that draining them is a prerequisite to getting hands inside the chassis, and a low-Z range makes that quick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup> Discharging is also a precondition for some measurements: ESR meters warn to discharge the capacitor before measuring, since applying a charged capacitor to the instrument can destroy it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup>

Discharge behaviour is itself diagnostic information. Watching a filter capacitor decay slowly at 10 seconds per division confirms both that it is holding charge and that it will take a long time to become safe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup> Conversely, a mains filter capacitor observed actively discharging on the meter confirms the discharge path is doing its job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> Where circuits deliberately discharge a capacitor, the timing shows up directly — a constant-current mode that switches a transistor to drain a 22 µF capacitor through 1 K to ground takes roughly 50 milliseconds to complete, and lowering that resistance toward zero shortens it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

Small capacitors matter too, at the opposite extreme. Bypass capacitors on a low-power [[microcontroller]] store enough energy to keep the circuit alive for several seconds after the supply is broken, and the device can appear dead and then come back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> The absence of a load is what preserves charge: in a Cockcroft-Walton multiplier with no load connected, the coupling capacitor stays charged indefinitely, and only when the input amplitude is reduced does the discharge on the capacitors become visible as they take some time to fall.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup>
