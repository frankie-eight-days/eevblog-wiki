A battery is an electrochemical energy store: it holds energy as chemistry and releases it as [[current]] flow when a circuit is completed, but it stores energy rather than power — "You can't store power."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Unlike a mains supply or bench [[power-supply]], a battery cannot hold a constant terminal [[voltage]] while delivering current, because its internal (ionic) [[resistance]] causes the terminal voltage to sag under load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> This single property — load-dependent terminal voltage — governs how batteries are specified, measured, and designed around.

## Capacity and energy ratings

[[battery-capacity]] is commonly quoted in milliamp-hours or amp-hours, but the strictly correct figure for stored energy is watt-hours, because a battery's voltage is not constant over discharge — it tapers off rather than staying flat and then dying abruptly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> A 25 watt-hour battery can in principle deliver 25 watts for one hour or 1 watt for 25 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Constant-current discharge testing therefore accumulates amp-hours, not watt-hours, because the load current is fixed while the voltage falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Representative nameplate ratings encountered in practice include a single-cell lithium polymer at 3.7 V, 4325 mAh (16 Wh) in a tablet,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> a 1420 mAh, 5.25 Wh single cell in an e-reader,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup> a 15.4 V, 5180 mAh (80 Wh) laptop pack,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p287qKMXqxQ" title="Lenovo Legion 5i 17&quot; Ryzen 7 RTX2060 Laptop">•</a></sup> and a 7.3 V, 20,000 mAh (74 Wh) pack in a portable [[oscilloscope]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup>

## Internal resistance and terminal voltage

The electrochemistry inside a cell presents an internal ionic resistance that is highly dynamic, varying with load, state of charge, temperature, and discharge history.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Terminal voltage under load is the only voltage that matters to a powered device; a bench power supply, having essentially zero internal resistance, behaves as an ideal battery and can deliver whatever pulse current a load demands, which is why products that refuse to run on depleted cells still work on a power supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Battery [[equivalent-series-resistance]] is also not constant — it changes with load and internal heating, particularly at higher powers — so characterising a cell for emulation requires discharging it at several different loads to build up the ESR curves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y2rcx4vKxlc" title="Unboxing the Keysight E36731A DC Power Supply, Electronic Load, and Battery Emulator">•</a></sup>

## Open-circuit voltage is misleading

A discharged cell recovers to a higher terminal voltage once the load is removed, because the internal drop disappears when current stops flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Cells discharged all the way to zero and held there have been observed to recover to 1.2 V open circuit, and a cell discharged to 1 V at half an amp recovered to 1.38 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> This recovered voltage implies almost nothing about remaining energy — fully depleted cells may retain perhaps 1% of their energy, enough to run a low-power device dimly for a while, but the open-circuit reading does not reveal this either way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> The sound engineering rule is to never ever measure the open circuit voltage when assessing state of charge or cutoff behaviour; manufacturers publish loaded discharge curves for exactly this reason, and measuring a battery removed from the product after use produces the recovered, unrepresentative value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>

## Product cutoff voltages

Well-designed battery products extract nearly all usable energy from a cell. Typical cutoff thresholds measured under load sit around 1.1 V per cell, with many devices operating lower: an Xbox controller and a Game Boy function down to 1 V per cell, a vacuum-fluorescent calculator works at 0.8 V per cell, a DAT Walkman at 1.1 V per cell, and a four-AA differential probe at about 0.95 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Below roughly 0.8 V per cell the remaining capacity "drops off a cliff" and essentially nothing is left.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> Because waiting days or months for a real battery to reach cutoff is impractical, the standard method for measuring a product's cutoff voltage is to power it from a bench supply and ramp the voltage down; the supply's zero source resistance means the measurement reflects the product's true terminal-voltage threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

## Measuring batteries

Meaningful battery measurement requires a load. A [[multimeter]]'s low-[[impedance]] mode, presenting a load of a couple of kilohms, is useful for this: a cell reading 1.21 V and falling quickly under that modest load is effectively dead, while the small droop seen at the top of a fully charged cell's range settles and stops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> Note that an unloaded cell can read acceptably on a 10 MΩ digital meter input while still failing in circuit due to high ESR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup> Dedicated battery-emulator instruments combine a power supply and [[electronic-load]] to record a real cell's discharge behaviour and then reproduce it, allowing a product to be tested against a simulated battery at chosen states of charge rather than a fixed voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y2rcx4vKxlc" title="Unboxing the Keysight E36731A DC Power Supply, Electronic Load, and Battery Emulator">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Charging and management

Lithium packs increasingly embed authentication: one e-reader's battery carries an I²C bus (SCL/SDA) with an ID chip, and the device refuses to power up from a source lacking that chip, so fitting a larger aftermarket cell requires reverse-engineering the interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup> An over-discharged lithium cell that has fallen to around 2.5 V can enter undervoltage lockout and refuse normal charging; kickstarting it with a bench supply in constant-current mode at a few hundred milliamps can raise it out of lockout so the normal charger takes over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KTr-44n0bbU" title="Garmin GPS Battery Kickstarting">•</a></sup> Fast NiMH chargers may interleave charge and bypass windows — 500 ms charging, 500 ms bypassed, alternating between cells — with charge and bypass never active simultaneously since that would short the battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> Where one charger IC must serve two packs that operate in series, the packs cannot be paralleled for charging, so the system charges them one at a time under [[microcontroller]] control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup>

## Failure modes and degradation

Lithium polymer cells swell visibly when degraded — a "puffy" pack is a recognised end-of-life sign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1330" title="EEVblog #1330 - Mailbag with GUEST LABS!">1330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o4S-TkTuHZs" title="Samsung Galaxy Tab A Troubleshooting">•</a></sup> Capacity also fades with age; decade-old cells may still function but deliver only a couple of hours of runtime where they once delivered many.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1613" title="EEVblog 1613 - Audiophile REPAIR: $4500 Chord Hugo DAC">1613</a></sup> Cells in series strings discharge unevenly, with one example pair reading 1.477 V and 1.29 V from the same device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup> Alkaline leakage can creep under the outer wrap and corrode the label and cell internals, and on some cells the positive terminal is directly welded to the can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1350" title="EEVblog #1350 - Fake Energizer Batteries?">1350</a></sup> A direct short across battery terminals is destructive even in small consumer devices — shorted toy battery wiring melted the conductors and the adjacent switch body.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1219" title="EEVblog #1219 - Don&#x27;t Trust Switches - Toy Repair">1219</a></sup> Batteries dislike heat, a constraint when a product's enclosure doubles as a [[heat-sink|heatsink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Battery management system software defects can themselves be a safety hazard serious enough to trigger a vehicle recall.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xb-uEIz_XKo" title="2020 Hyundai IONIQ Recall + Mysterious Relay Clicking">•</a></sup> Abuse tolerance varies by design: some lithium automotive replacement packs survive a nail penetration test by catching fire without exploding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UXnVOoBtlcU" title="eevBLAB 120 - DCS sues Youtuber for Defamation! - Threatens entire review industry">•</a></sup>

## Backup and long-life cells

Equipment that must retain [[calibration]] or timekeeping uses long-lived backup cells, often integrated into battery-backed [[sram]] modules. A 1987-vintage Dallas SRAM module has been found still holding its checksum decades later, and losing that battery corrupts the stored checksum at every power-up; good practice is to read out and archive the contents before the cell dies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> Instrument designs that store factory calibration separately in [[eeprom]] allow recovery after backup-battery failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> Where a device must sit dormant for years, low self-discharge cells rated at a maximum of 5% per month are still a marginal choice compared with primary cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1136" title="EEVblog #1136 - Banksy Artwork Shredder - Part 2 (Still Trolling!)">1136</a></sup>

## Large-scale storage

Home storage batteries apply the same principles at kilowatt-hour scale. A 15 kWh lithium iron phosphate system is typically configured with a 20% discharge floor — using only 80% of nameplate capacity — to extend service life, though the full range remains available under warranty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gAu8CvMjDrU" title="15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery - First Analysis">•</a></sup> High-power household loads drain such a system quickly: a 3 kW oven plus air conditioning can pull a full charge down to the floor within an evening.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gAu8CvMjDrU" title="15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery - First Analysis">•</a></sup> In hybrid-[[inverter]] installations, a current clamp on the grid connection detects export and diverts excess solar production into the battery, and storage discharges to cover load whenever solar output falls short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1634" title="EEVblog 1634 - 15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery Install!">1634</a></sup> Electric vehicles recover kinetic energy the same way in reverse, with regenerative braking returning energy to the traction battery instead of dissipating it as brake heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7nUgmEuyKIU" title="Hyundai IONIQ Regen Braking vs Coasting Test">•</a></sup> A full hybrid-battery recall remedy can consist of nothing more than updated battery-management software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xb-uEIz_XKo" title="2020 Hyundai IONIQ Recall + Mysterious Relay Clicking">•</a></sup>
