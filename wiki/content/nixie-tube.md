A nixie tube is a cold-[[cathode]] gas-filled display in which a stack of digit-shaped wire cathodes sits inside a glass envelope with a common anode, and applying a few hundred volts between the anode and one cathode makes that digit glow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-606" title="EEVblog #606 - Mailbag">606</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> Each numeral is a separate physical layer, so the digits light at different depths within the tube -- in a display showing a six, the six visibly stands proud of the zero, which sits further towards the back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup> They are decimal devices rather than seven-segment ones: a nixie counts zero through nine and nothing else, which is why the natural driver for one is a one-of-ten decoder rather than a segment decoder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-606" title="EEVblog #606 - Mailbag">606</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>

Nixies belong to the high-[[voltage]] neon and [[crt|CRT]] era that preceded [[led|LED]] displays, and they were displaced by it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kSvB7hV0XU8" title="Space Time Continuum with Fran Blanche">•</a></sup> They remain in demand for clocks, counters and similar conversation pieces, and the supply is entirely from old stock -- new tubes are difficult to obtain, and prices for desirable types have escalated from around twenty dollars for a Burroughs tube on the secondhand market a decade earlier to the point where three at a discount cost around a thousand dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-adSk6suM29E" title="Silicon Chip Electronex 2014">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wuFqB9ZeqKI" title="Fran &amp; Dave - The Amp Hour">•</a></sup>

## Electrical behaviour

A nixie needs a striking voltage appreciably higher than the voltage at which it will continue to glow, and both figures matter to the circuit designer. An IN-9 bar-graph tube run from a [[current]]-limited supply will not strike at 100 V or 120 V, is marginal at 130 V, and lights reliably by 140 to 150 V, drawing about 1 mA at 150 V compliance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup> Numeric tubes behave similarly: an array driven from a 170 V rail through dropper resistors sustains its glow down to about 130 V, falls off between 130 and 140 V, and does not all re-strike immediately when the voltage is brought back up, because individual tubes differ in threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> The IN-12B is specified for operation from about 120 V upwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup>

Current is set by a series [[dropper-resistor|dropper resistor]] from the anode rail, one per tube. A 22 k [[resistor]] from 170 V gives roughly 1.5 to 1.6 mA through a lit digit, which is a normal operating point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Dropping the dropper to 10 k pushed a seven-tube array to about 12 mA in total, still within a sensible range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> A high-voltage boost module producing 170 V from a 12 V input drew 5.6 W, or 24 mA at the input, driving seven tubes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>

The off cathodes are not at [[ground]]. Because each unlit cathode floats and is coupled to the anode through the gas and the tube geometry, the open-circuit voltage on the unused pins varies wildly from tube to tube and pin to pin: measurements around one IN-12 gave 49, 24, 43, 122, 118, 69, 47 and about 123 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RXk_wr0g8UM" title="Nixie Tube Voltage Drop">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Tying all the unused pins together settles at roughly 125 V, the extreme of the individual drops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> The practical consequence is that a designer cannot rely on the tube's own [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] to protect a low-voltage driver -- the driver has to withstand the full open-circuit voltage, which can exceed 120 V and may be higher on other tubes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RXk_wr0g8UM" title="Nixie Tube Voltage Drop">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup>

## Driving

The classic part is the 74141, a one-of-ten decoder with the high-voltage driver transistors built in, taking four binary inputs and pulling one of ten outputs low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup> It is long obsolete, though production is reported to have continued in Russia.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Period instruments used it as a matter of course, often socketed against failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup>

Modern alternatives fall into two camps. Discrete high-voltage transistors such as the MPSA42 will do the job, and 400 V parts leave ample margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-561" title="EEVblog #561 - Mailbag">561</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> A multiplexed scheme is electrically feasible -- nixies switch fast enough to be multiplexed -- but the high-side digit switch must itself be a high-voltage [[transistor]] driven by another high-voltage transistor, giving a two-transistor solution per digit and negating the saving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> The alternative is a driver array. The jelly-bean ULN2003 contains seven driver transistors rated only to 50 V, so on its own it cannot survive the off-pin voltages; with a suitable Zener clamp on the common pin it can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Its seven channels do not even cover one ten-cathode tube, so drivers have to be shared across tubes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup>

Whatever the driver, its outputs must be open collector or open drain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup> A totem-pole output pulls the unselected cathodes hard up to the anode rail, and that is destructive to the tube: shorting one spare pin to the 170 V supply took the current through the dropper from about 1.5 mA to roughly 9 mA while the selected digit stayed lit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Connecting the driver's high-voltage pin to the far side of the dropper resistor instead, so that the spare pin is only shorted to the tube's own anode node, is harmless and leaks only around 80 to 100 µA -- but it forces one driver chip per tube, since the 32- and 64-output serial drivers otherwise span several tubes, each with its own dropper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup>

## Clamping and pre-bias

Strapping the unused cathodes together through a Zener to a fixed potential -- a pre-bias -- is the standard fix for a driver that cannot take the full open-circuit voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> With all spare pins commoned into a 30 V Zener, total leakage was about 330 µA and the lit digit showed no change in brightness at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup>

The clamp voltage cannot be chosen freely, because the voltage left across the off cathodes is the supply minus the clamp, and that difference must stay below the tube's sustaining voltage or the off digits glow faintly. A 33 V clamp on a 170 V rail leaves 137 V, comfortably above the 120 V minimum, so the unselected digits will show some glow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> A 50 V clamp on 170 V gives exactly 120 V, which sits on the specification limit with no margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> Reducing the supply to 160 V with the same 50 V clamp leaves 110 V and restores a usable margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> The effect is measurable: at 180 V a set of eight nominally off segments biased on and drew 0.44 mA in total with a visible faint glow, and that residual glow persists even when a segment is properly selected; at 160 V with 50 V clamping the off segments stay dark.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> Behaviour varies between tube types, so the margin should be verified with the tubes actually in hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup>

## Construction and assembly practice

Tube construction is hand-wired, with each digit a separate stamped layer held apart by spacers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup> The IN-12 series is the common Russian numeric type, in A and B variants; the B adds a decimal point on pin 12, so an eight-tube display can place the point anywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> IN-12s are end emitters -- the vacuum tip is on the back rather than the side -- which lets them mount directly on a [[pcb|PCB]] or behind a front panel, unlike the round side-emitting types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup> Bar-graph tubes such as the argon-filled IN-9 exist as well, glowing purple and having only two pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-251" title="EEVblog #251 - Mailbag">251</a></sup>

Tubes should be socketed rather than soldered directly to the board: they are fragile and may need replacing, and sockets made specifically for nixies are available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> Sockets also raise the tubes about 10 mm off the board, freeing the space beneath them for driver chips and even for a [[power-supply|power supply]] module mounted on the underside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

At board level the high voltage governs the layout. A 160 V bus and its dropper resistors run along one edge, with the anodes dropping down to the tubes, and the low-voltage serial bus runs along the opposite edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> [[ground-plane|Ground plane]] is flood-filled only over the low-voltage half, deliberately kept out from under the high-voltage section, and clearance rules matter throughout because of the DC potentials involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup> On the [[schematic]] side, a driver symbol is best drawn with all its outputs on one side facing the tube regardless of the physical pinout, with the open-collector or open-drain output symbol shown explicitly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup>

## Systems and applications

Serial-driven displays are shifted in as a bit stream on the negative clock edge, with a one meaning digit off because the open-drain output must be pulled low to light a cathode; the whole display then updates at once on a register clock pulse after the data is shifted in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup> A complete display built this way was driven by an ESP8266 on a Wemos D1 Mini, converting a fetched value to BCD before calling the write routine, with the source polled roughly once a minute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup> The supporting supply used a 12 V jack, a high-voltage module with a 1.1 k resistor setting 160 V, and a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] for the 5 V logic, whose dissipation was negligible because the driver chips are CMOS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup>

Historically nixies were the display of choice for instruments and calculators. A Systron Donner counter dating from 1973 used 7475 four-bit latches, 74141 drivers in sockets, and National tubes with a 7328 date code, alongside an ovenized [[oscillator]], BCD output and a remote-control interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup> A Soviet Iskra 111 desktop calculator of 1972 carried twelve nixie tubes plus a sign digit and ran directly from 220 V AC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-481" title="EEVblog #481 - Mailbag">481</a></sup> Kit-form nixie clocks and thermometers remain common, some of them entirely passive designs without a [[microcontroller]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-adSk6suM29E" title="Silicon Chip Electronex 2014">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-251" title="EEVblog #251 - Mailbag">251</a></sup> There are also LED imitations, in which surface-mount LEDs light stacked printed digit layers through light pipes to mimic the depth effect of a real tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1155" title="EEVblog #1155 - Mailbag">1155</a></sup>
