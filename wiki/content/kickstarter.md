Kickstarter is a reward-based crowdfunding platform on which a hardware creator offers pledge levels against a funding goal, collects money from backers only if the goal is met, and then delivers a product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-noNyZ2A3k4Q" title="eevBLAB #46 - Indiegogo Campaign Logo Fraud">•</a></sup> For small electronics manufacturers it functions as a way to get a production run commission-prepaid — proof that people will actually buy the thing before a thousand pieces are committed to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> It is not a retail channel: pledge levels are minimum contributions to a project rather than prices in a catalogue, and the platform was deliberately not designed as a store.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

## Funding mechanics

Funding is all-or-nothing. A campaign that fails to reach its goal collects nothing, in contrast to Indiegogo's flexible funding, where the creator keeps whatever is raised regardless of whether the target is met.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-noNyZ2A3k4Q" title="eevBLAB #46 - Indiegogo Campaign Logo Fraud">•</a></sup> Because backers pledge rather than buy, a pledge at a given level may exceed that level's minimum by any amount, which leaves the creator with a database of mismatched pledges to reconcile manually against actual rewards at the end of the campaign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

Fees are material to a hardware budget. On a campaign raising $116,575, Kickstarter's fixed 5% cut plus roughly 3.1% taken by the credit card merchant came to just over 8% of the total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> Payment is not immediate: charges are placed on backers' cards when the campaign closes, the money sits with Kickstarter for approximately two weeks while charges are verified, and only then is it released as a single lump sum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4tcC_TdhzUU" title="eevBLAB  #43 - Kickstarter Vultures">•</a></sup> For a campaign ending 31 December, funds arrived on 15 January.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> That delay is a real constraint on a hardware project, since parts cannot be ordered until the money lands — purchase orders raised in anticipation of the transfer carry the creator's own risk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4tcC_TdhzUU" title="eevBLAB  #43 - Kickstarter Vultures">•</a></sup> Some platforms behave differently: on the Australian site Pozible, funds were released to the creator as soon as the target was reached, and PayPal was accepted, which Kickstarter did not do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup>

Payment plumbing varies by region. Australian campaigns did not use the Amazon payment system used for US backers; the Australian arm collected through a third-party credit card merchant and held the money itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup>

## Campaign management

The backend supports the logistics of fulfilment reasonably well. Backer surveys collect shipping addresses, responses update live so a creator can watch addresses come in and message backers who have not yet responded, and the whole backer list can be exported to CSV as many times as wanted — sorted by pledge level or by backing order, so that the earliest backers can be shipped first, and imported into a spreadsheet for sorting by country and feeding to a label printer or mailing house.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> The principal limitation of the survey system is that it can only be sent once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> Project page formatting is minimal, with no formatting available inside the pledge descriptions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> The comment system does not support nested comments, so a campaign owner can post a global comment but cannot reply to an individual backer's comment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> Kickstarter also hosts live video from within a campaign page, with backers and non-backers viewing it on the Kickstarter site rather than on an external streaming platform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> A Make 100 campaign format exists as a way to gauge interest in a kit at small volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup>

## Discovery value

Kickstarter's own traffic contributes less to a campaign than is often assumed when the creator already has an audience. For the µCurrent Gold campaign, platform statistics attributed 22% of pledges to Kickstarter itself, with the remainder arriving from external referrers — 279 pledges direct, 262 from the creator's own website, 211 from the video channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> Because some of that 22% consisted of people who already knew about the project and simply went to Kickstarter directly rather than clicking a link, the realistic figure for genuinely incremental sales is closer to 10 to 15%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> The pledge curve is characteristically front- and back-loaded: a strong opening driven by an existing audience, a long flat middle, and a rise at the end as people move before the campaign closes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup>

## Delivery risk

Something on the order of 60 to 70% or more of hardware projects on Kickstarter fail to deliver on schedule, which is unsurprising to anyone who has taken a product through electronics manufacturing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-467" title="EEVblog #467 - Walktime Rant - Crowd Funding Projects">467</a></sup> The majority of failures are not fraud; they are well-intentioned projects that do not deliver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-467" title="EEVblog #467 - Walktime Rant - Crowd Funding Projects">467</a></sup> A common failure pattern is running out of money during the transition to production — reaching a headline funding figure that still leaves the project in the valley of death, short of what volume manufacture actually costs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup> Another is a complete absence of logistics capability: a campaign that raises a large sum and then has six weeks to deliver, without appreciating that building kits requires a warehouse of parts to pick from and floor space measured in tens of thousands of square feet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wuFqB9ZeqKI" title="Fran &amp; Dave - The Amp Hour">•</a></sup> Anyone starting a campaign without at least the basics of manufacturing and logistics lined up beforehand should not be running one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4tcC_TdhzUU" title="eevBLAB  #43 - Kickstarter Vultures">•</a></sup>

Schedule slip is routine even for competent projects. A backpack strap light backed in early 2016 with promised June 2016 delivery shipped in July 2017, over a year late.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup> Long stated timelines are themselves a warning: a campaign whose own roadmap runs through prototype version five, engineering validation, design validation, consumer testing and packaging finalisation is a year away at minimum, with a great deal of mechanical and production risk still ahead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1251" title="EEVblog #1251 - LifeSaber Kickstarter - A Master of None FAIL">1251</a></sup> For the [[121gw|121GW]] [[multimeter]], choosing a December delivery date proved to be the mistake, and the campaign itself was launched roughly a year and a half into a development that had already been re-spun mid-project.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> Pre-production lifecycle testing on that project was carried out under time pressure to get the Kickstarter units out, without the luxury of a month to qualify a test jig.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Bh3gcBr0DKs" title="How to do Lifecycle Testing">•</a></sup>

## Vetting and the scam problem

Kickstarter requires a demonstrable working prototype before a project can be listed, a rule that pushes projects with nothing tangible toward Indiegogo, which has fewer requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-467" title="EEVblog #467 - Walktime Rant - Crowd Funding Projects">467</a></sup> Kickstarter is also nominally reviewed: each project is verified and approved by staff, of whom roughly 70 to 75 of 148 Brooklyn-based employees work on the community side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HfqpuhYiR3o" title="eevBLAB #60 - Kickstarter Free Energy SCAMS!">•</a></sup> The vetting is not technical. Free-energy and over-unity projects have been approved and funded on the platform, including one seeking $1.6 million for a machine whose own campaign video describes a closed loop of perpetual motion two minutes in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HfqpuhYiR3o" title="eevBLAB #60 - Kickstarter Free Energy SCAMS!">•</a></sup> The remedy is straightforward — retain or employ an engineer competent to evaluate technical campaigns, which non-technical staff working from a checklist cannot do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HfqpuhYiR3o" title="eevBLAB #60 - Kickstarter Free Energy SCAMS!">•</a></sup> Reporting projects does sometimes work: a graphene infrared heater campaign claiming eight times the [[efficiency]] of conventional heaters was eventually shut down after being reported.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HfqpuhYiR3o" title="eevBLAB #60 - Kickstarter Free Energy SCAMS!">•</a></sup>

The physics of the failures is usually elementary. A panel radiator with graphene paste smeared in it is still a panel radiator, and no amount of graphene changes the number of BTUs needed to heat a room against its leakage — a 200 W panel does not produce as much heat as a 1,500 W radiator, and a campaign whose own test showed 440 W consumed for a nominally 300 W panel has not verified its own specifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup> Once a creator is committed, every test they run will be slanted toward a favourable result; only properly controlled A-B comparisons settle it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup> Linking a patent application in which the examiner dismisses the applicant's entire argument is a self-inflicted wound for an over-unity campaign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aTpm62ToNOM" title="eevBLAB #82 - Kickstarter Over-unity Gravitational Power Generator!">•</a></sup> Claims of harvesting usable phone-charging energy from Wi-Fi signals recur on the platform and do not survive an energy budget calculation, RF harvesting being viable only for ultra-low-power sensor applications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup>

Marketing convention is a further tell. Crowdfunding videos are now routinely shot and edited by professionals with multiple cameras and off-axis cut-ins, so production quality carries no information about the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup> An as-seen-in or as-featured-in logo strip is a staple of Kickstarter and Indiegogo campaigns and frequently cites outlets with no technical standing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1290" title="EEVblog #1290 - Hapbee: Anatomy of a Snake Oil Product">1290</a></sup> Buzzword loading is similar: [[bluetooth|Bluetooth]] connectivity added for its own sake because that is how money is raised, blockchain tokens bolted onto an idea with no coherent basis, graphene invoked as a mechanism.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1099" title="EEVblog #1099 - BattBump Kickstarter REDACTED EDITION!">1099</a></sup> An [[open-source-hardware|open source hardware]] gear logo on a campaign page conveys nothing verifiable either, since it may mean full compliance with the community definition or merely a [[schematic]] released as a PDF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup>

The blunt test for a backer is whether the thing already exists: confronted with a fancy product on Kickstarter, ask whether it can be bought somewhere already, and what the device actually does when stripped of the marketing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1454" title="EEVblog 1454 - Water from Air AGAIN! - The Kara Pure">1454</a></sup>

## Secondary predation

A successful campaign attracts an immediate flood of unsolicited email from marketing, fulfilment and manufacturing intermediaries offering to handle supply chains, upsell conversion and backer happiness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4tcC_TdhzUU" title="eevBLAB  #43 - Kickstarter Vultures">•</a></sup> Some of these services — logistics, manufacturing liaison — are genuinely useful; the volume and the sales approach are not, and an auto-filter is the practical response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4tcC_TdhzUU" title="eevBLAB  #43 - Kickstarter Vultures">•</a></sup> Indiegogo separately solicits successful Kickstarter campaigns to move over to its in-demand program, and traffic between the platforms is predominantly in that direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lKCNS85RDdA" title="121GW Indiegogo SCAM!">•</a></sup>

Outright campaign cloning also occurs. The 121GW campaign was copied wholesale onto Indiegogo — backer levels, photographs, text, profile picture and the announcement video — by an unaffiliated party who had raised $1,000 from six backers before the fraud was flagged, plausibly the product of a bot scraping successful Kickstarter campaigns.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lKCNS85RDdA" title="121GW Indiegogo SCAM!">•</a></sup> Campaigns cannot be fully deleted from Kickstarter; a cancelled project's name remains even after the owner deletes their account and photo.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-U_ftKolqq80" title="eevBLAB #48 - The end of Tech Journalism on Youtube?">•</a></sup>

## Representative hardware campaigns

The µCurrent Gold campaign raised $116,575 against a $9,900 goal from 1,268 backers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-581" title="EEVblog #581 - How To Do Kickstarter Surveys &amp; Project Management">581</a></sup> The 121GW multimeter raised A$644,000 from 2,296 backers, having passed $113,000 with 417 backers and $147,000 with 539 backers during the campaign, against a goal of $42,000 that was chosen more or less arbitrarily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4tcC_TdhzUU" title="eevBLAB  #43 - Kickstarter Vultures">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> Its predecessor campaign had drawn a little over a thousand backers for a lower-cost product with correspondingly broader appeal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

Elsewhere on the platform, the Pebble Time smartwatch raised $20 million from about 78,000 backers, twice the sum raised by the original Pebble, which had 68,000 backers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-758" title="EEVblog #758 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Unboxing &amp; Review">758</a></sup> The uArm four-axis desktop robotic arm reached $164,000 with 856 backers against a $5,000 goal, with the funds earmarked for hiring software developers — a well-founded priority, since software tooling is the binding constraint on a small robot arm whose serial command set is otherwise sufficient for users to write their own control code.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-586" title="EEVblog #586 - Open Source Hardware uARM 4-Axis Desktop Robotic Arm Kickstarter">586</a></sup> The Giroptic 360cam raised around $1.5 million.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-734" title="EEVblog #734 - Giroptic 360cam Kickstarter Prototype">734</a></sup> Digilent's OpenScope MZ raised roughly $107,000 from just over 1,000 backers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup> The Chronos high-speed camera, developed over about ten years by a single designer and priced at $2,500 against comparable instruments costing five to ten times as much, was funded from a genuine working prototype rather than renderings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-947" title="EEVblog #947 - Chronos High Speed Camera Review">947</a></sup> The Edison educational robot raised around $112,000 against a $20,000 target and delivered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> Cartesian Co raised US$140,000 for a desktop [[pcb|PCB]] printer, selling nearly a hundred units, and was among the first projects on the Australian arm of the platform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VGwCZ8_EzqI" title="Maker Fair Sydney 2014 Talk - Growing Maker Communities">•</a></sup>

Small campaigns are common and viable: a T-slot construction beam raised about $27,000 against a $12,000 ask,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-715" title="EEVblog #715 - Mailbag">715</a></sup> a set of manual SMD tapes trays for hand pick-and-place met a sub-$1,000 goal and took a few thousand,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> and a student space group raised $15,000 for laser driver research hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup> A [[usb|USB]]-C [[power-supply|power supply]] adapter campaign drew almost 1,800 backers and $141,000 and delivered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup> The Vion multimeter raised over half a million dollars across simultaneous Kickstarter and Indiegogo campaigns, delivered late after repeated delays, and demonstrated that the ability to run a campaign and keep backers updated is unrelated to the ability to implement basic instrument functionality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>
