Output voltage is the potential a source, regulator, converter, or amplifier presents at its terminals, and it is the parameter around which most power and signal-source design is organised. It is rarely a single fixed number: it is set by a [[resistor]] ratio or a digital word, it moves with load and with supply, it is bounded by the device's own headroom, and it is specified with a tolerance that real hardware sometimes fails to meet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f_SdM6sXHD4" title="FNIRSI SG-003A Process Meter + DSO-TC2 Oscilloscope &amp; Transistor Tester">•</a></sup> Because almost every other specification — [[efficiency]], compliance [[current]], dropout, accuracy — is quoted at a particular output voltage, the number chosen up front propagates through the whole design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

## Setting the output

In a classic adjustable [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] the output voltage is fixed by a two-[[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] from the output back to the adjust terminal. For an [[lm317|LM317]] targeting 3.3 V, the lower leg is a nominal value — 240 Ω is the usual [[datasheet]] figure, though 220 Ω with a variable upper leg works equally well — and the upper resistor is then calculated from the standard formula.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Switching converters work the same way: a DC-DC output of about 7.6 V follows from a 51 kΩ upper resistor over a 10 kΩ lower one, plus one, multiplied by the converter's 1.245 V internal reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup> The same principle extends to high-[[voltage]] supplies, where a single 680 Ω external resistor sets a 170 V rail from a 12 V input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>

Because the divider is the only thing that defines the output, two otherwise pin-equivalent parts that share the same output-voltage setting arrangement can be substituted directly, though [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] ESR must still be checked for stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> Conversely, when an unknown part must be identified, knowing the input voltage and the intended output voltage narrows the search substantially, since the resistor values can then be worked back through the candidate datasheet formulas.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup>

Where the output must be programmable, the reference is replaced by a DAC or a [[pwm|PWM]] signal. A 0–20 V output range — or 20.48 V, which falls out neatly on DAC steps — is a convenient design target because it needs only a doubling of the DAC-stage gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Driving the control node from a PWM output works down to very small duty cycles, with the output tracking the duty ratio: 20 % of a 6.6 V control span through a times-two amplifier gives the expected 1.3 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Such a chain can go wrong in ways that show up only as a scale error — an output moving 200 mV per intended one-millivolt step points at an unintended times-two amplifier stage rather than at the DAC itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>

## Accuracy, tolerance, and deliberate offset

The setting resistors do not tell the whole story. Current out of the LM317 adjust terminal flows through the upper resistor and adds directly to the output: taking the worst-case 100 µA rather than the 50 µA typical, across 363 Ω, adds about 36 mV, shifting a nominal 3.3 V output to 3.336 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The conventional choice of 240 Ω for the lower resistor exists largely to keep this adjust-terminal error small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Ordinary 5 % resistors are enough to land a predicted 7.6 V output at a measured 7.723 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup>

Instrument-grade supplies are expected to read back what they are set to — 4.97 V displayed against a 4.9 V setting on a repaired bench supply is normal behaviour — and to hold that after abuse, with output voltage and current still within specification after a full sequence of load tests.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> A default 1 V output sitting about 3 mV under nominal is unremarkable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> A factory-fresh process meter that delivers 7.9 V when commanded to output 8.000 V, and roughly 0.7 V when set to 1 V against a 0.1 % specification, is out of [[calibration]] and fails outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f_SdM6sXHD4" title="FNIRSI SG-003A Process Meter + DSO-TC2 Oscilloscope &amp; Transistor Tester">•</a></sup>

Not every deviation from the nominal figure is an error. A DIN-rail 5 V supply measuring 5.15 V is doing so on purpose — "you don't want it actually precisely 5" — because cable drop between the supply and the load subtracts from whatever leaves the terminals, so starting slightly high means the load still sees 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1656" title="EEVblog 1656 - MAILBAG: ElectroPermanent Magnet, DIN PSU&#x27;s, Sponge, Beelink EQ14 N150 PC">1656</a></sup>

## Limits of range

No output stage reaches its own supply rails. An OPA277's minimum output voltage is the negative rail plus roughly half a volt, so with the negative rail at [[ground]] the output simply cannot approach zero, and the operating point must be lifted above that floor — about one silicon [[diode]] drop, 0.6 V, suffices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup> Output swing also depends on the supply: reducing an op-amp's rails from ±5 V to ±2.5 V drops the output voltage while the input stays constant, because gain-[[bandwidth]] product is not independent of supply voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-572" title="EEVblog #572 - Cascading Opamps For Increased Bandwidth">572</a></sup>

Published maxima are sometimes conservative. A high-voltage differential probe specified at 7 V maximum output does deliver ±7 V, and in fact clips only above about 8 V — over specification on the voltage actually presented to the scope input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1414" title="EEVblog 1414 - MicSig DP10007 High Voltage Probe - Turning it up to 11">1414</a></sup> A later model in the same family reduced the output swing to ±3 V from the ±7 V of its predecessors, with consequences for [[noise-floor|noise floor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> [[function-generator|Function generator]] and source ranges are quoted the same way: ±12 V from an integrated function generator, 0–35 V from a 6641A-class supply, 0–100 V (or 200 V in series) from a 100 V bench supply, and roughly 200 V at 1 A from a general-purpose source-measure unit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup> A newer 800 W supply doubles both the output voltage of its predecessor, with a 64 V option, and the current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup>

Devices with a fixed or menu-selected output offer a discrete set instead of a range: 1.8, 2.5, 3.3 and 3.6 V from an energy-harvesting front end, five levels from 3.3 down to 1.2 V on a programmer's supply rail, or a continuously adjustable 4.8 V to 12.5 V on a model-aircraft regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-991" title="EEVblog #991 - Mailbag">991</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

## Output voltage as a selection parameter

Datasheet performance curves are plotted at a specific output voltage, and applying them at any other output is a trap. Characteristic curves drawn for Vout = 1.8 V have no bearing on a 5 V design, and a graph that omits the output voltage entirely is useless; the correct approach is to find the curve family explicitly labelled for the intended output — Vout = 5 V — which will also list the [[inductance]] and input/output [[capacitance]] values at which it was measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> A [[boost-converter|boost converter]] advertising low input voltage and 94 % efficiency is disqualified immediately if its fixed output voltage is 3.3 V and the requirement is 5 V, an outcome a [[parametric-search|parametric search]] sorted on output voltage would have caught earlier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

Efficiency itself is strongly a function of the output voltage chosen. A buck module from a 30 V input reaches roughly 95 % at 24 V, 12 V and 5 V outputs, but a 1.5 V output would fall to around 60 %; the peak is placed where the designer optimised the output power level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> The same module from a 40 V input can only produce its two highest outputs, up to a maximum of 32 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

## Behaviour under load and in circuit

Output voltage is meaningful only alongside the source [[impedance]] behind it. A Cockcroft-Walton multiplier that reads high on a light load falls to 75.6 V into 10 MΩ and to 70 V into 1 MΩ, a load most circuits would consider negligible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup> A regulator's own output is also affected by what is connected: a rail measured at 14.7 V with a load disconnected is the correct starting point for deciding whether that load should then function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup>

Switching the output on and off does not necessarily take it to zero. A soft-latching switch leaves about 500 mV at the output after turn-off, which then decays, rather than snapping to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup> In a supply where a series pass [[mosfet|MOSFET]] performs the switching, the gate [[waveform]] and the 30 V output waveform can be watched together on two channels to confirm that the switch, not the regulator, is what is acting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

Feedback arrangements make the output voltage of one stage a dependent variable of another. In a composite amplifier the precision front-end op-amp no longer sits at zero once the loop senses the buffer's output: its own output moves to −55 mV to compensate, which is the mechanism by which the buffer's offset is driven to zero at the sense point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1609" title="EEVblog 1609 - Composite Amplifier Tutorial + Practical Demo">1609</a></sup> A peak detector driven too fast, or loaded too heavily through its discharge path, shows [[overshoot]] on its output voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>

Sources that are not regulators still have an output voltage worth reasoning about. A thermoelectric generator produces a voltage from the temperature difference across its plates, and reversing the differential reverses the polarity of the output; the current, however, comes from the heat actually transferred through the module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> Even a poor, unregulated rail can be useful if its output voltage clears the headroom of a following regulator — 3.64 V from a charge-pump [[inverter]] built with 1N4148 diodes and small capacitors is enough to feed a 3.3 V low-dropout regulator at a couple of hundred microamps and produce a clean supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup>

## Measurement and diagnosis

Monitoring output voltage alongside input voltage is what allows [[firmware]] to know whether a regulator still has enough headroom to regulate, so an instrumented supply digitises both the output voltage and the incoming source voltage rather than the output alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> The same pairing is used on the bench: feeding a regulator's output from a second supply while watching input voltage, output voltage, and the current flowing back in characterises behaviour at switch-on and switch-off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-233" title="EEVblog #233 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 6 - LT3080 Testing">233</a></sup>

A working output is often the fastest confirmation that a repair is sound — 12.6 V present after replacing marginal capacitors settles both the repair and the choice of 16 V parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> Output voltage is equally capable of misleading: an anomalous integrator output voltage in a microvolt DMM proved to be a red herring unrelated to the actual fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup> Prototype converters are verified the same way, with 15 V out from a 5 V input confirming the topology before [[output-ripple|output ripple]] is examined under load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Where a source is being characterised rather than used, input current and output voltage are measured together to check the claimed 0.2 % figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-197" title="RAW! Testing µCurrent&#x27;s, Keithley Current/Voltage Sources, DIY Pick And Place - EEVblog #197">197</a></sup>

Bench supply displays are built around this set of quantities: measured output voltage, measured [[output-current|output current]], and measured output power at 1 mW resolution, with the set values shown separately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> A supply that switches its display between measured output current and the adjustable current setting depending on whether the output is enabled removes the need to short the terminals to set a [[current-limit|current limit]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup>
