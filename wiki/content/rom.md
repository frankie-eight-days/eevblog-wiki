Read-only memory is non-volatile storage that holds the instructions a computer executes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup> In the classic microprocessor architecture it is one of three mandatory blocks — a [[processor]], some [[ram|RAM]] and some ROM — with everything else on the board being interface, glue logic and drivers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-641" title="EEVblog #641 - Dumpster Cash Register Teardown">641</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1008" title="EEVblog #1008 - Mystery Teardown">1008</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup> The distinction between a microprocessor and a [[microcontroller]] rests on where that memory lives: a microprocessor requires external RAM and external program memory, whereas a microcontroller has both built in along with its I/O.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup>

## Role in a system

On a bare processor board the ROM sits on the address and data bus close to the processor, and its position in the memory map determines where execution begins; on the RC2014 Z80 kit the base address of the ROM is set by jumper links, so that at power-up the processor reads instructions from a known address and drops into BASIC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup> That kit runs Microsoft BASIC from ROM alongside 32 K of RAM and a 7.3 MHz clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup> Layout follows the same logic: on the Amiga 500 the ROM is placed immediately adjacent to the processor, with data flowing out through buffer and latch stages towards the I/O.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup>

The dependency can be removed entirely in either direction. The Philips PCF80C31 is functionally an 8051 with no internal ROM, which forces a separate external ROM device onto the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-967" title="EEVblog #967 - Mystery Teardown">967</a></sup> At the other extreme the Gigatron TTL microcomputer has no microprocessor at all — only a ROM and a 62256 RAM, with every remaining function implemented in TTL logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1080" title="EEVblog #1080 - Gigatron TTL RISC Kit Computer Review">1080</a></sup> The Texas Instruments TMS1000, the first microcontroller, integrated RAM, ROM and I/O in one part costing a couple of dollars, which collapsed the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] for handheld electronic games to a single chip plus LEDs and a case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup>

## Mask, one-time-programmable and erasable types

A mask ROM has its contents fixed at manufacture and is the cheapest and least serviceable option. The IBM PC Jr uses a mask ROM soldered directly to a [[multilayer-pcb|multilayer board]] rather than socketed, carrying an 8th-week-1984 date code — before the machine's official March 1984 introduction — so any BIOS change requires [[desoldering]] the part; a second TMM23256 on the same board is likewise a mask ROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> Apple's Newton splits its [[firmware]] across a low ROM and a high ROM, both Apple-branded mask parts at version 1.30.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-418" title="EEVblog #418 - Mailbag Apple Newton Teardown">418</a></sup>

One-time-programmable devices sit between mask and erasable parts. The Atmel AT27C256 is a 256 Kbit, 32 kilobyte OTP ROM; a PDA carrying two of them holds 64 K of ROM in total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> Program ROMs with no window on the package cannot be UV-erased and are therefore write-once, as in the Compucorp 322G calculator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup> Genuinely erasable parts are identifiable by a clear quartz window and must be exposed to ultraviolet light to be cleared, which is precisely why in-circuit emulation was needed to examine or substitute program memory during development.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Field-programmable memory carried a price premium of roughly 20 to 25 percent over mask ROM, which was accepted for its time-to-market advantage and for low-volume or high-value products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup>

## Capacity

ROM sizes track the era of the machine rather than any single trend. A 1970s 8085 design kit shipped with 2 K of ROM against 512 bytes of RAM once expanded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> Early PDAs pair 32 K of ROM with 8 K of [[sram|SRAM]]; mid-generation devices move to 4 megabytes of ROM with 512 K of SRAM; late devices reach 48 megabytes of ROM with 128 megabytes of SDRAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> A 1985 pocket computer offered 32 K of ROM and 8 K of RAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup> Integrated parts follow the same pattern, with the TMS320C5504 DSP carrying 256 K of on-chip SRAM and 128 K of ROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup> The Compaq Portable's BIOS lives in a 64 Kbit device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup>

## Uses beyond program storage

Not every ROM holds processor instructions. A 1970s programmable calculator contains six different ROMs of which only some are program ROMs — the set also includes a keyboard mapping ROM that translates key positions, a character generator ROM, and a printer controller with a ROM of its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1153" title="EEVblog #1153 - 1970&#x27;s Programmable Calculator Teardown">1153</a></sup> The same lookup principle drives an electronic translator, where a typed word is mapped to a ROM entry and the mapped word displayed, with separate language ROMs — including an Italian one copyrighted 1980 — plugged in as cartridges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1003" title="EEVblog #1003 - Mailbag">1003</a></sup>

ROM is also a substitute for computation. A cardiac simulator stores its [[waveform|waveforms]] in a one-megabit Atmel ROM and has the microcontroller do nothing but stream them out, which offloads the processor entirely and is effectively required if high-resolution signals are wanted rather than an algorithmically generated approximation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup> Test gear uses the same trick, with dedicated waveform ROMs on a generator board separate from the processor board's own ROMs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1256" title="EEVblog #1256 - GORGEOUS Mystery Test Gear Teardown!">1256</a></sup>

Inside custom silicon, ROM appears as a control store. The Fluke 27's custom analog IC contains digital circuitry that runs a state machine over an A/D decoding ROM, which is what implements [[auto-ranging|autoranging]] and the modified dual-slope conversion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup> A Sony video effects processor uses an internal ROM alongside dividers, latches, rounding and square-root hardware within a single signal-processing block.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup> Where a sensor has a non-flat response, [[calibration]] constants characterised at the factory may be programmed into the instrument's ROMs so the firmware can compensate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1366" title="EEVblog #1366 - Mystery Bunker Item Teardown">1366</a></sup> Security material is a poor fit for ROM, since anything held there is readable by anyone who dumps the part; [[battery]]-backed SRAM is the appropriate place for secure keys.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup>

## Servicing, dumping and preservation

Socketing decides how repairable a machine is. Where ROMs are socketed they can be reseated to cure intermittent contact, and simply pushing them home is worth doing before deeper diagnosis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> Where they are soldered — as in the Tandy 102, whose ROM carries a 1983 Microsoft copyright — they are not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> Sockets are also the failure point: the Acorn Archimedes A3000 suffers corrosion on the ROM pins and in the sockets themselves, and its four ROMs are numbered so they can be returned to the correct positions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup> Date codes on those ROMs read 1992 against a board otherwise dated to early 1990, which indicates the RISC OS 3 ROMs were fitted after manufacture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup> Some designs anticipate the problem: the Cadex C7000 battery analyser places its ROM where it can be reached and swapped without disassembling the surrounding mechanics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup>

Reading the contents out before doing anything else is the correct first step with old equipment: dump the ROM, archive the image, and locate a reprogrammable replacement that can be fitted if the original fails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> A universal programmer such as the TL866 will read parts it does not formally list, because pinouts and algorithms are shared across manufacturers — the Compaq Portable ROM reads correctly despite its Mostek part number being absent from the device list.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup> A dump alone is not always enough to change behaviour: the Prema 6047's 27C256 image contains no model-number strings at all, so distinguishing it from a related model would require disassembling the 65C02 binary rather than patching text.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup>

ROM and RAM are, by a wide margin, the parts most likely to go obsolete over a product's production life, which makes them the usual trigger for a redesign of a long-lived instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 5 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Physical and electrical behaviour

ROMs are among the device families most vulnerable to [[electrostatic-discharge|electrostatic discharge]], alongside PROMs, CMOS and MOSFETs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup> Thermally they are undemanding: on a running 8085 kit the ROM measures about 35 °C, the coolest device on the board, against 47 °C for the active RAM and close to 50 °C for the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3zAWgNjx5Ew" title="Intel MCS-85 Design Kit THERMAL IMAGING">•</a></sup>

Electrically, the address and data lines into a ROM are among the noisiest nodes on a digital board. A near-field probe held directly over a ROM picks up some of the highest-amplitude broadband emissions anywhere on the assembly, well above what a general sweep of the board shows, and the ROM is a standard probing location when comparing two-layer against four-layer construction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Despite that switching activity, stripping every [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] from the section of a board carrying the instruction register, the RAM and the ROM produced no functional failure in a low-clock-rate TTL machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>
