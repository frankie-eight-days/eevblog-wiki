A low-pass filter passes signal content below a corner [[frequency]] and attenuates everything above it. In its simplest form it is a [[resistor]] in series with a [[capacitor]] to ground — a first-order RC filter whose corner frequency is set by nothing more than the value of that resistor and that capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-611" title="EEVblog #611 - Electret Microphone Design">611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> It is one of the most widely deployed blocks in electronics, appearing as a two-component afterthought on an ADC input, as a distributed copper structure in a microwave front end, and as a dedicated eighth-order instrument costing more than the equipment it is patched into.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

## Corner frequency, order and roll-off

The corner or cutoff frequency of a first-order RC section is fixed by the product of [[resistance]] and [[capacitance]], and moving either value moves the corner directly: doubling the resistance halves the corner frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-611" title="EEVblog #611 - Electret Microphone Design">611</a></sup> Time constants translate to frequencies the same way — a 50 ms charge time constant, the figure microphone manufacturers conventionally use, corresponds to an electrical roll-off pole with a −3 dB point in the region of 3 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-609" title="EEVblog #609 - Condenser Microphone Design Tutorial">609</a></sup>

Order determines how steeply the response falls beyond the corner. A first-order section rolls off at 6 dB per octave; a second-order section at 12 dB per octave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-169" title="EEVblog #169 - Samson StudioDock 4i USB Monitor Speaker Review">169</a></sup> High-order filters approach a brick wall — an eighth-order low-pass set to 50 kHz stays flat across the passband and then drops away almost vertically at the corner.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> Order costs components and complexity, so the choice is a trade between transition-band sharpness and part count.

Beyond first order, the usual construction is an LC ladder: a shunt capacitor to ground, a series [[inductor]], another shunt capacitor. A pi-section arrangement of two capacitors around one inductor is the canonical form, and Butterworth pi designs are standard enough to be generated from online filter calculators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup> Op-amp based active low-pass filters cover the audio and instrumentation range where inductors are impractical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-836" title="EEVblog #836 - Tour Of The Australian Synchrotron">836</a></sup> Switched-capacitor devices offer another route: an eighth-order low-pass switched-capacitor filter can be bought as a single part, as in the MAX7400 used in the Agilent U1733C [[lcr-meter]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-234" title="EEVblog #234 - Agilent U1733C LCR Meter Teardown">234</a></sup>

## Non-ideal behaviour

A real low-pass filter does not attenuate indefinitely. Above a certain frequency the response bottoms out and recovers, producing a notch shape rather than continuous roll-off — so a filter should be designed to place its deepest attenuation at the frequency actually being suppressed, since everything above that point comes back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup> Much of this degradation is physical rather than topological: parasitics in the response come from the construction of the filter, and the same [[schematic]] built with different layout and component choices behaves differently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup> Dead-bug or free-air construction, which minimises distributed capacitance, improves matters over a compromised layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup>

## Converting PWM to DC

Passing a [[pwm]] [[waveform]] through a first-order RC low-pass filter averages the duty cycle into a proportional DC [[voltage]] — a 5 V [[microcontroller]] output switching between 0 V and 5 V yields a linearly proportional 0–5 V analogue level, provided the filter values are chosen correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A 10 kΩ resistor with a 100 nF capacitor is a workable combination for a 100 kHz PWM carrier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> The resulting node is high [[impedance]], so it is normally followed by a voltage-follower [[op-amp]], which draws no input [[current]] and therefore does not disturb the filter it is buffering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

## Signal conditioning ahead of an ADC

Low-pass filtering is standard practice on the analogue path into a converter. In an energy meter the chain runs [[current-transformer]], burden resistor, low-pass filter, ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> Multiplexed cell-[[voltage-measurement]] in a [[battery]] charger routes divided taps through a 4051 analogue multiplexer and a low-pass filter before the main ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup> Around a [[current-shunt]], a low-pass filter removes switching noise and load transients before amplification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

Source impedance matters here, because converter sample-and-hold inputs need a low-impedance drive. A series resistance of 330 Ω in the filter is low enough not to cause a problem when driven from an op-amp output; higher values may need a buffer between the filter and the converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Feedback-based [[current-measurement]] uses the same idea, with low-pass filtering capacitors placed across the transimpedance feedback resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup>

Filtering also solves architecture-level problems. Zero-drift amplifiers chop at a specific frequency, and if that frequency falls inside the measurement band it corrupts the result — the original µCurrent chopped at roughly 13 to 15 kHz, squarely in the range of interest. A device chopping above 200 kHz allows an output low-pass filter to place the chopper artefacts entirely outside the operational [[bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup>

## RF front ends

Superheterodyne receivers place a low-pass filter between the input attenuator and the first mixer, so that only content below the intended input range reaches the mixer and generates the intermediate frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup> At these frequencies the filter is often built from copper rather than parts: a distributed element filter, in which the pads are capacitors to ground and the meandering traces are series inductors, forming a multi-stage LC low-pass in the layout itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup> Bowtie low-pass filters, named for their shape, appear at several points in a spectrum analyser signal chain to take the upper edge off a band.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> The trade is cost against performance — a distributed element filter is cheaper than discrete parts, and a redesign may swap a distributed structure for discrete capacitors and inductors, or the reverse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup>

## Instrument functions

Many bench multimeters include a low-pass filter mode for work on motor drives and other electrically noisy equipment, removing high-frequency content that would otherwise corrupt an AC reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup> The Agilent U1272A provides a 1 kHz low-pass mode on both millivolt and volt ranges specifically to reject switching noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Meter VFD modes are the same idea packaged for variable frequency drive work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CYm-4gbl1Zc" title="ElectroBOOM Stalks Sydney">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> A filter can be forced on rather than offered: some [[firmware]] enables the low-pass filter by default even in DC mode, which suppresses coupled RF but limits what the instrument will show.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

Universal counters carry switchable input filters — a 100 kHz low-pass keeps noise above that frequency from producing false counts, though it cannot help with noise inside the passband.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-961" title="EEVblog #961 - Monkey Debouncing">961</a></sup> Oscilloscopes implement low-pass filtering in maths and DSP: an adjustable-bandwidth low-pass on a maths channel visibly smooths high-frequency components and reduces amplitude as the bandwidth is lowered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-310" title="EEVblog #310 - Cheap Siglent and Agilent scopes">310</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> Absence of any such function is a real limitation on an otherwise capable instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup> Power-measurement modes apply a selectable cutoff — up to 100 kHz — so that a switching-noise-laden current waveform can be measured cleanly at line frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTHcxTst_RA" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz MXO3 3 Phase Power Measurement">•</a></sup> Field-measurement instruments do the same, using a low cutoff such as 2 Hz to strip high-frequency content from a [[magnetic-field]] reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJ6JG4eV0nY" title="BM036 Clamp Meter Magnetisation Test">•</a></sup>

Standalone programmable filters, such as the Stanford Research SR650, provide independent high-pass and low-pass channels; cascading a 10 kHz high-pass into a 20 kHz low-pass produces a band-pass response between those frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

## Audio

Loudspeaker crossovers are the archetypal audio application, splitting the band between drivers with a low-pass to the woofer and a high-pass to the tweeter — for instance a 1400 Hz Linkwitz-Riley low-pass at 12 dB per octave paired with a 4 kHz high-pass at 6 dB per octave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-169" title="EEVblog #169 - Samson StudioDock 4i USB Monitor Speaker Review">169</a></sup> A powered subwoofer carries its own low-pass filter on its amplifier board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - FREE Your Sonos Speaker! (HACK)">1519</a></sup> Digital audio devices with user-settable filter coefficients allow the same shaping in software, in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1569" title="EEVblog 1569 - A Very Ploopy Open Source Mailbag">1569</a></sup> Mixing consoles and PA equipment stack high-pass and low-pass sections around each channel for equalisation and for extracting specific tones from a line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-840" title="EEVblog #840 - Yamaha M3000 Mixing Console Teardown">840</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup>

## Measuring the response

The response of a filter is found by sweeping a source through it and plotting output amplitude against frequency. A spectrum analyser with a tracking generator does this directly, feeding the source through the filter under test and displaying the resulting curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-343" title="EEVblog #343 - Spectrum Analyser Tracking Generator Tutorial">343</a></sup> The same measurement can be made with a scope driving a [[function-generator]] over [[usb]], with the filter input on one channel and its output on another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LbqnHtNPt9Y" title="Siglent Oscilloscope - Function Gen USB Connection Test">•</a></sup> A flat reference sweep taken with the source connected straight to the input establishes the baseline before the filter is inserted in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> Instruments with built-in [[frequency-response]] analysis produce the plot on screen in real time rather than requiring the sweep to be tabulated into a spreadsheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-N63dJHbCdbk" title="Sneak Peek: NEW Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>

## Diagnostic use

A low-pass filter is also an investigative tool. Filtering the output of a 555 oscillator strips the 55.5 kHz carrier and leaves a 55.5 Hz modulation component that is otherwise invisible on the raw waveform — the filter separating two superimposed phenomena that share a single node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup>
