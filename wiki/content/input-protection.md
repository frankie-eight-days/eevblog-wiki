Input protection is the circuitry placed at the measurement or signal inputs of an instrument (or at any external connector of a circuit) whose purpose is to absorb, clamp, or interrupt energy from overloads, transients, ESD events, and user misapplication before that energy can reach the sensitive front-end electronics. In test equipment — handheld multimeters above all — it is the primary determinant of user safety and of whether an instrument survives a high-energy event; in low-[[voltage]] electronics it more often takes the form of ESD clamping at IC pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

## Threat model

Input protection exists against several distinct classes of abuse. High-energy transient pulses on mains wiring are the dominant threat for handheld multimeters: measurement category (CAT) ratings are defined by how many high-voltage transient pulses, up to 8 kV, an instrument can survive, so a meter marked CAT III 1000 V is actually designed to withstand 6 kV or 8 kV transients.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Sustained wrong-range application is a separate threat — for example, applying 240 V mains to an ohms range — which a protected meter should survive even if it needs minutes to recover, that recovery time being attributable to a PTC device heating up and cooling back down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-252" title="EEVblog #252 - Multimeter Ohms Overload">252</a></sup> ESD discharge into an input pin, overload pulses, and lightning strikes are the corresponding threats for general electronic inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup> Programming interfaces can present their own hazard: a device that normally talks 0–3.3 V logic may receive a 12 V programming pulse that destroys an unprotected processor or [[fpga]] connected to the same line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-134" title="EEVblog #134 - The Maxim Manipulation">134</a></sup>

## Protection devices

**Metal oxide varistors (MOVs)** are the principal transient-energy absorbers. They clamp the input at a defined level — multimeters commonly use two 900 V MOVs in series to clamp at roughly 1800 V, keeping the residual stress under 2 kV so the rest of the protection chain can do its job — and they dissipate the transient energy themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup> Physically larger MOVs absorb more energy before failing, so MOV size is a direct figure of merit when comparing instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> MOVs are sacrificial: in a severe overload they are designed to take the brunt of the energy and be destroyed, and a blown MOV alone, with no other damage, is the protection working as intended.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-84" title="EEVblog #84 - High Energy Multimeter Destruction">84</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup> MOVs do not have a sharp, instant turn-on; their function is specifically to protect against high-voltage transients, and certification testing applies large peak-energy transient [[waveform|waveforms]] to verify that both MOVs and meter survive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup>

**PTC devices (positive [[temperature-coefficient]] resistors, polyswitches)** sit in series with the input as a [[current]]-limiting element whose [[resistance]] rises sharply when heated by overload current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W2o8otvVML4" title="ZERO INPUT PROTECTION! Micsig MHO14 Oscilloscope/Multimeter">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-252" title="EEVblog #252 - Multimeter Ohms Overload">252</a></sup> A single PTC in series, with nothing else, constitutes only bare-minimum protection and is what one typically finds in pocket and budget meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup>

**Gas discharge tubes (GDTs)** and **[[pcb]] spark gaps** perform the same clamping function as MOVs, by arcing over at a defined voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> A spark gap formed from exposed PCB copper is one of the crudest protection techniques but is effectively free, since it costs nothing to add during board fabrication; it is designed so a high voltage arcs across the smallest gap rather than propagating along a trace into an IC pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup> Spark gaps appear in equipment ranging from pocket multimeters to 7.5-digit bench DMMs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-556" title="EEVblog #556 - Mailbag">556</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

**[[diode]] clamps** steer overvoltage energy into the supply rails or across a shunt. IC inputs are protected by diode clamps to the positive and negative rails: when a pin is driven above or below a rail, the diode conducts and dissipates the energy, and this scheme is universal from standard 74HC logic to complex microcontrollers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup> On instrument inputs the same function is implemented with discrete low-[[capacitance]] diode clamp packages behind series resistors, which is cheaper and easier than individual diodes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup> Back-to-back Zener clamps and Zener/transistor clamp pairs are common on [[oscilloscope]] and meter front ends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1734" title="EEVblog 1734 - Uni-T UT892 2000V High Voltage Multimeter TESTED">1734</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup>

**HRC fuses (high rupture capacity)** protect the [[current-measurement]] paths. Proper current-input fuses are 1000 V-rated ceramic types, and breaking capacity matters — on the 400/600 mA range a larger breaking-capacity [[fuse]] is a genuine protection advantage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Cheap glass M205 fuses or 250 V-rated fuses are a marker of inadequate protection, and an unfused 10 A jack is worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1420" title="EEVblog 1420 - Mailbag">1420</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-872" title="EEVblog #872 - Mailbag">872</a></sup>

**Fusible resistors and high-voltage [[series-resistor]] strings** limit current and drop voltage ahead of the divider network; multiple resistors are strung in series to achieve the required voltage rating and isolation, each device individually rated for only a couple of hundred volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup>

## Multimeter input architecture

The protection architecture differs fundamentally between the voltage/ohms jack and the current jacks. The fuses protect only the current ranges; they have nothing at all to do with protecting the voltage jack, which is a common misconception.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> On the current ranges, fuses do not blow instantly — they take seconds — so a properly designed meter places a [[bridge-rectifier|diode bridge]] across the current input as a belt-and-braces measure: the diodes conduct during the overload, guaranteeing the fuse has time to blow and protecting the [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> Heavy back-to-back diodes directly across the current terminals can also cap [[burden-voltage]] at about half a volt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> On the voltage input, the typical protected path is series input resistors and a PTC feeding MOV clamps to ground, with extra MOVs where a tap runs off to the [[range-switch]] and ADC input because that extra path also needs protecting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> Shared millivolt/milliamp and voltage jacks, or physically joined current terminals, are design weaknesses that bypass these separations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W2o8otvVML4" title="ZERO INPUT PROTECTION! Micsig MHO14 Oscilloscope/Multimeter">•</a></sup>

Mechanical and layout measures complete the scheme. High-voltage isolation slots milled into the PCB, with matching blast shields moulded into the case that mate into those slots, separate the voltage input from the fuses and contain an explosion if one occurs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Generous creepage spacing between traces, deep blast-protection grooves in the case, cutout slots under relays and high-voltage resistors, and routing the entire input section onto a dedicated riser board are all standard practice in well-designed front ends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-432" title="EEVblog #432 - Brymen Multimeters">432</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup>

## IC-level and low-voltage input protection

For logic and low-voltage signal inputs, the standard arrangement is a series resistor (for example 510 Ω) limiting current into low-capacitance diode clamps tied to the rails; low capacitance is essential so the protection does not degrade the measured signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup> The same combination of series resistance and clamping provides filtering plus protection on sensitive analog inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> Where protection is required, good practice is to clamp the energy right at the input pin rather than letting a transient propagate along the trace into the IC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup> A worked example of the consequence of omitting this: a CMOS op-amp (OPA2376) connected directly to an external input with no protection was destroyed by an overvoltage, failing shorted despite the device's nominally infinite [[input-impedance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-727" title="EEVblog #727 - How To Kill An Opamp">727</a></sup>

## Design trade-offs across instrument classes

Bench multimeters generally carry less input protection than handhelds — CAT II ratings rather than CAT III/IV — because they are rack- or bench-bound and not exposed to field surge conditions, though some bench instruments are exceptions with full HRC fusing, polyswitches, spark gaps, and MOV protection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup> High-attenuation inputs can make discrete protection unnecessary: a power analyser that attenuates its input through four dropper resistors per leg needs no explicit protection because no realistic transient can damage the attenuated node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> Conversely, minimalistic input circuitry on LCR meters and [[usb]] instruments — no MOVs, no back-to-back power diodes — leaves them comparatively easy to damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-229" title="EEVblog #229 - IET DE-5000 LCR Meter Teardown">229</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> Spectrum analyser front ends with unpopulated clamp diode positions are a recognised weak point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup> Sensitive metrology front ends are a special case: a femtoammeter uses a FET input stage with guard pins routed directly through the chip and low-leakage protection devices, since conventional clamps would swamp the measurement with leakage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

Equipment predating the CAT standards typically has little or no meaningful input protection — the concept simply was not part of designs of that era — and this is a standing caution when using vintage meters on modern mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-991" title="EEVblog #991 - Mailbag">991</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-802" title="EEVblog #802 - Mailbag">802</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-827" title="EEVblog #827 - Mailbag">827</a></sup> Analog moving-coil meters are an exception of sorts: back-to-back diodes across the meter movement make even cheap ones hard to destroy by simple overload.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

## Ratings, verification, and failure behaviour

Printed CAT ratings on budget instruments are frequently not independently certified and not backed by the internal protection the rating requires; a claimed CAT IV 600 V / CAT III 1000 V marking on a meter containing one PTC and no MOVs, with 250 V fuses, should not be believed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1420" title="EEVblog 1420 - Mailbag">1420</a></sup> Absence of any series protection on the volts/ohms/amps jack — input wired straight into a [[resistor-divider]] — disqualifies an instrument entirely from mains use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W2o8otvVML4" title="ZERO INPUT PROTECTION! Micsig MHO14 Oscilloscope/Multimeter">•</a></sup> Practical verification includes high-voltage overload testing, in which MOV-protected meters clamp cleanly at their MOV stack voltage (typically under 2 kV) while unprotected meters fail violently; a MOV-protected $2 meter survived repeated 6.9 kV electric-fence pulses with no measurable accuracy shift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1292" title="EEVblog #1292 - $2 Meter vs JVA Electric Fence Controller!">1292</a></sup> Destructive testing of unprotected meters shows the characteristic signature: charred boards, PCB tracks peeled straight off, and internal pressure sufficient to blow the range-switch knob clean off the case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-84" title="EEVblog #84 - High Energy Multimeter Destruction">84</a></sup> Protection devices are intended to fail sacrificially and locally; energy that gets past them destroys regulators and downstream circuitry, so the desired failure point is always right at the input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup> Applying 240 V mains to the ohms range and observing both survival and recovery time is Dave Jones's standing bench test for whether a meter's input protection is any good.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-252" title="EEVblog #252 - Multimeter Ohms Overload">252</a></sup>

## Comparative practice

Among handheld [[multimeter]] makers, physically larger MOVs and more complete device counts (three MOVs plus two PTCs plus fusible resistors on a dedicated input board) indicate superior transient-energy handling at equal CAT rating; two meters with identical CAT III 1000 V / CAT IV 600 V listings can differ substantially in actual protection hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup> Brymen's input sections are treated as the reference standard in the corpus — assessed as equal to or better than Fluke's on transient absorption — while the distinguishing feature of premium handhelds generally is complete device coverage (MOVs, PTCs, GDTs, HRC fuses, fusible resistors, isolation slots, blast shields) rather than any single component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> High input protection capability is not, however, what differentiates premium pricing: measurement confidence, longevity, and trust are the actual drivers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ay9wFQAW19Y" title="eevBLAB 91 - Why Are Fluke Meters So EXPENSIVE?">•</a></sup>
