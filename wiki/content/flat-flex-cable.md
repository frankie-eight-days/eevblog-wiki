A flat flex cable is a set of printed conductors on a thin flexible carrier, used to carry signals between the boards, modules, displays and mechanisms inside an assembled product <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup>. It is the dominant interconnect in consumer electronics because it is effectively the only viable way to design a product into a usable form factor: a camcorder of ordinary specification contains half a dozen to a dozen separate flat flex cables and assemblies <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup>. The same technology appears at the other end of the market as well, joining the digital and acquisition boards of bench instruments, the head assemblies of hard drives, and the driver boards of [[lcd|LCD]] panels <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1398" title="EEVblog 1398 - Western Digital RED 6TB WD60EFRX Hard Drive TEARDOWN">1398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup>.

## Termination methods

Flat flex is terminated in three broadly different ways, and the choice governs both cost and repairability. The cheapest and most common is a [[connector]] on the board — either a simple press-fit clip, or a lever-and-bar type where the cable is inserted and a hinged bar is folded down to clamp it <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1255" title="EEVblog #1255 - Apple iPad 5th Gen Screen REPAIR">1255</a></sup>. Some connectors are locking types <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup>. A retention clip pressing the cable against pads on the board is also used, and it is not always obvious by inspection whether such a joint is soldered or merely a pressure contact <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup>. In one design the board carries only pads and no connector at all, with a bracket and a screw pushing the flex down onto them, the screw doing double duty as a board hold-down <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup>.

The second method is direct [[soldering]] of the flex to the board, either by hand or as a separate production process <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup>. The third is hot bar attachment, in which a heated bar comes down across the end of the flex and reflows [[solder]] to bond the conductors directly onto the pads beneath <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup>. Hot bar joints are also known as tape automated bonding, or TAB connections <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup>. They are common where a driver chip is mounted directly on the flex — chip-on-flex — with the die flipped onto the flexible carrier and the whole assembly bonded down to the [[pcb|PCB]] <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-914" title="EEVblog #914 - Sony VAIO UX Micro PC Teardown">914</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1398" title="EEVblog 1398 - Western Digital RED 6TB WD60EFRX Hard Drive TEARDOWN">1398</a></sup>. A hot-bar-attached flex is not reusable in salvage, whereas a press-fit one is <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-547" title="EEVblog #547 - Dumpster Phone Teardown">547</a></sup>.

## Attachment to display glass

For an LCD, flat flex is one of three ways to get signals onto the glass, alongside through-hole pins and elastomeric zebra strips; the flex is bonded to the glass with a conductive glue and runs straight out to pins <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup>. It does not reduce the pin count in any way — all three are simply different interface styles to the same glass <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup>. Where the display is chip-on-glass, the flex tail is the only interface, and its pin field is documented in the panel's [[datasheet]] <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup>. Without an on-glass driver, every pin of the display must be driven individually, which for a 40-pin panel becomes a design problem in its own right <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup>.

The conductive [[adhesive]] bond is a known failure point. A photocopier fault traced to the adhesive holding the flat flex strip to the LCD glass was recovered simply by heating each bond in turn <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1100" title="EEVblog #1100 - Dumpster Photocopier Repair">1100</a></sup>.

## Pitch, density and electrical limits

Pitches run from coarse, low-density cables to modern fine-pitch parts, and both can appear in the same product <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-809" title="EEVblog #809 - Mailbag">809</a></sup>. Fine pitch is fiddly to work with: 0.5 mm pitch on a 36-way display tail is small enough to be an obstacle in prototyping, to the point that a header connector may be wired on instead <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup>. A common pattern is a very dense trace pitch at the panel end that fans out to a wider-pitch connector at the board end <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup>. Some sizes have become de facto standards — a generic 50-pin flat flex header on a 7-inch LCD makes many different panels interchangeable <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-g2kCBfUa9H0" title="Will this $5 Oscilloscope Work?">•</a></sup>.

Electrically, flat flex and its connectors have little [[current]] capacity per pin and per conductor, so power is routed through several conductors paralleled up <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup>. Powering a module entirely through its flat flex, with no separate supply terminals, is a design weakness in a part sold as a self-contained module <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup>. An ordinary flexible flat cable cannot provide controlled [[impedance]] — the conductors are simply tracks running across it — and while some suppliers now sell flex with EMI [[shielding]], shielding and controlled impedance are separate properties <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1694" title="EEVblog 1694 - Scarlet Rae Teardown + Design Discussion with Xentronics">1694</a></sup>. Where an application demands many connections, shielding and controlled impedance together, rigid-flex is required, and its cost has to be justified by that requirement rather than by appearance or convenience <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1694" title="EEVblog 1694 - Scarlet Rae Teardown + Design Discussion with Xentronics">1694</a></sup>. Shielding may also be added over an ordinary flex carrying differential pairs to a screen <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup>.

## Mechanical handling and strain

The flexible portion is the reliable part; the terminations and the transitions are not. In a repair context the ribbon itself virtually never breaks, while the connectors can be troublesome, particularly if the cable has been pulled out at the wrong angle <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup>. A flex that steps from a thicker section to a thinner one will shear at that transition if the assembly is opened carelessly <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-687" title="EEVblog #687 - EFTPOS PIN Pad Terminal Teardown">687</a></sup>. Large traces on a hard drive flex showed chunks missing around a bend, consistent with mechanical stress rather than any impedance feature <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1398" title="EEVblog 1398 - Western Digital RED 6TB WD60EFRX Hard Drive TEARDOWN">1398</a></sup>. When a panel is folded out of its bezel for service, keeping stress off the flat flexes is the first concern, because the whole screen hinges on the flex drivers <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1758" title="EEVblog 1758 - A Greek Dumpster Tragedy">1758</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OdovWOP7ik4" title="OF COURSE this happened">•</a></sup>.

Manufacturers add stiffeners and restraints for the same reason. A clear fiberglass stiffening plate glued to the back of a camcorder flex gives it rigidity over its unsupported length <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup>. Tape is routinely used to hold cables and connectors down so they cannot flap around <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup>. One design goes as far as a dedicated PCB carrying no traces and no components whose sole purpose is to retain a flat flex and adjacent wiring with clips <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-378" title="EEVblog #378 - Dumpster Diving Teardown Repair">378</a></sup>.

## Failure modes and repair

Fine cracks in the conductors are a common fault, and they can be small enough to need magnification to see <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-736" title="EEVBlog #736 - World&#x27;s First IR Thermal Camera Watch">736</a></sup>. With enough spare length, a broken flex can be cut across with scissors, the white covering scraped away to expose fresh conductor, and solder applied at very low temperature to rejoin the breaks — a delicate operation in which excess heat burns straight through the carrier <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-736" title="EEVBlog #736 - World&#x27;s First IR Thermal Camera Watch">736</a></sup>. Attempts to repair individual traces in place, without that care, tend to fail outright <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup>. Hot bar attached flexes are the hardest case: there is no good way to fix them, and TAB connections are a known failure mode in instruments such as Fluke scopemeters <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup>.

Standard diagnostic practice on a dead display is to reseat the flex and inspect it for broken conductors before looking further <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup>, and to flex, tap or press on the cables and their hot bar joints with an insulated poker while the unit runs, watching for any change in the fault <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup>. A fault that gets steadily worse with time, and is unaffected by pressure on the flex, is not indicative of a flex problem, and attention moves to the T-con board <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup>. Suspicion of a damaged flex is not always right — one intermittent failure after a user upgrade proved to be a missing screw rather than flex damage <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1739" title="EEVblog 1739 - UNUSUAL FAULT! in a Beelink Ryzen 9 Mini PC">1739</a></sup>. Contamination is another mode: a leaking [[battery]] corroded the contacts of a keyboard flex to the point of destroying them <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup>.

Replacement is often the only option, and depends on obtaining the part by number or by cannibalising a junk unit with a smashed screen <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o4S-TkTuHZs" title="Samsung Galaxy Tab A Troubleshooting">•</a></sup>. Recovered flat flex cables have limited salvage value, though a couple are worth keeping <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-488" title="EEVblog #488 - Dumpster Dive Parts Salvage">488</a></sup>.

## Cost and design effort

Each flat flex in a product is a custom part that must be designed, and each generally implies a separate soldering or assembly production step <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup>. A moderately complex camera or camcorder therefore carries a large hidden engineering cost in its flex alone, alongside the mechanical parts, brackets and moldings it interfaces with <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-816" title="EEVblog #816 - Chinon Film Camera Teardown">816</a></sup>. The connector-based approach also adds bill-of-materials items and additional things that can go wrong, which is one argument for integrating multiple rigid sections into a single flex PCB instead <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>. At the margins the calculation is fine-grained: a dedicated flex carrying a single tactile dome switch to the main board is a cost that a right-angle switch on the board might have avoided <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-514" title="EEVblog #514 - $5 Mobile Phone Teardown - LG800G">514</a></sup>.

## Historical use

Flat flex has been used in cameras since the Polaroid instant cameras of the 1970s, which were among the first consumer items to use the technology for joining things together <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-625" title="EEVblog #625 - Retro Teardown: Sony&#x27;s First Digital Camera">625</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup>. It appears in a 1972 printing terminal running to the printhead <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1169" title="EEVblog #1169 - TI 1972 Computer Interfacing">1169</a></sup>, in a 1970s programmable calculator <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1153" title="EEVblog #1153 - 1970&#x27;s Programmable Calculator Teardown">1153</a></sup>, and in an HP calculator of 1979 design where it forms the battery contacts and the expansion module contacts <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-582" title="EEVblog #582 - HP41CV Calculator Teardown">582</a></sup>. A vintage HP computer uses a flex ribbon roughly an inch wide as a single large copper strap between boards, in a machine otherwise wired discretely <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup>. An early 1980s portable [[crt|CRT]] television has a flat flex made up and glued onto the back of one board to join it to another, an unusual level of effort for the period given the discrete cable looms used elsewhere in the same set <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup>.
