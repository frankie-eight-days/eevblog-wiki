Aliasing is the artifact produced when a sampling system is fed a signal whose [[frequency]] is too high for its [[sample-rate|sample rate]]: the instrument returns a coherent, plausible-looking [[waveform]] at a completely different frequency from the one actually present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> The essential point is not that the display becomes messy but that it stays believable — the trace is still a clean [[sine-wave|sine wave]], just the wrong one, so a capture taken without attention to the [[time-base|time base]] and sample rate can be silently and entirely wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> It is a property of sampling itself rather than of oscilloscopes in particular, and it affects every sampled instrument: scopes, logic analyzers, [[usb|USB]] acquisition hardware, and integrating digital multimeters alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Aliasing is one of the big problems with digital storage oscilloscopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>

## Mechanism

The governing requirement is Nyquist: the sample rate must be at least twice the signal frequency, and below that threshold the result is aliasing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Content above the limit does not disappear — frequencies fold back into range, producing components that never existed in the input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> The consequences extend past simple waveform viewing into any analysis performed on the captured record.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> In an [[oscilloscope]] the failure is normally provoked by winding the time base out, because at slow sweep speeds a fixed record length forces the sample rate down until the input is grossly undersampled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup>

The aliased frequency displayed depends on the arithmetic relationship between input and sample rate, which makes the effect strongly sensitive to the exact input frequency. A 10 MHz signal on a scope sampling at 2 GSa/s can appear as a 1 to 2 Hz waveform at 5 ms/div.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> Sweeping the source down through 9, 8, 7 and 6 MHz leaves the alias present throughout, but an oddball, non-round frequency behaves differently: at 8.1 MHz the trace aliases instantly, while 8.105 MHz merely shifts the apparent [[ripple]] frequency and 8.123 MHz or 9.12345 MHz smooths the display out so that no alias is apparent at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> The same distinction explains why 250 MHz on a 180 MHz instrument shows a clear beat against the sample rate rather than a random-looking artifact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup>

Display processing complicates interpretation further. A stopped acquisition can look significantly different from the same signal running, because the stopped-display algorithm and the real-time display algorithm need not be the same, and the stop lands on a different set of samples.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> Reconstruction algorithms can turn an aliased record into elaborate patterning that bears no resemblance to either the input or a simple low-frequency alias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I3FhAhxet7s" title="Rigol DHO800 Bug Testing">•</a></sup> Every scope handles the situation differently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup>

## Sample rate, bandwidth and memory depth

Two separate ratios matter. The first is sample rate against front-end [[bandwidth]]. A four-channel 100 MHz scope that splits 1 GSa/s across all channels delivers 250 MSa/s, or 2.5 times its bandwidth — in theory just enough to prevent aliasing given sin(x)/x interpolation, but marginal, with pulse waveforms and similar signals the likely casualties of running full bandwidth on all channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> The same 2.5-samples-per-cycle figure on another instrument is judged technically adequate and not an aliasing risk, while a good scope would provide roughly ten times bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup>

The second ratio is [[memory-depth|memory depth]] against time base, and it is usually the one that decides whether a given screen aliases. With only 14k points a scope aliases at slow sweeps and simply cannot do otherwise; with 14 Meg points the same signal is captured without trouble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> A 4k record aliases where 500k does not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> Reducing an Owon's record to 10k produces wobble even while the waveform is still nominally on screen, and at 1k the trace is gone entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> The MDO4000 aliases readily at short records but not when a 1 Meg depth is selected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> The pathological case is an instrument with no acquisition memory beyond the display buffer — roughly 320 samples across — which cannot be zoomed and aliases a 100 MHz input outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup>

Automatic memory depth is therefore the practical defence. A scope with auto memory depth selects a record length appropriate to the time base and refuses to alias on signals that defeat manual-depth instruments, and it does so even with any dedicated anti-alias mode switched off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Its absence is a genuine shortcoming: requiring the user to select memory depth by hand is what turns an otherwise capable scope into one that aliases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> A 20 Meg record makes aliasing hard to provoke but not impossible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup>

## Anti-alias filtering and warnings

Most oscilloscopes contain no anti-aliasing hardware filter, and the undersampled input reaches the digitiser unattenuated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> Where such filtering exists it is worth noting: one instrument switches an 800 MHz anti-alias filter with an audible [[relay]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lXvXBdTP7Tg" title="Haasoscope Pro - An Oscilloscope Space Oddity">•</a></sup> Some scopes apply anti-aliasing behaviour only in automatic acquisition mode, so the mode setting must be checked before concluding an instrument is immune.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> A separate mitigation is warning rather than filtering: at least one scope detects the condition and tells the user that the capture is aliasing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup> Most give no warning at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I3FhAhxet7s" title="Rigol DHO800 Bug Testing">•</a></sup>

A hardware [[frequency-counter|frequency counter]] is unaffected by the acquisition path and continues to read the true input frequency while the trace shows an alias, because the signal is fed to dedicated counter hardware directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> A software counter derived from the sampled record is not independent in this way, and its digit count falls as the time base is slowed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>

## Instrument behaviour

Aliasing behaviour under a slow time base with a fast sine wave is a standard bench check, and many otherwise good scopes fail it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> Instruments that hold up include a 2000 X series scope which, given a 20 MHz sine wave, still does not alias at 1 second per division;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> a benchtop USB instrument that passes cleanly where many better scopes have failed;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> an $80 handheld that cannot be made to alias at all;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup> a 50 MHz handheld that resists aliasing with the available generator and has more than enough memory for its purpose;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup> and a handheld DMM/scope combination that shows no aliasing on a 1 MHz [[square-wave|square wave]] and simply displays nothing above its limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup>

Instruments that alias under the same test are more numerous: a 12-bit Rigol at slow enough time bases, which is expected behaviour rather than a fault;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I3FhAhxet7s" title="Rigol DHO800 Bug Testing">•</a></sup> an R&S HMO1002;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-793" title="EEVblog #793 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1002 Oscilloscope Hands-On">793</a></sup> a tablet scope where a 101 MHz input reads as roughly 43 MHz;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup> a LeCroy at extended time bases;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> a Red Pitaya;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #858 - Red Pitaya Extended Cut">2</a></sup> a $5 scope at 20 MHz;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1492" title="EEVblog 1492 - $5 Oscilloscope Repaired! + Oz GIVEAWAY">1492</a></sup> a Fluke 91 Scopemeter, which presents a 10 MHz square wave as an apparently perfect sine wave at 50 µs/div;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> and a handheld that aliases a 100 kHz signal, an unusually low frequency at which to fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> A 10 Hz sine on a low-end handheld is barely readable, and by 100 Hz the display is either mistriggering or aliasing outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup>

## Beyond oscilloscopes

Logic analyzers alias in the same way, and the effect worsens as the sample rate drops: at 8 MSa/s whole sections of a captured bitstream contain nothing at all, and even at 24 MSa/s a signal of two distinct duty cycles is misreported across most of the capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup> A USB instrument with a slow ADC produces heavy aliasing on a serial line, recoverable only by zooming in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup>

Integrating multimeters are subject to the identical constraint through their aperture time. Measuring a 1 V peak-to-peak 50 Hz signal at 0.2 power line cycles — a 4 ms aperture — produces a modulated reading that is classic aliasing, the sampling artifact of an aperture too short relative to the signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup>

Bode plotting by sweeping a generator into a scope is bounded at the top end by the same limit: the sweep must stay well clear of the region where the scope begins to alias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup>

## Practice

Aliasing is a condition to watch for in any sampling system, not a scope-specific quirk, and an unwary capture can come a cropper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> The defences are ordinary: keep the sample rate at least twice, and preferably around ten times, the highest frequency of interest;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup> use automatic memory depth where the instrument offers it, and select a long record manually where it does not;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> cross-check any suspicious frequency against a hardware counter rather than the trace;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> and treat a clean sine wave appearing at a slow time base with suspicion, since that is exactly what a successful alias looks like.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup>
