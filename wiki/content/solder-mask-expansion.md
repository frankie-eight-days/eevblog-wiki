Solder mask expansion is the amount by which the opening in a board's [[solder-mask|solder mask]] is enlarged relative to the copper pad beneath it: the mask opening expands past the width of the pad, leaving a ring of bare laminate around the copper before the mask begins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> It is set as a design rule in [[pcb|PCB]] CAD, and it governs whether a sliver of mask survives between adjacent pins of a fine-pitch package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Getting it wrong in either direction is a manufacturing problem rather than an electrical one — too much expansion removes the mask between pins and invites [[solder]] bridges, too little leaves a mask feature the board house cannot reliably print.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

## Why the mask between pins matters

Without solder mask between the pins of a package, shorts happen easily during assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Excess solder applied by hand, by drag [[soldering]], or by reflow has nothing to stop it walking from one pad to the next and bridging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> A mask sliver between the pads gives the solder a physical barrier, which is why a well-designed footprint with mask between the pins is one of the preconditions for soldering fine-pitch surface-mount parts quickly and cleanly with nothing more than a chisel-tip iron and fine solder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup>

The failure is visible in CAD before it is visible on a board. Where expansion is set too large, the individual openings merge into one solid block over the whole package outline, and a quad flat pack laid out that way will bridge across its pins on assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> The same condition arises from the opposite error — pads drawn slightly too wide for the pitch, so that even a modest expansion consumes the gap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-425" title="EEVBlog #425 - Mailbag">425</a></sup> A footprint that would have broken through in this way can be repaired by narrowing the pad rather than by touching the global rule, leaving a mask gap of roughly four or five thou between pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

## Typical values

Expansion is a small number, and the useful range is narrow. Around four thou is a reasonable general-purpose value for an ordinary board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> On a dense package it must come down: 0.05 mm, or two thou, is a very small expansion used where the [[pin-pitch|pin pitch]] leaves no room for more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> An expansion of 1.5 thou on a 44-pin quad flat pack of reasonable pitch yields a measured mask width of 5.5 mil between the pads, which is more than adequate to manufacture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Setting 0.025 mm on a fine-pitch part likewise leaves 0.1 mm — four thou — of mask between the pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup>

At the other end, 0.1 mm is a huge expansion despite sounding negligible, wide enough to swallow the gap entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> Pushing the value down to one thou to win clearance is possible in the editor but not necessarily on the panel: a board house is unlikely to be able to hold it, and will price accordingly if it accepts the job at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> The sliver left at that setting is negligible in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

Fabricators publish their own floor, and it is the binding constraint. One prototype service specifies a minimum solder mask opening of 0.05 mm around pads without stating a mask sliver width directly; a design carrying 0.025 mm expansion can be automatically rejected against that rule, sending an apparently finished board back to the global rules for rework.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup>

## Where the value is set

Expansion can be applied globally across the board, per component or package, or pin by pin, and which level is appropriate depends on the package in use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> Because it is a rule rather than a per-pad drawing, a single global change propagates to every footprint, which is what makes an out-of-range default so easy to ship unnoticed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>

Footprints brought in from another tool are a specific hazard: an imported layout can arrive with the mask opening cut under the entire part rather than around the individual pads, a state that must be fixed before Gerbers are generated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup>

## Checking it in 3D

The standard check is the 3D view with the component models suppressed, looking directly at the mask over the pads to answer whether mask survives between every pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup> Design rule checks can catch some of this, but a visual look at what the board will physically be is the more reliable confirmation, and it is a principal reason to use 3D mode at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Early KiCad's 3D mode omitted solder mask rendering altogether, which removes most of the point of having one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-254" title="EEVblog #254 - KiCAD PCB First Impressions">254</a></sup> The mask can be rendered in the intended colour, so the pads read as copper and the surrounding expansion as mask.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

## Vias and tenting

Expansion interacts with [[via]] tenting. A via left open near a pad allows [[solder-paste|solder paste]] to flow into it and short out, so vias under and around fine-pitch parts are force-tented — the mask is deliberately carried over the via with no expansion at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> Tented and untented vias are conveyed to the fabricator implicitly through the Gerbers, which encode the mask expansion; setting the tenting option at [[gerber|Gerber]] generation is sufficient and no separate instruction is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Expansion also constrains via geometry directly. On a 0.4 mm pitch [[fpga|FPGA]] a 0.3 mm via will not fit between the pads at all, and a 0.2 mm via becomes feasible only if the surrounding expansion is reduced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

## Deliberate expansion

Mask expansion is sometimes wanted at maximum. Fiducials are built as a plain pad — 1 mm in one panelisation example, with no hole — that simply has the solder mask expanded over it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Older wave-soldered assemblies used enormous openings with no mask between pins whatsoever, and had no bridging trouble doing so.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> One such board prompted the observation that "This has to be the world's largest solder mask expansion."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup>

## Reading shorts under fine-pitch packages

Absence of expansion explains a recurring diagnostic puzzle. Where a manufacturer has removed the mask under a quad flat pack as one large square, adjacent pins sit on bare board with no mask sliver between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> A designer routing a trace between two pads — a long-standing and legitimate practice — then produces what looks on inspection like a solder short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> The tell is position combined with mask state: a bridge sitting precisely in the centre of the pads on a package with no mask expansion is almost certainly a deliberate connection rather than an assembly defect, and this is a common design technique.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> It remains poor layout practice, since the trace could have been routed around the package once it was known that no mask expansion would be present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup>

## Fabrication and tolerance

Mask expansion is a designer's decision, not a fabricator's. A board house is entitled to make minor adjustments where process requires it — a specified 1 mm finished hole may be drilled at 1.05 or 1.1 mm to allow for plating — but altering solder mask expansion, enlarging pads, or removing [[ground]] fill falls outside that latitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> The board should be manufactured as sent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Realised expansion also depends on mask quality. Cheaper prototype boards use a dot-matrix-printed mask rather than a photo-imageable one, giving thick, coarse outlines; alignment around the pad can still be good enough that a thin sliver of mask survives between pins, which is acceptable for a prototype.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> Comparisons between low-cost fabricators show cases where the mask was neither expanded nor broken between pads, and cases of imperfect registration that nonetheless did not encroach on the pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-241" title="EEVblog #241 - Circuit Labs PCBs from New Zealand">241</a></sup>
