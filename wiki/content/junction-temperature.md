Junction temperature is the temperature of the semiconductor die itself — the actual junction inside a [[transistor]], [[diode]], regulator or [[led|LED]] where power is dissipated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> It is the quantity thermal design exists to control: [[airflow]], [[heat-sink|heat sink]] size, thermal compound, copper spreaders and vias are all only means to the end of holding the die at a safe value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> It is also the temperature that [[datasheet]] performance curves are plotted against, so it governs a device's electrical behaviour as well as its survival.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

## Why it cannot be measured directly

The junction is buried inside the package and cannot practically be probed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Putting a probe on the case gets closer, but the case is still not the junction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The working method is to measure what can be reached and calculate the rest: measure the case or heat sink temperature, take the junction-to-case [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] from the datasheet, multiply it by the dissipated power, and add the resulting rise to the measured temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Heat builds up in stages along the path — die to case, case to heat sink, heat sink to ambient — with each thermal resistance adding its own rise, so the junction always sits at the top of the ladder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup>

A worked example on a power transistor: with 10 W dissipated and a junction-to-case [[resistance]] of 3.1 °C/W, the junction sits 31 °C above the case. A measured case temperature of 87 °C therefore implies a junction of roughly 118 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Adding forced cooling to the same setup brought the case down to 47 °C, putting the junction at 78 °C — the same 31 °C differential, but a drastically lower die temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> In another version of the calculation, a heat sink at 70 °C with 3 °C/W and 10 W put the junction at 100 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

Which thermal resistance to use depends on the mounting. Junction-to-ambient covers a device standing free on the board with no heat sink; one part with a junction-to-ambient figure of 62.5 °C/W reaches 82 °C at just 1 W in a 20 °C room, hot enough to burn a finger on contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Once a heat sink is attached, the junction-to-case figure is the one that feeds the calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

Some devices report their own junction temperature through an on-chip PN junction used as a [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]]. Such sensors are crude but adequate, and respond visibly to something as simple as a finger placed on the package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup>

## Choosing a target

There is no universal correct junction temperature; the target is usually a ballpark engineering choice, with something around 80 °C a common aim, on the reasoning that every 10 °C of margin below the limit buys reliability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Absolute maximum ratings sit far higher — a semiconductor might be rated for 120 °C absolute maximum junction temperature, an [[lm317|LM317]] for around 125 °C, a power [[mosfet|MOSFET]] for 175 °C junction and storage — but designing to the absolute maximum defeats the point, particularly for equipment intended for long industrial service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup>

Manufacturers also impose secondary limits that must be read alongside the junction rating. An 80 W LED module rated at 4.8 A maximum forward [[current]] and 115 °C maximum junction temperature also carried fine print requiring the aluminium [[pcb|PCB]] to stay below 85 °C, which in turn constrains how hot the heat sink behind it may run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup>

The design lever is the number of thermal resistances in series. Going straight from case to heat sink, avoiding intermediate vias, sil-pads and heat transfer bars, keeps the whole system at a lower temperature and gets heat away from the junction efficiently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Surface-mount parts in small handheld enclosures make this considerably harder, since the usual escape route of a fan is unavailable when only a few watts are involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup>

## Effect on electrical behaviour

Datasheet curves are frequently plotted against junction temperature, not ambient, and treating the two as the same is a standard trap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> A specification quoted at 25 °C refers to the die, not the room; a supply dissipating real power can easily push its junction to 100 °C or beyond while the lab stays at 25 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> Amplifier [[offset-voltage|offset voltage]] is one casualty — a part specified at roughly 0.5 mV of offset at 1 A load drifts to about 0.75 mV once the junction reaches 125 °C, which matters in precision designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> MOSFET gate-source [[threshold-voltage|threshold voltage]] is another, characterised in datasheets explicitly as a function of junction temperature; where a device runs cold and dissipates almost nothing, the junction stays near ambient and the curve can be ignored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>

Zener diodes are a particularly direct case because they are typically used as power devices and heat themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Their dynamic resistance varies with both current and junction temperature, so self-heating feeds back into the regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Dissipating a quarter of a watt in a small package — 5.1 V at 50 mA — is enough to raise the junction and shift the dynamic resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> With the [[series-resistor|series resistor]] dropped to around 100 Ω and the input taken to 15 V, the output rose from 5.22 V to 5.47 V as the junction heated, against roughly 100 mV of movement with a 1 kΩ [[resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

Passive components are exempt from this class of limit. An [[inductor]] dissipating power in the same converter has no maximum junction temperature to violate and is correspondingly more tolerant than the active semiconductors around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

## Design failures

Excessive junction temperature is a recurring finding in commercial equipment. A bench [[power-supply|power supply]] using an ST LM317 rated to roughly 125 °C junction was measured with its heat sink already over 100 °C; with 5 °C/W and 5 W dissipated, the junction sat at least 25 °C above that, comfortably past the rating even with the intended airflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> The same design used thin tabs offering little [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> An [[electronic-load|electronic load]]'s linear regulators were too hot to touch at 80 °C on the case alone, placing the junction well above that figure and with the lid still off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

A model-aircraft BEC regulator measured 161 °C on its heat sink under nominal rated airflow, against a calculated rise consistent with that figure, and far above the maximum junction temperature of the semiconductors it fed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Its MOSFET carried an absolute maximum junction and storage temperature of 175 °C, and the heat sinking sat on the opposite side of the board with only vias conducting heat through, adding further loss to the path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>
