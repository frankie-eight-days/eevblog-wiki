Voltage measurement is the most basic operation in electronics test, and the one on which nearly every other measurement is built. A [[multimeter]] has three fundamental functions — [[voltage]], [[current]] and [[resistance]] — and voltage is the one no instrument omits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> An [[oscilloscope]] is no different in kind: it is also a voltage measurement device, distinguished only by displaying voltage with respect to time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> Wattmeters, power analysers, LCR meters and electronic loads are all voltage measurements with something else layered on top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-705" title="EEVblog #705 - Mailbag">705</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

## The golden rule of troubleshooting

The first rule of fault-finding is to measure the voltages, and it is stated so often that it functions as a rule of the bench rather than as advice. It appears in several wordings — "thou shall measure voltages",<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup> "Thou shalt measure voltages",<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> "thou shalt check voltages",<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-147" title="EEVblog #147 - Agilent 3000 $12000 Smoke">147</a></sup> "thou shall test voltages"<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup> — but always as rule number one, ahead of any theorising about which chip has failed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-507" title="EEVblog #507 - The First ARM Processor Computer - Acorn Archimedes A3000">507</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup>

The reasoning is a probability argument. A dead-screen, dead-everything failure is far more likely to be a supply problem than a [[microcontroller]] fault, so the rails are where the search starts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup> The rule extends to [[ground]] and power pins specifically, because a chip missing its VCC pin or its ground pin can still appear to work through its ESD protection diodes, producing a fault that looks like anything but a missing supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

The rule is most often cited retrospectively, after a repair took longer than it should have. An Onkyo receiver's fault was a dead −35 V rail feeding a [[vacuum-fluorescent-display|vacuum fluorescent display]], caused by an open carbon film [[resistor]] — and it was the last supply measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1395" title="EEVblog 1395 - Onkyo Repair SUCCESS">1395</a></sup> A JBL monitor repair would have reached its conclusion faster had every pin been measured early, though the fine [[pin-pitch|pin pitch]] made probing genuinely risky.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> On a Data I/O programmer, the voltages were simply forgotten in the excitement of the repair; measured afterwards, all the test points were fine, which at least eliminated the supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup> The corollary rules that accumulate around it are of the same character: never assume something is overloaded, actually measure it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> and understand how the equipment works before trusting what it tells you.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup>

Where measurement is awkward, the practical form of the rule is to measure the easy things first — the points that can be reached without disassembly — and only then work toward the hard ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup>

## Where the probe goes

A voltage reading is only meaningful at the point it was taken, and most measurement errors are errors of location rather than of instrument.

Probing a regulator means probing right on the input and output terminals, with the ground lead on the ground pin, because anything further away includes the drop caused by load current in the wires or [[pcb|PCB]] traces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The magnitude is not academic: a model-aircraft regulator reading 6.18 V at the load terminals was still holding 6.55 V measured directly on the brick's own output, the difference being loss in the connecting leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> The extreme form of this principle is the four-wire technique, where a separate pair of sense wires carries no current and the voltmeter reads directly across the component itself, nulling out the test lead resistance entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup>

The same logic governs [[battery]] testing. Cells that measure adequately out of circuit may not deliver at the load, so the voltage must be probed at the contacts where the load actually sees it — a device that runs from a bench supply all the way down to 0.9 V may still refuse to run from two cells totalling 2.5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> Measured properly at the right point, a bench supply and a battery are equivalent sources.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>

## Ground reference

Every voltage is a difference, so a wrong ground reference produces a wrong reading with no outward sign of error. Tracing a supposed ground back to a [[connector]] that was not actually ground has sent a measurement session down a long dead end, resolved only when the [[schematic]]'s ground pins turned out not to match the physical board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XUyjRm1Upjs" title="That moment you realise the Schematic &amp; PCB don&#x27;t match">•</a></sup> A prototype that read a healthy 5 V directly on its own pins was nonetheless failing from a classic system ground problem, invisible until the ground connection itself was changed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup>

The practical response is to establish a known reference first: identify the ground point, verify it by continuity, and if necessary [[solder]] on a dedicated ground test point so the probe can move freely around the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-135" title="EEVblog #135 - Kindle Case Mythbusting">135</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-297" title="EEVblog #297 - Canon LANC Bus Reverse Engineering">297</a></sup> For the same reason, node voltages in a network are only defined once a reference node has been chosen — in a resistor cube, symmetric nodes measured against a common reference read identically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>

## Test points and service documentation

Marked, accessible voltage test points make the golden rule cheap to follow, and their absence makes it expensive. On an HP dynamic signal analyser, the lack of accessible marked test points on the top of the board meant time was spent suspecting the crystal and reset lines before the supplies were properly checked; the eventual measurement required [[soldering]] pins onto a 79L12 regulator to get at its input and output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup> Reference voltages, supply rails and DC quiescent voltages are the correct first pass whenever they can be reached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup>

The best service documentation annotates the schematic with the voltages themselves. The Sony D50 schematic carries voltage test points marked with their expected values — 2.8 V and 2.7 V DC bias among them — supporting a procedure of measuring supplies first and then working through the DC bias levels point by point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup> Locating the voltage test points is the first move on an unfamiliar board even before the circuit is understood.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup>

## Reading the result

A measured voltage is usually interpreted against an expectation rather than an absolute standard. Standard rails identify themselves: 1.2 V across low-ESR output caps is the expected core rail for a high-density VLSI part, even on a board with no [[silkscreen]] marking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TE3il-V6XCE" title="This is NOT a repair video">•</a></sup> Finding 3.3 V and 5 V alongside a crystal identifies a microcontroller section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> A +5 V and −5 V pair points at a 7660 [[capacitor]] voltage [[inverter]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup>

Deviations localise the fault. A protection [[mosfet|MOSFET]] pair reading the full 3.5 V cell voltage across it is open — a good cell behind an open switch measures exactly as it would with the switch removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RasOXxxEhCk" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL Investigation - Part 1">•</a></sup> A backup supply pin can be confirmed by measuring across the capacitor that sits on it, in one case 2.9 V present and therefore not the cause of a no-boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup> A battery reading 4.15 V at the terminals eliminates a suspected bad spring contact in one measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1428" title="EEVblog 1428 - Sony RX100 IV Camera Repair and then WTF!">1428</a></sup> A regulator whose input reads −19.2 V on nominal ±18 V rails but whose output reads −1.85 V has failed unambiguously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup> Barely 0.4 V across a cap where a rail should be points at a dead supply rather than a dead [[processor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1739" title="EEVblog 1739 - UNUSUAL FAULT! in a Beelink Ryzen 9 Mini PC">1739</a></sup>

Equally, a reading that is nearly right is still a fault. A 5 V interface reading 5.68 V is outside any plausible ±5% tolerance and warrants explanation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-297" title="EEVblog #297 - Canon LANC Bus Reverse Engineering">297</a></sup> A rail that has recovered from 4.5 V only as far as 4.83 V has not actually been fixed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-379" title="EEVBlog #379 - Yamaha RX-V557 Receiver Fix">379</a></sup>

## Instruments and resolution

Resolution and accuracy are chosen against the job. Bench and system-level voltmeters run from six-and-a-half digits — capable of seven-and-a-half with averaging in high-resolution mode, though only over GPIB rather than the front panel<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> — down to handheld meters where a 3.5-digit display with a manual ×10 multiplier makes 6 V read as .594.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-930" title="EEVBlog #930 - Mailbag">930</a></sup> Small-signal work needs the opposite end: a meter with 10 µV resolution and a 50 mV range is what makes thermoelectric generator output measurable at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> The available span of laboratory equipment stretches from nanovolts to a thousand volts, but chasing the last digits is a separate discipline that most bench work does not require.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s2KkgI-kyK0" title="The Signal Path Lab">•</a></sup>

Resolution should be spent where the [[dynamic-range|dynamic range]] is. In a bench instrument measuring both voltage and current, an 8-bit or 10-bit converter is entirely adequate for the voltage channel, because the voltage is known and roughly constant; the current is what must be resolved across many decades and many ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

Verification is by comparison. Two meters on the bench is the working minimum for serious measurement, one on the input and one on the output,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> and having more than one available is repeatedly what settles a doubtful reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Where two independent instruments agree — a scope and a meter both reading 10.01 V peak-to-peak — the accuracy spec can be trusted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup> Where a meter reads 5.07 V and the supply's own display reads 5.00 V, nothing has been established about which is correct without a third reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-482" title="EEVblog #482 - Retro Iskra Multimeter Teardown">482</a></sup> Against a proper standard the question is settled directly: a reference [[diode]] expected at 6.1730 V measured 6.173 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> Instruments that display a measured rather than a set output value are more trustworthy for this reason — the number shown is the actual measured [[output-voltage|output voltage]], not the programmed one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup>

Poor instruments fail visibly on voltage. A meter that reports 10.19 V from a single cell, and holds that reading stably, is not merely inaccurate but unusable, since a battery is about the lowest-[[noise]] source available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> Conversely, an insulation resistance tester's rudimentary DC and AC volt modes, with a single coarse range and no [[true-rms|true RMS]], do not make it a substitute for a multimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup>

## Loading and input impedance

A voltmeter is part of the circuit it measures. Meter loading is irrelevant across a low-[[impedance]] source such as a thermoelectric module, where the difference between a 10 MΩ input and something lower changes nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> It matters greatly at the other extreme: with a high-voltage probe across an electrostatic speaker output, the unknown probe loading makes the 40–60 V observed at 20 V/div difficult to trust as the true open-circuit figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1150" title="EEVblog #1150 - Electrostatic Speaker Teardown">1150</a></sup> Low-impedance measurement modes exist for the opposite purpose, deliberately loading the input below a [[threshold-voltage|threshold voltage]] to suppress ghost voltages in electrical work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

## High voltage and indirect methods

Mains-level and higher measurements are ordinary work for a suitably rated meter — 248 V on a nominal 240 V supply is a normal lab reading<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup> — and range switching between volts and millivolts is done by [[relay]] inside the meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup> Overvoltage behaviour is part of the specification: a meter presented with 600 V AC should read it and warn, not misbehave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup>

Some voltages are only accessible by measurement rather than by inference. The off-state pin voltages on a [[nixie-tube|nixie tube]] driven from a 170 V supply through a 22 kΩ resistor vary enormously from pin to pin — 24, 43, 47, 49, 69, 118, 122 and 123 V were all present on a single tube — so the driver's required standoff voltage cannot be assumed from the supply rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RXk_wr0g8UM" title="Nixie Tube Voltage Drop">•</a></sup> A solar string of twelve panels in series nominally at 456 V is diagnosed by first confirming that any voltage at all arrives from the array, since one failed panel takes out the entire string.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1217" title="EEVblog #1217 - My Home Solar Power System FAILED!">1217</a></sup> Even a battery can carries voltage: the outer can of a cell is connected to the positive terminal, so piercing the skin against a grounded chassis shorts the cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1350" title="EEVblog #1350 - Fake Energizer Batteries?">1350</a></sup>

Non-contact measurement at a distance is possible in principle. Laser voltage probes exploit Faraday rotation through air, where the angle of rotation is proportional to the Verdet constant for air, the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] and the path length in that field, allowing field strength and hence the conductor's voltage to be extracted optically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-263" title="EEVBlog #263 - Extech LP100 Laser Test Probes">263</a></sup>

## Logging and instrumented measurement

Voltage is the easiest quantity to log over long periods, and battery and supply characterisation depend on it. A meter's CSV export gives a reading number and the voltage; fixing the meter to a single range — the 10 V range, for instance — prevents it switching to exponent notation partway through the log and complicating the data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> The resulting discharge curve shows what a spot reading cannot: an [[alkaline-battery|alkaline cell]] falling away in a near brick-wall response at 0.8 V, with little useful energy below 1 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Chart auto-scaling is frequently the wrong choice for mains logging, where manually fixing the axis to 235–245 V makes both the variation and any data dropouts obvious.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1684" title="EEVblog 1684 - Awesome Logging Software:  Test Controller">1684</a></sup>

Purpose-built measurement hardware follows the same pattern at smaller scale: end-of-line test fixtures with ±10 V voltage measurement inputs alongside digital I/O and analog output,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1391" title="EEVblog 1391 - Mailbag">1391</a></sup> compact voltmeter modules monitoring ±36 V in real time,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> and dataloggers storing 0 to 15 V as 8-bit or 10-bit samples, the range extensible if the [[input-protection|input protection]] diodes are removed and the [[firmware]] modified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-294" title="EEVblog #294 - Mailbag">294</a></sup>

## Voltage as half of every other measurement

Most derived electrical quantities are a voltage measurement paired with a [[current-measurement|current measurement]]. A wattmeter requires both, brought in on separate voltage and current terminals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-705" title="EEVblog #705 - Mailbag">705</a></sup> A power analyser carries two nearly identical signal paths, one for voltage and one for current, and from them derives [[phase-angle|phase angle]], real power, [[apparent-power|apparent power]] and inrush current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> An [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] can be built on the voltage-and-current technique: excite the device under test at a fixed [[frequency]], measure the voltage, the current and their phases, and calculate everything else from those basic measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup> Modern power meters may split the work across separate processors, one handling voltage and current processing and another the real-time energy calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>

The generality of the measurement is itself notable: any two coupled classical analog circuits will always have some voltage measurable at the output, which is precisely the property that quantum systems do not share.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1316" title="EEVblog #1316 - Quantum Computing for Electrical Engineers">1316</a></sup>
