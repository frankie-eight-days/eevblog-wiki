Production testing is the functional verification of assembled boards and products as they come off a manufacturing line, distinct from prototype debugging and from final [[calibration]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup> It exists because assembly is never perfect: once a reflow thermal profile is dialled in, surface-mount [[soldering]] can approach 100% yield, but every manual step reintroduced afterwards — a [[connector]] plugged in, a screw terminal tightened by an operator who is not paying attention — is a fresh opportunity for escape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup> The engineering problem is rarely whether to test but how to get the per-unit test time and fixture cost down to a level the product can carry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup>

## Throughput and economics

Test time is the governing metric. A production panel of small precision boards can be taken through a full three-range functional check in under 20 seconds per board, including plugging leads, moving panels and swapping them out, giving roughly 450 units in 15 minutes, better than 200 units an hour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup> Costed against the hourly rate an assembly house charges for a line worker plus overhead, that adds very little to unit cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup> The corresponding judgement is that further automation has to earn its place: a full automated bed-of-nails fixture would be quicker still, but at low volumes the return on the time and money invested in building it does not justify it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup>

Handing the testing to the contract assembler rather than performing it in-house is normal industry practice, and becomes unavoidable at volume — testing 1,800 units personally is not a realistic use of a designer's time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup> This shifts a requirement onto the test fixture: it must be operable by someone other than its designer. Purpose-built jigs are shipped out to the assembly house, which then runs them as part of the line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-727" title="EEVblog #727 - How To Kill An Opamp">727</a></sup>

## Panelisation and panel-level test

Manufacturing [[efficiency]] and test efficiency pull in opposite directions unless the panel is designed for both. A naively panelised product — for example a 5 by 2 array of ten boards — lowers fabrication cost but forces each board to be depanelised, fitted with its [[battery]], powered and probed individually, with leads plugged and unplugged for every unit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Even four leads per board becomes painful across a production run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

The design-for-manufacture fix is to route power and test signals across the panel itself, joining the individual board traces in the breakaway rail so that the whole array becomes one electrical entity at the [[gerber|Gerber]] layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> A test board then mates with a single female header at the edge of the panel, powering every board at once and giving access to all the output test signals from one connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Doing this properly implies a panel-level [[schematic]] sitting above the individual board schematics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> The measures are cheap to implement and pay for themselves when test time arrives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## Test access: pads, pogo pins and connectors

The dominant physical interface is the bed of nails. Rows of bare test pads are placed on the board specifically so a fixture can descend with pogo pins onto all of them, avoiding the need to physically insert cables to reach the circuitry, which is faster and more efficient during the test phase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup> The pattern is visible across product categories: test pads [[ringing]] a payment terminal's main board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-687" title="EEVblog #687 - EFTPOS PIN Pad Terminal Teardown">687</a></sup> test pads on a wearable's [[processor]] board feeding through to a combined programming and test interface,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-412" title="EEVblog #412 - Jawbone UP Pedometer Teardown">412</a></sup> and pads around an RF module that mate with a bed-of-nails fixture to perform the RF testing, which demands expensive dedicated production RF test gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup>

Where a board carries a connector that goes nowhere in the finished product, production test is the usual explanation. Card-edge connectors on stacked boards with no backplane or bus joining them allow each card to be plugged into a test jig and verified before assembly into the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1705" title="EEVblog 1705 - World&#x27;s First Desktop Personal Computer: Olivetti Programma 101">1705</a></sup> An extra card-edge slot running down to the main bus can accept a dedicated production test or diagnostic card.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup> Edge connectors and multiplexed test interfaces are also used to hook up, test, calibrate and program a unit during production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-236" title="EEVblog #236 - FE-5680A Rubidium Standard Teardown">236</a></sup> The same reasoning applies to unexplained test headers and test ports found on spectrum analysers,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> [[impedance]] analysers,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1104" title="EEVblog #1104 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Teardown">1104</a></sup> wireless multimeters,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup> hard drives,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1398" title="EEVblog 1398 - Western Digital RED 6TB WD60EFRX Hard Drive TEARDOWN">1398</a></sup> cameras<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-937" title="EEVblog #937 - Retro Canon Still Camera Teardown!">937</a></sup> and vintage equipment retaining a test connector on the board edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-541" title="EEVblog #541 - Mailbag">541</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup> Dedicated test points may also be placed at critical signal nodes — for instance on a differential driver output — purely so the stage can be characterised in production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> Test pads that are clearly not an unpopulated connector, feeding an on-board soft-core processor, suggest a deliberate and expensive dedicated production test path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

Not every product relies on physical access. An instrument may carry almost no production test pads at all, because the strategy is instead to assemble the board, program the [[fpga|FPGAs]] and programmable devices, and then run thorough software-defined tests and calibration through the product's own interfaces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> JTAG boundary scan serves the same end, allowing pin states on an assembled board to be observed and driven independently of the [[firmware]]; the capability lives in the software package rather than in the programmer hardware, which is close to irrelevant by comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup> Some products ship their production self-test as part of an open-source firmware tree.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup>

## Pass/fail limits and measurement

Production testing is a go/no-go decision, so the measurement chain has to be built around limits rather than readings. A bench meter with programmable high and low limits will flag a reading red when a constant-[[current]] stimulus is driven outside the window, turning a precision measurement into an automatic pass or fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ux7WdK6oym4" title="uCurrent GOLD Kickstarter First Manufacture">•</a></sup> Instruments offering current, maximum, average and programmable high/low limit fields are directly suited to setting up production or other automated testing, particularly with an audible over-limit alert.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ruxzp0OMicg" title="Brymen BM787BT Bluetooth App First Impressions">•</a></sup>

The calibration burden scales with how tight the product specification is. Testing a unit guaranteed to 0.05% requires the test system to be calibrated to better than an order of magnitude beyond that specification, which is not trivial to achieve — a laboratory [[voltage]] standard, precision resistors and cross-checked instruments are needed, and a setup adequate for verifying hand-built units is not necessarily the right architecture for volume production testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ux7WdK6oym4" title="uCurrent GOLD Kickstarter First Manufacture">•</a></sup>

Coverage is a deliberate choice, not an absolute. Dave Jones's practice on a precision current adapter whose accuracy rests on 0.1% resistors is not to test every parameter of every unit, on the reasoning that such resistors either arrive in specification or are outright the wrong part; instead the first build is verified fully and subsequent units get a spot check on each current range before shipping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> Separate jigs are built for the different current ranges of the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-727" title="EEVblog #727 - How To Kill An Opamp">727</a></sup> Where a board is designed with right-angle pin headers on its underside, the intent is that it drops into an automated test system so that every unit is thoroughly tested.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-c0evM3-GTAw" title="Weird Uni-T UDP6731 PSU Fault FIXED. I got a DUD?">•</a></sup>

Test data can be retained per unit. A product identified by serial number may carry its complete production test record — the duration of the test, the measured values and the acceptance window they were judged against, such as 5.15 against limits of 4.9 to 5.4 — retrievable after the fact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup> More informally, results of production stress testing are sometimes written by hand on the heat sinks of a large [[power-supply|power supply]], recording the conditions the unit was run at.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup> For component sorting, LCR tweezers are the standard tool, used for select-on-test and for binning surface-mount parts in production; [[bluetooth|Bluetooth]] data acquisition allows every measured value to be logged and analysed statistically as parts are worked through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1375" title="EEVblog #1375 - Mailbag: IEEE">1375</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup> Interface isolation matters here: an instrument with a non-isolated [[usb|USB]] interface is unsuitable for production work unless an external isolator is added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup>

Semiconductor manufacturers publish the aggregate results of their own production test. An [[input-offset-voltage|input offset voltage]] distribution plot in an op-amp [[datasheet]] is the production test data for hundreds of parts, binned — in one case in roughly 0.3 microvolt bins, showing 40% of devices falling within plus or minus 1.15 microvolts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup>

## Instrument features aimed at production

Test and production engineering is a large enough market that manufacturers build oscilloscopes specifically for it, including deliberately limited two-channel models that facilities request over four-channel versions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1632" title="EEVblog 1632 - WHY sell 2CH Oscilloscopes? + Test Engineering">1632</a></sup> Several instrument features exist principally for this use. Mask testing defines a tolerance envelope around a [[waveform]], marks any excursion outside it as a failure, and accumulates statistics — total tests run, failure rate as a percentage, sigma, and timestamps — at rates of order a hundred thousand tests in a couple of seconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> Pass/fail measurement logging saved to CSV on USB serves the same role.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> A programmable default setup button, which returns the instrument to a stored configuration at a single press, is useful in production and test environments where an instrument is shared and must return to a known state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> Bench supplies with fast transient response are valued for production test racks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup>

## Design for test

Test considerations feed back into the architecture of the product. Splitting a design into separate modules rather than one large board reduces the yield risk: the larger the board, the more assembly value is destroyed when a single part fails to reflow properly, whereas a modular sub-assembly can be tested separately before it is committed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup> The same logic favours modular mechanical construction — a display assembly built as one self-contained frame is easy to test at the production stage and easy to replace, by design rather than by accident.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup> Test pads, panel test rails and edge connectors are all cheap at layout time and expensive to retrofit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup>

## Test engineering as a discipline

Designing and building one-off or low-run test gear for production facilities is a distinct engineering career, and a substantial market in its own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1632" title="EEVblog 1632 - WHY sell 2CH Oscilloscopes? + Test Engineering">1632</a></sup> Its output has a characteristic form: hand-wired, unoptimised, multi-board construction on rails, of the kind that results when only ten units will ever be built and per-unit cost is irrelevant compared with development time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup> Its input is the automated test suite — a product entering production may have a whole battery of automated tests covering on the order of fifty separate aspects of its behaviour, exercised through prototype and pre-production runs before volume manufacture begins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup>
