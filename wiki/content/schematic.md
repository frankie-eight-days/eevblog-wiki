A schematic, or circuit diagram, is the symbolic representation of a circuit: components drawn as standardised symbols and their electrical connections drawn as nets, with no attempt to represent physical size, shape or position. A symbolic diagram and the working implementation of the same circuit bear essentially no resemblance to one another — the [[transistor]] symbol looks nothing like the small black package that embodies it on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> The schematic is nevertheless the document from which the rest of an electronic design flows, and it is the single most valuable piece of documentation when a finished product has to be repaired.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

## Place in the design flow

The normal sequence is to draw the schematic first, run electrical rules checking on it, lay out the [[pcb]], run design rules checking, generate the manufacturing files, and then have the board assembled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> [[bill-of-materials]] consolidation belongs in the same chain, before or around the rules-checking stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Getting the schematic and all of the component footprints correct in the libraries is a prerequisite step: only once every symbol carries a footprint can the components be dumped onto the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> If the footprints and nets are all correct and the electrical rules check passes, that component dump should in theory only have to be done once, although PCB design is inherently iterative and there is sometimes value in starting layout before the schematic is finished.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

Selecting parts, drawing the schematic, checking it, and placing the components is roughly ninety-five per cent of the work in a design; routing is the small remainder, which is why automatic routing saves far less time than it appears to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-93" title="EEVblog #93 - PCB Autorouters Suck">93</a></sup> An autorouter has no access to schematic information at all — it knows nothing about what the circuit does, only about connectivity and geometry — which is why it cannot make the judgements a human layout engineer makes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup>

Changes can flow the other way as well. Swapping two pins during layout to obtain an easier routing path is a legitimate edit that is then back-annotated into the schematic so the two stay in agreement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-245" title="EEVblog #245 - PSU Design Part 10 - PCB Layout Editing">245</a></sup>

## Drawing a readable schematic

Readability is a design deliverable in its own right. Designators and values should be placed consistently — designator above, value below, for instance — across the entire sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> Components should be physically lined up rather than scattered, and wasted white space closed up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup> A schematic in which designators are strewn about and wires run straight through the middle of chip symbols is unusable regardless of whether the underlying circuit works.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1127" title="EEVblog #1127 - Mailbag Monday">1127</a></sup> Line weight matters too; excessively thick nets make an otherwise sound schematic hard to read.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup>

Symbol design determines much of this. When drawing a component symbol, the pin order should not simply follow the [[datasheet]] pinout: a device whose drains are split half on one side and half on the other in the datasheet produces an awkward diagram if copied literally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup> Grouping all the outputs on the side facing the thing being driven, and putting power and ground where they will not obstruct the signal wires, produces a compact symbol that the surrounding wiring flows naturally into.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup> Symbol detail carries information: drawing the open-collector or open-drain marker on the output pins conveys the electrical nature of those pins to anyone reading the sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup> Creating a custom symbol takes only a minute or two, so the absence of a part from the vendor library is not a reason to accept a poor one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup>

Structure at the sheet level matters as much as the details. A modular schematic, with functional blocks drawn out and descriptive boxes around them, lets a layout engineer see where each module goes and how the system fits together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> The failure mode is a schematic that is modular in name only and does not show the system flow, so the reader cannot follow a signal from input to output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Circuits whose switch contacts are scattered across several parts of the sheet in different positions are particularly hard to follow, oscilloscopes being a classic example.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup> Cramming a complete design onto a single A4 sheet compresses it at the cost of modularity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

On multi-board designs, reference designators are conventionally allocated in blocks per board — the 200 series for one board, and so on — specifically so that designators are not duplicated across the whole design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup>

## Notes on the schematic

A schematic is also the place to record design intent that layout cannot infer. Three electrolytics drawn in parallel say nothing about how they must be physically arranged, so a note on the schematic explaining the required layout configuration to the [[pcb-layout]] person is standard good practice — and serves as a reminder to the designer, who may be the same person months later.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> The same applies to star [[grounding]]: the diagram may show a star ground, and the layout engineer has to be told that it is deliberate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup> A boxed design note with an arrow to the relevant node, explaining that a particular ground must return to a specific point because it is the reference, prevents someone unfamiliar with the topology from destroying it during layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup> Without such notes, a problem such as forgotten power routing may only surface two weeks into a layout, when the fix is to rip the board up and start again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

Bypassing is a case where schematic convention and physical need diverge: specifying one [[bypass-capacitor]] per supply pin on the schematic because it is the done thing leads to boards carrying far more capacitors than the circuit requires, to the point of leaving no room for [[silkscreen]] designators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup>

A theory of operation, formerly a routine companion to a published circuit, is largely absent from modern project releases that consist of a schematic, a PCB file and [[firmware]] dumped in a repository.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup>

## Electrical rules checking

Electrical rules checking is the schematic-domain analogue of design rules checking on the PCB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> It detects conditions such as two output pins shorted together, floating pins, and a power net driven by an output pin — errors capable of releasing the magic smoke on first power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-953" title="EEVblog #953 - What Is Schematic ERC?">953</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5eJorvyA708" title="Altium Designer Serious ERC bug">•</a></sup> With online checking enabled, offending nodes are flagged directly on the sheet with a red marker, and compiling the project produces the full error list.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-953" title="EEVblog #953 - What Is Schematic ERC?">953</a></sup> Rules-check markers are conventionally annotations rather than editable schematic objects, and packages that make them real objects behave unusually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup> Errors deliberately introduced to exercise the checker have gone undetected in at least one shipping release of a major package, so the check should not be trusted blindly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5eJorvyA708" title="Altium Designer Serious ERC bug">•</a></sup>

Even a simple auxiliary board, such as active circuitry added to an otherwise wasted PCB panel, needs an associated schematic if it is to be rules-checked at all — which in a serious flow means individual board schematics plus a panel-level schematic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## When the schematic and the board disagree

Schematics are not automatically authoritative. A troubleshooting session can stall entirely because the pins shown as ground on the schematic — pins 19 and 20 — are [[led]] standby and key input on the actual board, with 14 and 15 being the real grounds; the schematic simply did not match the PCB, despite carrying the correct model number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XUyjRm1Upjs" title="That moment you realise the Schematic &amp; PCB don&#x27;t match">•</a></sup> In a similar case the discrepancy resolved as a symbol drawn upside down relative to the board, pin 1 corresponding to pin 33 and vice versa, because the schematic on hand was for a different regional version of the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> The general lesson is to follow anything that looks wrong rather than explaining it away: the initial symptom was simply a measured [[voltage]] that did not match expectation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup>

Internal inconsistency also occurs. One camera service schematic showed a backup supply rail entering an IC on one sheet while the sheet for that IC showed the connection going nowhere, a contradiction that cannot be resolved from the documentation alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup> Schematics can also contradict a component datasheet — an instrument schematic showing a device pin grounded where the datasheet shows it tied to the positive rail — in which case the instrument schematic describes the hardware and the datasheet does not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup> Revision errors are common enough in one's own work too, such as a [[current]]-sense amplifier output routed to the inverting rather than the non-inverting op-amp input on a revision B board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>

Silkscreen labelling is not a substitute for the schematic. Header pins labelled with their port names may still be shared with debug lines and connected to other circuitry on the board, so trusting the labels instead of reading the schematic produces signals that appear shorted to something else for no visible reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup>

## Schematics in repair

For fault-finding, a schematic converts an open-ended search into a bounded one. Without one, troubleshooting a complex analogue board becomes a matter of chasing one's tail indefinitely, particularly when the failure does not manifest as an obvious short drawing excess current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup> Concrete time penalties are routine: three quarters of the effort in one [[multimeter]] repair would have been saved by having the schematic, and a significant analysis error in another would not have been made at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup> In another case the absence of a schematic would have led to a full reverse-engineering exercise on the board before the actual fault could be understood.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> Where no schematic exists, questions that should be trivial — whether a break-before-make contact arrangement implements a power-on reset, what the voltage supervisor functions in an intelligent [[power-supply]] are, whether an off position physically disconnects the [[battery]] — simply cannot be answered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup>

Schematics are not always necessary. Faults that are visible on inspection, such as a failed component or a broken [[solder-joint|solder connection]], are found and fixed without reference to any diagram.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-884" title="EEVblog #884 - EEVBlog BM235 Multimeter REPAIR">884</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup> But it is at the point where measurement stops making sense — no voltage on a filter [[capacitor]], a rail collapsing under load — that the schematic becomes the next step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup>

Service documentation quality varies enormously. Some manufacturers publish complete service manuals with full schematics, including instrument makers whose manuals can be bought or downloaded outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-667" title="EEVblog #667 - Agilent 6643A Power Supply Binding Post Hack">667</a></sup> Some vintage manuals include full schematics, [[calibration]] and servicing information decades after the instrument shipped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup> Others provide extensive troubleshooting and block diagrams but omit the schematics entirely, leaving the service procedure to terminate at board-level replacement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup> Documentation practice from earlier decades is markedly more thorough: fold-out schematic sheets, transparent colour component overlays registered to the circuit, internal block diagrams of the chips themselves, and full theory of operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZBVyJaLAZDY" title="Scanning Vintage Computer Service Manuals - Tandy 1100FD Laptop Computer">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-381" title="EEVblog #381 - How Do Ultrasonic Delay Lines Work?">381</a></sup> Consumer television sets once shipped with a circuit diagram behind the back cover so that an independent technician could troubleshoot the set at reasonable cost, and early personal computers came with full service information.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-moa3YBdLepg" title="eevBLAB 88 - Right To Repair Questions &amp; Answers">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y0SNOsQOBYk" title="Right to Repair - Random Answers">•</a></sup> Semiconductor design kits of the period likewise included schematics as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup>

Related practice makes the schematic more useful at the bench: coding test points identically on the schematic and on the printed circuit board so that a point on the diagram can be located on the hardware immediately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> Boards laid out without labelled test points make tracing a signal back through multiple ribbon cables far harder than it needs to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>

## Withheld schematics and reverse engineering

Some manufacturers decline to release schematics at all, including to their own dealers, which forecloses both repair and any analysis of the front-end design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Yk_T-uCbg10" title="Returned BM036 Clamp Meter Investigation">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-964" title="EEVblog #964 - Mailbag">964</a></sup> Instruments whose schematics were never released can be effectively unrepairable when the design is complex.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a4Xpsenpd6E" title="The Amp Hour #643 - Calibration &amp; Repair with Ian Johnston">•</a></sup> The extreme case adds physical obfuscation: part numbers ground off every device on the board so that neither a schematic nor a parts list can be reconstructed easily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-956" title="EEVblog #956 - Countersurveillance Monitor Teardown">956</a></sup> Announced open-source releases sometimes turn out to contain wiring harness drawings and modular block diagrams rather than board-level circuits, which is high-level system information rather than what is needed to understand or repair the electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1581" title="EEVblog 1581 - Tesla Roadster now OPEN SOURCE?">1581</a></sup>

Where no schematic exists, it can be reconstructed. On a symmetrical design only one channel needs tracing, since the other is identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup> The immediate output of tracing is unreadable and has to be redrawn before it conveys anything — at which point features such as a screen grid tied to the plate, converting a pentode into a triode, become visible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup> A practical starting method is a high-resolution photograph of the board, printed out, with traces marked off as they are followed; the process is not conceptually hard but is very time-consuming to get right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup> A partial, hand-drawn schematic representing what the circuit is believed to be doing, mapped onto the physical components, is a useful intermediate even before tracing begins, provided its provisional status is understood.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup>

## Release and openness

Publishing a PDF of the schematic together with [[gerber]] files does not constitute open hardware: the original schematic and PCB source files must be released, along with the firmware, any PC software required to make the product work, the bill of materials and supporting documentation, so that the design can be modified and not merely reproduced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup> Releasing a schematic on its own is nevertheless a real, if partial, step toward opening a design, and a licensing regime that lets a company open as much or as little as it chooses — provided it states which parts — is preferable to an all-or-nothing definition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup> The open hardware gear logo carries no information about what has actually been released; a proposed remedy adds letters to it, S for schematic, P for PCB, F for firmware and M for mechanical CAD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup>

A schematic released only in a proprietary CAD format forces every reader to install that package merely to view the circuit, which is why a PDF should always accompany the native files.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> Providing a well laid out single-sheet PDF that can be printed and kept on the desk for reference is the practical standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> A schematic released without the design in electronic form, and without the software and mechanical files, can be close to useless in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1wrin8FYtEo" title="eevBLAB #72 - BUSTED! Medtronics Open Ventilator FAIL">•</a></sup> Projects promoted as open source have in some cases had no schematic available on their own website at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #858 - Red Pitaya Extended Cut">2</a></sup>

## Schematic entry as a design medium

Schematic capture has been used for logic design as well as circuit design. Older [[fpga]] designs were entered schematically rather than in a hardware description language, and modern tools will not accept those schematics, which makes migrating such a design to a current part extremely painful — the documentation is never sufficient to hand the design to someone else, so the original engineer has to be involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 5 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Learning to read schematics

Instructional electronics kits have long treated the schematic as the destination rather than the starting point. The early projects give a picture of the board and a wiring diagram showing which spring terminals to connect, and later projects progress to schematic diagrams, with explanatory text for each project.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-54" title="EEVblog #54 - Electronics - When I was a boy...">54</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1135" title="EEVblog #1135 - Science Fair 200-in-1 with Sagan">1135</a></sup> The transition point is explicit: the first projects are built by rote from a pictorial layout, after which circuit diagrams are introduced and grow progressively more complex.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1135" title="EEVblog #1135 - Science Fair 200-in-1 with Sagan">1135</a></sup> Teaching material introduces the symbols individually — a nine-volt battery, an LED, a [[resistor]] with its value marked, in one common first circuit a 330 ohm resistor between the LED [[cathode]] and ground — and then how those symbols connect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1163" title="EEVblog #1163 - Xmas Mailbag">1163</a></sup> Conventions such as the distinction between a junction, marked with a dot, and a crossing where two wires do not connect, are part of the same body of basic literacy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup>

Beginner kits are markedly more usable when a schematic is supplied alongside the assembly instructions and the component values are printed on the overlay, rather than only designators that force the builder to cross-reference a small schematic to find each value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup>

## The hand-drawn era

Before CAD, schematics were assembled physically. Symbols were set as dry-transfer lettering and glued down individually, with over a hundred separate stick-downs on a single moderately complex circuit diagram, and the adhesive used eventually browned and destroyed the artwork.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-1 - Talking Electronics - History - Part 1 of 5">280</a></sup> Manuals of that period nevertheless contain beautifully executed hand-drawn circuit diagrams, including entire digital sections drawn by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup> Hand-drawn schematics also survive as published artefacts in their own right, reproduced from the original notebooks in recreated project kits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup>
