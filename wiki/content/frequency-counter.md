A frequency counter is a bench instrument that measures the [[frequency]] of an input signal by counting its cycles against a precision [[time-base]], delivering far more resolution than an [[oscilloscope]]'s derived measurement. Its accuracy is ultimately bounded by its internal reference oscillator, which is why better instruments offer ovenized time-base options and an external reference input — almost always 10 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> Related instruments called universal counters or timer counters add period, phase, duty-cycle, and event-counting (totalize) modes, and are considered essential equipment in a fully equipped lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup>

## Operating principle

In the classic direct-counting architecture the input signal increments a counter chain for a precisely defined gate period; the accumulated count over that interval is the frequency. A counter with a 10-second gate visibly counts the input pulses for that whole counting period before displaying the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup> The gate time therefore sets both the resolution and the update rate: a 1-second gate updates once per second, a 10-second gate gains an extra displayed digit, and a 12-digit mode can stretch the gate to the order of 50 seconds per reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-459" title="EEVblog #459 - Counter Shenanigans">459</a></sup> Independent of gate time, the count carries a fundamental quantization uncertainty of plus or minus one least significant digit, depending on how the counter is designed and how its clocking is arranged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-459" title="EEVblog #459 - Counter Shenanigans">459</a></sup>

An elementary counter can be built from CD4026 decade-counter/display-driver chips: the input signal drives the clock pin, a clock-disable signal freezes the count so it can be displayed, and a reset pulse clears the chain before the next gate. With a one-second gate interval the display reads directly in hertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> The CD4026 integrates a decade counter and 7-segment decoder in one chip, which made it a staple of counter and clock designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup>

## Reciprocal counting

Direct counting loses resolution at low frequencies, since few input cycles fall inside the gate. Reciprocal counting reverses the roles: the instrument counts its reference clock pulses over one or more whole input periods and computes frequency from the measured period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup> This yields high resolution — many decimal places — even on signals of a few hertz, and some instruments select it automatically when they detect a low frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-325" title="EEVblog #325 - Rigol DG4162 Voice">325</a></sup> A genuine reciprocal counter measures 10 Hz or 1 Hz as readily as 50 Hz; an instrument that merely claims the capability betrays itself by jumping around or stopping updates entirely as the input drops through tens of hertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-325" title="EEVblog #325 - Rigol DG4162 Voice">325</a></sup>

## Time base and accuracy

Frequency counters are sold with a hierarchy of reference-oscillator options, and the option fitted largely determines how many of the displayed digits mean anything. Budget counters carry a cheap crystal, and their last two or three digits are meaningless; a standard temperature-compensated oscillator gives roughly 5–10 ppm; an oven-controlled oscillator (OCXO) does far better but can draw 10–20 W just to hold its internal temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> On a 10 MHz measurement, 1 ppm of time-base error corresponds to 10 Hz, so a 5–10 ppm standard oscillator already renders the last two digits of an eight-digit display useless, before aging is even considered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup> The standard time base of the Agilent/HP 53131A, for example, is a ~5 ppm crystal with a small adjustment pot and very little trim range, and a common upgrade is a third-party ovenized module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-647" title="EEVblog #647 - Agilent 53131A Frequency Counter Oven Upgrade">647</a></sup> Where more stability is needed, the instrument is switched to its external reference input and disciplined by a rubidium frequency standard; rubidium units output the industry-standard 10 MHz, internally disciplined around a much higher intermediate frequency such as 50.255 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup>

A counter referenced to its own 10 MHz output reads exactly 10.000… MHz by construction, regardless of the oscillator's actual error, because the measurement and the reference share the same source; the result is bang on, worst case plus or minus one least significant digit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-459" title="EEVblog #459 - Counter Shenanigans">459</a></sup>

Quartz reference oscillators are also sensitive to orientation in the gravitational field, a real phenomenon of the order of 1–10 parts per billion. Detecting it requires an 11- or 12-digit counter referenced to a rubidium standard; tilting the instrument or flipping it over shifts the last digits measurably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JobJswHcnLE" title="eevBLAB #8 - New Tektronix AGO3000 Oscilloscope">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-646" title="EEVblog #646 - Gravity Detection Using A Frequency Counter!">646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hE45DSUdD58" title="How Gravity Can Change Your Watch&#x27;s Time!">•</a></sup> This sensitivity only becomes visible once counters reach 12 digits, which is why it is not normally a concern in oscilloscope time bases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JobJswHcnLE" title="eevBLAB #8 - New Tektronix AGO3000 Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>

## Universal and timer counters

A universal counter is not restricted to frequency: its totalize mode simply counts how many times the input crosses its threshold, which suits tasks such as counting contact closures in switch-debounce testing (a pull-up [[resistor]] and a pair of batteries being enough to generate the events) or counting photon pulses from an [[led]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-961" title="EEVblog #961 - Monkey Debouncing">961</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup> Timer counters additionally measure period, phase, and duty cycle, and historically were built with dual input channels plus a high-frequency prescaled channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> An oscilloscope's hardware counter is not a substitute for event counting: it is not actually counting the pulses, it is counting the distance between the pulses, so random pulse trains produce meaningless readings and a totalizing counter is the correct tool.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup>

## Frequency range extension

Because the main counting chain tops out at some tens or hundreds of megahertz, high-frequency inputs are handled by a prescaler — a front-end divider that scales the signal down into the range the counter can process. On the Philips PM6672, channels A and B cover DC to 120 MHz while the C input covers 70 MHz to 1 GHz [[via]] a dedicated prescaler board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> Aftermarket prescaler modules were a popular accessory, extending a 60 MHz counter to 600 MHz by multiplying the reading by ten; the same technique took a vintage Fandar counter from its native 10 MHz capability to 200 MHz using an Intersil 7216 counter/display driver with a prescaled front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-991" title="EEVblog #991 - Mailbag">991</a></sup> The Motorola prescaler that appeared in the Systron Donner counter was effectively the standard input prescaler in do-it-yourself frequency counter designs from the 1970s through the 1980s and beyond.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup>

## Counters built into oscilloscopes and other instruments

Many oscilloscopes include a frequency readout, but there are two distinct implementations. A true hardware counter is driven from the trigger path: it keeps updating regardless of the time base, zoom, or even stop mode, and survives signals that are off-screen or buried in noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> A software counter computed from acquired [[waveform]] data fails as soon as the edges leave the display, needs roughly ten cycles on screen to produce any reading, and is generally the poorer instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup> Hardware counters have their own quirks: on the Siglent SDS1000X the hardware counter momentarily produces garbage for a second or two after the horizontal scale is changed before recovering, and on the Rigol DHO800 simply enabling the counter halves the [[sample-rate]] and maximum [[memory-depth]], an apparent capture-architecture limitation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> Oscilloscope counter resolution has also become a competitive spec, ranging from the Keysight 3000T's standard five-digit counter with an optional eight-digit upgrade to a 12-digit counter paired with a 0.05 ppm TCXO in Tektronix's gravity-compensated AGO3000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JobJswHcnLE" title="eevBLAB #8 - New Tektronix AGO3000 Oscilloscope">•</a></sup> Counters built into multimeters are typically limited affairs — on the order of 500 kHz — and a $25 [[multimeter]]'s counter may fail to read a 10 MHz signal at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> Function generators likewise embed counters; the Uni-T UTG9504T's built-in counter reaches 800 MHz on its external input and reports statistics at a one-second update rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cziiWo6Uh5M" title="$5000 Uni-T UTG9504T 500MHz Function Generator Partial Review (The Lost Tapes)">•</a></sup>

## Applications

The core use is checking absolute frequency and trimming oscillators and RF stages to frequency; where a design uses an off-the-shelf oscillator module good to 10–20 ppm, a counter is rarely needed, which is why it is a specialized rather than everyday instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Specific recurring applications include:

- Measuring an oscilloscope's [[waveform-update-rate]] directly in hertz by feeding its trigger output (one pulse per acquisition) into a counter and reading the mean frequency — for example 4 kHz, or over one million updates per second on a fast scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1583" title="EEVblog 1583 - Advanced Oscilloscope Triggering: Glitch/Pulse/Runt/Interval">1583</a></sup>
- Verifying clock derivation, such as confirming a processor clock of 3.266 MHz is a 9 MHz crystal divided by three.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup>
- Simultaneous measurement of output and intermediate frequencies on an RF bench, a reason to own two counters at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1410" title="EEVblog 1410 - Mailbag">1410</a></sup>
- Comparing 10 MHz references with histograms and stability plots over time, a [[calibration]]-lab and production task where multiple counter channels are needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1625" title="EEVblog 1625 - Electronex: Scope Wars: The Rise of Uni-T">1625</a></sup>

## Notable instruments

- **HP/Agilent/Keysight 53131A and 53132A** — regarded as the "industry standard" universal counter line, sold under HP, then Agilent, then Keysight. The 53131A is the 10-digit model; the 53132A reaches 12 digits (the display hardware contains all 12 digits in either case) and computes results 100 times faster at a given resolution. Readings are available over RS-232 at each gate interval.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6M-xXEn1_iI" title="FAILED PROJECT IDEA - 53131A Mod Board">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-455" title="EEVblog #455 - Auction Bonanza">455</a></sup>
- **Philips PM6672** — a 1 GHz timer counter with an eight-digit red LED display, channels A and B covering DC–120 MHz and a prescaled C input covering 70 MHz–1 GHz, offered with time-base options up to the ovenized PM9690/01.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup>
- **Systron Donner timer counter (early 1970s)** — an eight-digit Nixie-tube instrument with separate input-amplifier cards offering selectable [[ac-coupling]], trigger threshold, and attenuation; its manual reset makes the gating period directly visible as the count accumulates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1138" title="EEVblog #1138 - Systron Donner Counter Teardown">1138</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1139" title="EEVblog #1139 - OCXO Oven Oscillator Repair">1139</a></sup>
- **TTI TF930 and Marconi 2432A** — a 3 GHz counter and a second counter typical of a small RF bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1410" title="EEVblog 1410 - Mailbag">1410</a></sup>

## Hobbyist and historical designs

Frequency counters were a staple of magazine construction projects: a three-digit design built from CD4026s with a manually shifted display, small enough to fit inside a cassette case, and a published six-digit 40 MHz counter in *Silicon Chip* are representative examples.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-142" title="EEVblog #142 - Electronics Magazine Memories">142</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> A minimal modern equivalent uses CD4026 decade counters driving common-[[cathode]] 7-segment displays with an Arduino generating the clock-disable and reset gate waveforms; such a design is accurate to about 1 kHz with three digits, handles square waves only, and extends in range and resolution by adding more counter stages and digits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> Open-source front-end designs also exist to condition sine-wave signals such as a 10 MHz reference into the digital edges a counter input requires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-809" title="EEVblog #809 - Mailbag">809</a></sup>
