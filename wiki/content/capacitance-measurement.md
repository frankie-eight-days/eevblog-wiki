Capacitance measurement is the determination of a [[capacitor]]'s value in farads, and it is the function that separates an electronics-grade [[multimeter]] from an electrician's one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> It has become effectively a fourth core multimeter function alongside volts, ohms and [[current]], present on almost every modern meter either as its own dial position or multiplexed onto the ohms range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> The measurement is cheap to implement and enormously useful for troubleshooting — but the range, resolution and accuracy on offer vary so widely between instruments that the presence of a [[capacitance]] symbol on the dial says almost nothing on its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup>

## How a multimeter measures capacitance

The standard digital multimeter technique is a timed constant-current charge. The meter's front-end chipset drives a constant current into the capacitor under test and times how long it takes the [[voltage]] to rise to a threshold; from current and time the capacitance follows directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> The same analogue front end that generates the constant current for the ohms range is reused, with sense lines tapped off to read the voltage back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Because the technique is a charge-and-time process rather than a continuous conversion, capacitance readings update slowly compared with volts or ohms, and large values take a visible time to settle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup>

The same physics can be observed in reverse anywhere a constant current charges a capacitance: a bench supply set to a low [[current-limit]] ramps its output slowly while the output capacitor charges, and the capacitance can be derived from the ramp time and the current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup>

Dedicated LCR meters work differently, driving the part with an AC test signal at a selectable [[frequency]] and resolving the complex [[impedance]], which yields ESR, quality factor and dissipation factor in addition to capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup> They also require the user to choose a series or parallel equivalent model, and the accuracy specification depends on the range, the test frequency and the model selected — a single headline figure such as 0.5% is meaningless without them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup>

## Range, resolution and residual

For electronics work the useful span runs from picofarads up to a few thousand microfarads; values above that are rarely encountered on the bench, so a meter's maximum range matters far less than its minimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A lowest range of 2 nF is good, 20 or 40 nF is about the practical limit of usefulness, and a meter whose lowest range is 200 nF is of little value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> One picofarad of resolution is the mark of a properly specified electronics meter and appears on instruments across the price spectrum, from Keysight handhelds through the Uni-T UT71E to sub-$40 units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup>

The absence of a low range is the concrete reason a meter is classed as electrical rather than electronics-grade: a lowest range of 1 µF, adequate for motor run and start capacitors, cannot measure a 10 nF reference at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> A 1 nF minimum resolution is likewise near-useless for electronics even on an otherwise capable meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup>

Residual capacitance — the reading shown with the probes open — is the dominant practical error at the low end. Values of a few tens of picofarads are normal, but meters have been observed sitting at 230 pF, 300 pF and 130 pF with nothing connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> A relative or delta mode nulls this out and is therefore more important on the capacitance range than anywhere else; meters that show a true zero on open probes need it less, and meters with a large residual and no way to null it are effectively crippled at low values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> Lead and probe capacitance forms part of the same problem, and a body's own capacitance is easily large enough to shift a low-range reading — touching a device under test raises the reading measurably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup>

## Accuracy

Multimeter capacitance ranges are traditionally neither good nor accurate, and a proper [[lcr-meter]] is required to do the job well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A couple of percent is typical at best; 1% is very good for a handheld cap range and is what the top-tier Fluke handhelds achieve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15" title="EEVblog #15 Part 1 of 2 - Fluke 189/289 multimeter review">15</a></sup> Poor performance is common and often falls outside the manufacturer's own specification: mid-range handhelds have been measured 5 to 7% out at low values while meeting spec nearer the top of a range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Budget instruments specify 4% and worse, and some capacitance ranges are simply flaky, reading tens of nanofarads into an open socket.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1257" title="EEVblog #1257 - MORE! $9 0.02% AIMO Process Calibrator">1257</a></sup> Even within a single product family the cap range is frequently the weakest function and the standing criticism of an otherwise solid meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>

Verification on the bench is done against a known reference capacitor — a 10 nF standard is the usual working check, sometimes cross-referenced against a bench LCR meter or a decade capacitance box whose individually measured versus target values are documented.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1084" title="EEVblog #1084 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout Part 2">1084</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 2of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-510" title="EEVblog #510 - Mailbag">510</a></sup> Discrepancies of a few percent against such a standard are frequently within the capacitor's own tolerance rather than the meter's error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup>

## Test frequency and in-circuit measurement

Capacitance is not a frequency-independent quantity, and the test frequency is part of the measurement. An 855 µF capacitor measured out of circuit at 100 Hz reads 820 µF at 1 kHz and behaves as a short at 10 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Handheld multimeters do not expose this choice; LCR meters do, and 100 Hz, 120 Hz and 1 kHz are the common settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup>

In-circuit measurement is possible and is one of the more useful troubleshooting applications, particularly on power supplies where electrolytics dry out or vent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> It is very often inaccurate but frequently gives a usable indication.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> The failure mode is surrounding circuitry: parallel components and other capacitors charging through the network give readings that swing wildly and vary with test frequency, with one in-circuit part reading 700 nF at 10 kHz, 1600 nF at 100 kHz and 1200 µF at 100 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> Readings that come in somewhat under the nominal value — 27 µF for a 33 µF part, 19 µF for a 22 µF part, 8 µF for a 10 µF input capacitor — are typical of in-circuit conditions and do not by themselves condemn the component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1ngqB4mxZOI" title="Smeg Oven Goes Clunkedy Clunk + Capacitor Go Poop">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1299" title="EEVblog #1299 - Dumpster Laptop REPAIR">1299</a></sup> Where accuracy matters, one lead is lifted; if the capacitor connects to a socketed IC, pulling the chip frees a leg without desoldering anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup>

Out of circuit the measurement is decisive. Film capacitors that had failed in service measured 103.5 nF and 19.2 nF at 1 kHz against a 220 nF nominal value — less than half, and more than an order of magnitude down, respectively.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1486" title="EEVblog 1486 - What you DIDN&#x27;T KNOW About Film Capacitor FAILURES!">1486</a></sup>

## Safety

Meter leads must never be connected across a [[voltage-source]] while the function switch is in the capacitance, [[resistance]] or [[diode]] position; the [[input-protection]] on those ranges is not designed for it and the instrument can be damaged or destroyed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> This is a documented manual warning, and it is a warning worth taking seriously: an LCR tweezer instrument rated CAT III at its inputs exploded in the hand of its user when probed onto 240 V mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> A professional meter should nonetheless survive the abuse, and 240 V applied to the ohms and capacitance ranges is a standard survival test that competent meters pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup>

A related safety argument applies to the dedicated two-pin capacitance sockets found on cheap meters. They are convenient — a capacitor plugs straight in without probes — but they are not found on top-quality instruments, for high-voltage and explosion-rating reasons, and are not considered an essential feature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup>

## Special modes and applications

A low-impedance capacitance mode, entered on some Fluke handhelds by holding the range button during power-up, is intended for noisy measurements such as the capacitance of long cable runs in an industrial environment, where ordinary meters pick up interference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup> Cable capacitance measurement is a significant enough application that its absence has been called out as a real limitation on bench instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

Capacitance is otherwise a notable omission on high-end bench multimeters: some 6½-digit units ship without it while competing Fluke and Rigol models include it, and at least one vendor has added the function to a bench meter by [[firmware-update]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup> The gap is conspicuous because a bench meter intended as a general-purpose lab instrument is otherwise well suited to the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

LCR tweezers automate the measurement for surface-mount work, auto-detecting whether the part under the tips is a [[resistor]], capacitor or [[inductor]] and displaying the reading without a mode change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> Tweezer instruments trade range for convenience — 0.1 pF resolution up to a ceiling of 400 µF is typical — and their capacitance mode will also light and test LEDs, flashing them more slowly than diode mode does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup>

The measurement is also used to characterise stray capacitance in physical structures rather than components: solderless [[breadboard]] contact strips, quoted in circulating figures at anywhere from 2 to 25 pF per strip, were resolved by direct measurement rather than by reference to a [[datasheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> Similarly, direct measurement of a 99.3 pF capacitor in free air and resting on an anti-static mat showed no difference between the two, disposing of the claim that mat conductivity corrupts capacitance readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-250" title="EEVblog #250 - Anti-Static Mat Myth">250</a></sup>

## Analogue instruments

Capacitance measurement on analogue meters was rare and is historically notable where it appears. The Metrawatt and a small number of other analogue movements carried a genuine capacitance range, complete with trimmers for the gain of the capacitance and high-resistance functions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> Such ranges require the internal [[battery]], unlike DC volts on the same instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-633" title="EEVblog #633 - Mailbag">633</a></sup> The alternative analogue technique — watching the needle kick and decay when a capacitor is connected on a resistance range and inferring the value from the movement's decay — is inferior to a dedicated capacitance mode in both convenience and accuracy, since it requires calculating from a needle decay factor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

Capacitance was not a standard multimeter function when many classic instruments were designed; late-1980s Fluke handhelds omit it entirely, and a 1981 instrument offering capacitance testing represented substantial functionality for its era.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup>
