A backlight is a light source placed behind a [[lcd|liquid crystal display]] so that the display can be read without external illumination. Because an LCD does not emit light of its own — it only modulates light passing through or reflecting off it — the backlight is what makes the panel visible in darkness, and in fully transmissive panels it is what makes the panel visible at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> In [[battery]]-powered instruments the backlight is almost always the single largest [[current]] draw, frequently exceeding the entire measuring circuit by an order of magnitude, which makes it the dominant term in any battery-life estimate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup>

## Compatibility with LCD type

Whether a display can take a backlight at all is decided by which of the three LCD constructions is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1425" title="EEVblog 1425 - Fluke Repair 2 Electric Boogaloo">1425</a></sup>

A reflective LCD has a silvered mirror layer embedded behind the segments, so ambient light entering through the glass is bounced back to the viewer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> That mirror makes the display incompatible with a backlight: light cannot be placed behind the segments and get through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> Edge lighting is sometimes possible, but a conventional backlight behind the segment plane is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> The compensation is contrast — reflective panels give the highest readability in daylight, and they are the cheapest, simplest and thinnest option because no light source is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> A sharp memory LCD running with no backlight at all can still show excellent contrast.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> Fluke's 27 has very good contrast from almost any angle but no backlight capability whatsoever.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup>

A transflective LCD is the compromise: it is partly reflective, so it works on ambient light, but it also passes light from behind, so a backlight can be added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1425" title="EEVblog 1425 - Fluke Repair 2 Electric Boogaloo">1425</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1703" title="EEVblog 1703 - µTimer Project Part 2: An E-Paper Like LCD">1703</a></sup> This is the construction used in most good multimeters, which is precisely why they can offer a backlight that the user switches on only when needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Where a designer intends to integrate a backlight into a small product, the transflective type is generally the right choice, and vendors commonly offer the same part in both variants — a Lumex part number carries a TR or TF suffix to distinguish reflective from transflective.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup>

A transmissive LCD has no reflective or semi-reflective backing at all and relies entirely on the light source behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> The backlight must therefore be permanently on, which means permanent power draw and no option to switch it off; without the rear light source the display simply becomes unreadable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Transmissive panels are standard for televisions and computer monitors, but are uncommon in small handheld products, partly because they are power hungry and partly because they are difficult to read outdoors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Their advantage is that they work in all lighting conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup>

## Panel construction

In a large transmissive panel the backlight assembly is a stack. A diffusion plate at the rear turns the source into an even white field across the whole panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> That light then passes a polarizing layer, enters the TFT panel, and each red, green and blue subpixel either allows the light through its colour filter or blocks it — for a full HD screen, 1920 by 1080 triplets — before a final polarizing filter on the front.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> The whole image is therefore nothing more than selective gating of backlight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup>

Small segment displays use the same principle in miniature. A backlight panel behind a [[multimeter]] LCD carries a pattern of printed dots whose function is to disperse the light evenly across the area, with a reflective backing behind that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-979" title="EEVblog #979 - Mailbag">979</a></sup> Uneven illumination is the usual failure of cheap implementations: budget meters tend to inject light at the edge, giving a visibly patchy field, whereas a well-executed backlight lights the digits evenly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Hot spots from the individual LEDs remain visible through a mediocre [[light-pipe|light pipe]] even on instruments that otherwise perform well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup>

## Electrical construction and interconnect

Because the backlight sits in the display stack rather than on the [[pcb|PCB]], it has to be connected across a mechanical boundary, and how that is done is a reliable indicator of design care. Spring terminal contacts built into the LCD holder, which mate against pads on the board when the assembly is screwed down, eliminate wiring entirely and represent good practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup> Flying wires tacked onto the backlight, by contrast, read as an afterthought and mark out poor layout work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 2of2">99</a></sup> Hand-wired backlight leads appear even in otherwise neatly constructed low-cost meters,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> and in the worst cases the [[soldering]] leaves a stray strand about to short across the backlight terminals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-856" title="EEVblog #856 - Mailbag">856</a></sup> Where a replacement LCD module lacks the original [[connector]], the backlight can simply be soldered on with wires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup>

Backlight power in older equipment is generated by a dedicated supply. CCFL panels use a high-[[voltage]] [[inverter]] mounted in the chassis, which carries enough voltage to give a jolt on contact and should not be touched while running.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-851" title="EEVBlog #851 - Mailbag">851</a></sup> [[led|LED]] backlights use a separate driver board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-564" title="EEVblog #564 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Teardown Repair">564</a></sup> In a television, generating the backlight supply — alongside the logic rail — may be the entire job of the main [[power-supply|power supply]] board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eCKRl_Txa18" title="Is This Dumpster Hisense TV Worth Repairing?">•</a></sup> Vintage laptops fed the backlight from their own switching regulator, with the cable routed through the hinge to the screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup>

Switching arrangements can be idiosyncratic. In the Brymen BM2257 the backlight is physically connected by the rotary switch as the meter is turned on, using extra contacts on the [[range-switch|range switch]] wafer and a bodge wire added across the board to reach the backlight terminal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>

## Current consumption

Backlight current dwarfs measurement current in handheld instruments. Twenty-odd milliamps is "pretty standard for a backlight".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> Representative figures: a Fluke 87 V draws about 1.1 mA on most ranges but 14.8 mA with the backlight on low and 33 mA on the highest level;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup> a Fluke 28 Series II takes 1.7 mA on DC volts and 12 mA on low backlight, 30 mA on high;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> a bench-style meter running 1.5–1.8 mA in normal operation jumps to 36 mA with the backlight lit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup> One handheld product measured at roughly 5 mA in normal use rose to 42 mA with the backlight on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> An Extech [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] drawing about 12 mA almost doubles its consumption for an extra 10 mA of backlight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> A Ziboo meter takes 1.7 mA on DC volts and about 14 mA total with the backlight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>

Since brightness follows current, a deliberately dim backlight is cheap to run: the Fluke 17B MAX spends only about 5 mA on its backlight, which is exactly why it is not bright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup> Manufacturers quote [[battery-life|battery life]] with the backlight off for the same reason — a specification of 130 hours continuous operation with backlight off from four AAA cells is poor for a meter of that class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

Because backlight use is intermittent, the practical treatment in a battery-life calculation is to work from the base current and allow for the backlight as a pulse load the battery must be able to source; a 36 mA pulse is well within the capability of any sensible battery solution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Where a design must be low power, the discipline is to omit colour and permanent illumination, keep a reflective monochrome display, and switch backlighting on only when it is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup>

Two design details waste this current. On the Fluke 28 Series II the 4½-digit mode is entered by pressing and holding the backlight button, which also turns the backlight on and takes an unnecessary pulse of current; a press-and-hold ought not to light the display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> Separately, a backlight timeout is a common configuration item, adjustable in one-second increments on the [[agilent-u1272a|Agilent U1272A]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> and an automatic backlight can be disabled outright on some meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

## Failure and diagnosis

Backlight failure is distinguishable from display failure by the fact that a dead backlight does not produce a blank screen. Ambient light entering the panel and returning off the rear reflector should still yield a faint image, so a screen showing nothing at all points to a fault upstream of the backlight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup> A useful bench technique is to cut the panel's awkward backlight connector off, attach leads, and drive the backlight directly from a [[current-source|current source]] at a nominal 50 mA when the correct drive current is unknown.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup> The converse observation is equally diagnostic: a television whose backlight is confirmed lit while no image appears has a fault in the signal path, not the lamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eCKRl_Txa18" title="Is This Dumpster Hisense TV Worth Repairing?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1657" title="EEVblog 1657 - DUMPSTER Samsung 75&quot; QLED One Connect TV">1657</a></sup>

Backlights degrade with age. The backlight in a Tektronix TDS3054 is dim and fades with age, to the point that replacement with a bright LED backlight is an attractive upgrade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-564" title="EEVblog #564 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Teardown Repair">564</a></sup> A retrofitted LCD in a TDS540D shows the backlight fading out as brightness is raised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1515" title="EEVblog 1515 - Dumpster Tektronix TDS540D 500MHz Oscilloscope LCD Upgrade">1515</a></sup> The claim that an ageing backlight begins to interfere with the LVDS lines, curable by wrapping the assembly in [[shielding]] tape, is not well supported.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup>

A backlight can also couple into unrelated circuit behaviour. On the [[bm235|BM235]], switching the backlight on produced an audible high-pitched whine traced to the piezoelectric effect in a [[ceramic-capacitor|ceramic capacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> The backlight also worsens the usable viewing angle of a display rather than improving it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-898" title="EEVblog #898 - LCD Contrast Experiments">898</a></sup> In the MacBook design analysed for its failure mode, the questions that mattered were whether the backlight rail was fused and whether a guard [[ground]] separated the relevant pins — both decisions belonging to the higher-level design engineers rather than the layout designer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup>

Backlight brightness can serve as an informal battery indicator: on a meter that holds its accuracy down to 2.6 V, the backlight visibly dies well before the low-battery symbol appears.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>

## In instruments

A backlight is a feature worth requiring when selecting a multimeter, since it is genuinely useful in dim environments,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> and its absence is a real mark against a meter at anything above budget price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup> Implementations vary widely in quality. The Fluke 87 V has a two-level blue backlight that is effective for low-light work in poorly lit areas of a factory,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup> the Fluke 117 an even bluish-white one,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup> a Gossen Metrawatt meter a blue backlight with large digits readable with or without it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup> and the Agilent U1272A an unusual orange.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> The [[brymen-bm787bt|Brymen BM787BT]] uses white rather than the orange of the BM2257.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TkmMitYnHUA" title="New Brymen BM787BT Bluetooth Multimeter Teardown (Live Recorded)">•</a></sup> A Fluke 287's backlight is one of the first functions to check when confirming a repaired meter still works,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-FgkD8K0Ssdc" title="Fluke 287 Repair - My Arse">•</a></sup> and a Fluke 28 Series II survived a drop of more than 30 m with its backlight still functioning.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-66" title="EEVblog #66 - Death &amp; Destruction of a Fluke Multimeter">66</a></sup>

At the weak end, the Extech 380193 LCR meter's green backlight is barely visible,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> and the Uni-T UT61E+ gained a backlight that remains faint even with studio lighting off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup> Poorly designed backlights are a recurring complaint across Brymen's range as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-432" title="EEVblog #432 - Brymen Multimeters">432</a></sup> Displays that are permanently backlit because they are inverse-contrast types, such as that of the ANENG Q1, need the backlight simply to be legible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

Larger instruments expose backlight brightness as a setting: oscilloscopes offer low, medium and high intensity plus a backlight saver,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> a Zoyi handheld scope runs its display at selectable levels from 20% to 100% with a measurable effect on battery life,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup> and a Rigol DP832 exposes backlight luminosity through its RGB settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup>
