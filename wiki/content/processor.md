In an electronic product the processor is the general-purpose programmable device that runs the [[firmware]] or operating system, drives the display, reads the keypad, and coordinates whatever dedicated silicon does the actual measuring, sampling or signal handling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1571" title="EEVblog 1571 - Nobel Prize Winning Mailbag. 121GW, Retro calcs, Assembly, and Discrete Logic">1571</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup> It is rarely the part that performs a product's headline function: in a [[multimeter]] the measurement is done by a dedicated chipset and the processor merely receives the result,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1571" title="EEVblog 1571 - Nobel Prize Winning Mailbag. 121GW, Retro calcs, Assembly, and Discrete Logic">1571</a></sup> and in a modern [[oscilloscope]] the acquisition happens in a custom [[asic|ASIC]] while the processor runs the user interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> Because the processor is nonetheless the thing that boots, blinks, beeps and moves mechanisms, whether it is running is the single most useful early observation in [[troubleshooting]] almost any powered product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog 1053 - Part 2 : IBM PC Jr Troubleshooting">1053</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup>

## Division of labour with dedicated silicon

Instrument architecture is usually a partition between a programmable processor and hard-wired acquisition or display silicon. In a Siglent 1000X oscilloscope the acquisition ASIC funnels data directly into a display ASIC/FPGA, bypassing the processor entirely; the processor handles maths and similar functions rather than [[waveform]] updating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup> The same split appears in a Rigol design where a custom ASIC does the sampling and talks to the 500 Mpoint memory, while a Rockchip device runs the OS and the entire GUI.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> Keysight's MegaZoom generation moved the display into the digital ASIC itself; on the higher-end 4000 and 6000 products a processor with a camera interface takes the ASIC's output as video and overlays higher-resolution information, because the ASIC's resolution is hard-coded and this was the only way to extend the chipset's life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032  Part 4 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

Where that partition falls has direct consequences for behaviour. On a Rigol MSO7000 the [[fft|FFT]] will not update while a front-panel control is being scrolled, because the same processor handles both the FFT and the user interface; architectures that put FFT and serial decoding in hardware do not stall in this way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> Model-to-model updates often change only one side of the partition: between the Agilent 2000 and 3000 X-series the processor is an identical part with an identical specification, while the Xilinx [[fpga|FPGA]] grew from a Spartan 3 500 to a Spartan 3 1200.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup>

Not everything can be absorbed into the processor. Oscilloscopes continue to carry 4000- and 7400-series logic alongside their processors and custom analog blocks, because there is a limit to what modern integration can swallow and a small circuit element from the jellybean logic families is still needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> The same argument justifies logic minimisation inside FPGAs, ASICs and processors, where extra gates cost silicon and add [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]] that lowers the maximum achievable clock [[frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lJ3q9RHIatU" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 5 - Karnaugh Maps">•</a></sup> At the other extreme, a product can omit a processor entirely: an all-analog [[electronic-load|electronic load]] implements even constant-[[resistance]] mode without digital switching circuitry, specifically so the load contributes no radiated or conducted [[noise]] to low-level measurements on the supply under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-749" title="EEVblog #749 - Mailbag">749</a></sup>

## More than one processor

Multi-processor partitioning is common. A GoPro Hero 4 Silver contains what amounts to three processors — two ARM Cortex-A9 cores plus an ARM11 — along with caches, [[usb|USB]] 2, real-time clock, UARTs, NAND drivers, DDR memory drivers and separate video and image DSPs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup> A bench [[power-supply|power supply]] design can split the work between an isolated USB-side processor handling USB PD, USB HID and serial communications, and a second processor doing primary-side housekeeping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1264" title="EEVblog #1264 - uSupply Software Development Setup">1264</a></sup> Cameras follow the same pattern, with a main board processor alongside a separate front-panel control processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup> Instruments frequently place a processor on its own board, separate from the measurement hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup>

## Reset and power-on

A processor held in reset does nothing useful even though parts of it are alive: with the reset line low the [[oscillator]] does start up and run, but the device cannot execute its program.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Forcing that reset pin to the positive rail in an IBM PCjr keyboard controller drew an extra 6 mA and brought the device to life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> The conventional way to generate the reset is an [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]] into a Schmitt-trigger input, holding the processor in reset for some milliseconds while the supply rises so it gets a clean start rather than doing funny business at marginal [[voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> Where a device relies on an external reset and that input has failed, no amount of pull-up will help — an intercom controller with a damaged reset input produced no initial reset pulse and would not run, having no usable internal reset of its own; briefly shorting the reset pin to the positive rail through a [[resistor]] started it and the [[lcd|LCD]] initialised normally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

Soft power switching ties the processor into its own power-up. A processor commonly drives the gate of a series power [[mosfet|MOSFET]] through a latching [[transistor]] arrangement, so that once it boots it holds its own supply on and the momentary switch is no longer needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f-LTv1GqCMw" title="Brymen BM786 Power On Fault Investigation">•</a></sup> A failure to boot therefore looks identical to a failed power switch. Products with a programming header often feed power directly through to the processor, bypassing the front-panel off switch entirely, which is why they can be programmed in the switched-off position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

## Supply quality, brownout and resets

Processors are sensitive to supply disturbances that most other circuitry shrugs off. In a Rigol DP832 an overheating regulator dropping out produced [[ripple]] that ordinary 5 V logic or a downstream 3.3 V local regulator would have tolerated without complaint, yet something in the processing circuitry reset on it; the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] did not plummet to zero or collapse to 3 V as expected, which made the fault look for a while as though the regulator theory had been wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> The opposite extreme also occurs: in a Stanford Research SR650 the processor kept running with the rail sagging to 3.9 V, far below the nominal 4.75 V lower limit of a 5 V rail, with the display visibly dimming.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup>

Inrush is a related mechanism. In a Goal Zero Yeti 400, connecting a mains [[inverter]] with a large [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]] drew enough surge [[current]] that the processor hiccuped — starting up, shutting down and retrying in a repeating cycle visible as a flashing indicator — while loads with little input capacitance and hence little inrush caused no such trouble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1707" title="EEVblog 1707 - REPAIR: Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery">1707</a></sup>

## Power and thermal behaviour

Processor dissipation is usually the dominant thermal load in a compact product, and is often the reason a case gets warm at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-734" title="EEVblog #734 - Giroptic 360cam Kickstarter Prototype">734</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-402" title="EEVblog #402 - Flir E60 IR Thermal Camera">402</a></sup> It is not automatically the hottest part, though. On a failed Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 board the whole assembly drew a very consistent 6.3 W at 5.1 V with no video output at all, and thermal imaging put the DC-DC converter at about 101 °C and the failed Ethernet chip around 90 °C while the processor sat near 40 °C — a temperature entirely normal for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-E1IqcGcZKHE" title="RPi CM4 Compute Module FAILURE Part 2: Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Comparing an emissive plastic package against a bare metal can requires correcting for emissivity, for instance by taping the metal lid, before any such comparison means anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup>

Where a processor is capable of thermally destroying itself, cooling is not optional: a single-board computer processor may throttle, shut itself down, or be damaged if worked hard without a stick-on [[heat-sink|heatsink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-883" title="EEVblog #883 - Orange Pi One vs Raspberry Pi 2">883</a></sup> In laptops and desktop machines the practical maintenance item is the same interface — clearing accumulated dust and replacing degraded thermal paste on the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1345" title="EEVblog #1345 - HP DV7 Laptop Repair">1345</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1357" title="EEVblog #1357 - Dumpster Diving Santa PC">1357</a></sup>

At the low-power end the constraint reverses. In energy-harvesting work a storage [[capacitor]] charged from a thermoelectric generator can supply only a couple of milliamps, and the regulator output feeding the processor may be limited to 4 or 5 mA, while the processor itself might draw 10 mA on wake-up and considerably more if run continuously at 20 MHz — so short active bursts must be supplied from [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] rather than from the harvester directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> A solar calculator's processor, by contrast, kept running with its 220 µF reservoir capacitor shorted out, indicating the cell alone was sufficient under that illumination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1094" title="EEVblog #1094 - Casio FX260 Solar II Calculator Tritium Nuclear Battery Experiments">1094</a></sup>

## Firmware and silicon defects

Every type of processor on the market ships with silicon bugs, and the errata sheet for the specific device must be read before committing to it in a design, so that a feature that does not work or behaves incorrectly is discovered up front rather than late.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-53" title="EEVblog #53 - Mr Murphy and Microchip PIC Silicon Bugs">53</a></sup> Firmware corruption is a distinct and real field failure mode: the leading explanation for one instrument that stopped working after passing factory [[calibration]] was that the flash in the internal processor had been corrupted or incorrectly reprogrammed after test, since the calibration itself checked out as still correct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup>

A processor that fails to boot can leave peripherals in their power-on default state. A multimeter that displayed every [[lcd-segment|LCD segment]] at once had simply never got far enough to send the command clearing the display; reflashing the processor through the on-board programming header restored normal operation with the calibration intact to within one least significant digit of a reference meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup> Across one manufacturer's meters the majority of observed faults have been outright processor failures, often failing in unusual ways rather than dying cleanly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f-LTv1GqCMw" title="Brymen BM786 Power On Fault Investigation">•</a></sup>

## Diagnostic use

Evidence that the processor is executing is the first branch point in most repairs, and it can usually be gathered without opening anything. A floppy drive seeking, a transport mechanism moving, an [[led|LED]] flashing, a beep on a keypress or a boot delay followed by a display turn-on all require the processor to be fully operational.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog 1053 - Part 2 : IBM PC Jr Troubleshooting">1053</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1425" title="EEVblog 1425 - Fluke Repair 2 Electric Boogaloo">1425</a></sup> Serial output is stronger evidence still: a device emitting a decodable 9600 baud shutdown message on power-off demonstrates the processor is working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup> Conversely, a device drawing its expected running current with nothing on screen and no serial activity narrows the fault towards the display path — a dead LCD, a failed [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]], or in older architectures a separate video memory chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup> Where a programmer refuses to detect the processor at all, the first question is whether the processor is receiving its supply, since even dead silicon would still show voltage on its pins; measuring 3.2 V on a rail that never reaches the processor points at the delivery path rather than at the chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> The converse also holds: a programmer that reports an insertion error rather than a communication failure shows both processor and LCD driver are alive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup>

Physical inspection focuses on the processor's [[solder]] joints, which are worth reflowing when no [[schematic]] is available and other candidates have been eliminated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup> Processors can also be responsible for a system shutting itself down deliberately: a monitor speaker's amplifier chip reported a fault on its interrupt line and the processor, correctly implementing the design, reset the amplifier rather than let it continue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup>

## Design considerations around the processor

Modern high-pin-count processors and FPGAs come with strict, extensively documented requirements for bypassing and power-rail sequencing, running to dozens of pages, in contrast to older parts where a couple of bypass capacitors would do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Low-[[inductance]] capacitor technologies are favoured near high-end processors and FPGAs precisely for their inherently lower inductance than aluminium electrolytics, with multiple case sizes easing placement close to the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Board layout also has to keep high-voltage rails away from data pins entering a large [[bga|BGA]] processor, or provide a grounded guard trace to shunt any breakdown to [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup>

The processor's ground is generally common across the digital section, which permits single-probe debugging with one ground lead attached; the measurement chipset side of a multimeter is a different matter, with multiple non-common grounds where that assumption fails badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> Ground also leaves the product: the USB shield ground reaches the processor's ground on the board and, at the other end, [[mains-earth|mains earth]] through the PC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup>

Where a design needs to remain dormant for a long period and then act reliably, a processor-based system must be continuously watchdog-reset so it cannot latch up or drift into an unrecoverable state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1131" title="EEVBlog #1131 - £1M Prank - Banksy Artwork Shredded! HOW?">1131</a></sup> For general project work a hard processor is often preferred over an FPGA, with the caveat that some projects are better suited to an FPGA and some can only be solved with one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> Cost reduction pressure commonly lands on this part: selecting a lower-cost processor and reducing parts count was central to hitting a price point while still meeting expectations such as a colour LCD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032  Part 4 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Historical forms

Before single-chip integration, a processor could be a board-level or multi-chip assembly. A 1970s Compucorp calculator split its processor across four separate ASICs and needed two more for the display processor, most likely gate arrays with a limited logic budget rather than fully custom devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup> HP calculators used a BCD-based processor with a 56-bit register in the HP 41, an architecture that was the precursor to the later Saturn family.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-582" title="EEVblog #582 - HP41CV Calculator Teardown">582</a></sup> Vintage computers commonly used identical processor boards in different roles, distinguished only by their position and the memory board next to them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1404" title="EEVblog 1404 - The Amazing HP 9845B Computer">1404</a></sup> Older machines also placed the processor in a socket, making reseating of socketed chips a legitimate first step when a system will not boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup>

Old and new silicon age differently from the parts around them. In a Tektronix 2465B whose logic-board electrolytics had leaked and corroded the surrounding board, the processor remained fully functional with its calibration data intact in a Dallas non-volatile [[ram|RAM]] whose [[battery]] is internal to the [[sram|SRAM]] package and therefore unmeasurable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> In a Compaq Portable the mask [[rom|ROM]], the RAM, the processor and the video card all still worked after decades, the silicon itself proving quite robust.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1354" title="EEVblog #1354 - Compaq Portable Repair - Part 2">1354</a></sup>

## Identification in practice

Processors are frequently identifiable only by inference. A device sitting under a [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] with a heatsink, surrounded by flash and DRAM, is the applications processor; a large Atmel part with memory, DRAM and flash around it is almost certainly running a Linux-based system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1286" title="EEVblog #1286 - Mailbag">1286</a></sup> Package markings are often deliberately obscured, sanded off, or simply unreadable without a microscope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-940" title="EEVblog #940 - Mailbag">940</a></sup> Some parts are covered by non-disclosure agreements that prevent the specific device from being named at all, even when the programmer for it is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup> Others are genuinely undocumented: a Motorola XC390176 in a vintage MicroTAC phone appears to be a processor of some kind with no available information.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-492" title="EEVBlog #492 - Vintage Motorola MicroTAC Mobile Phone Teardown">492</a></sup>
