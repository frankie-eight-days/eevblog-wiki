A microcontroller is a single integrated circuit that combines a [[processor]] core with program memory, [[ram|RAM]], and input/output peripherals on one die, in contrast to a microprocessor, which requires external [[rom|ROM]] and RAM and therefore forms part of a multi-chip solution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Because everything needed for a functioning computer is on-chip, a microcontroller can serve as the entire control system of a product: the Texas Instruments TMS1000 family, for example, performed practically every function in late-1970s electronic games, needing nothing else to run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> Modern parts additionally integrate analog-to-digital converters, timers, UARTs, [[spi|SPI]] and I²C interfaces, [[lcd|LCD]] drivers, and capacitive touch sensing, making the microcontroller the default choice for embedded control in everything from toothbrushes to multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-510" title="EEVblog #510 - Mailbag">510</a></sup>

## Origins and economics

The first microcontroller, the 4-bit Texas Instruments TMS1000, appeared in 1974, integrating RAM, ROM, and I/O at a price of a couple of dollars per chip; it took roughly four years for the toy and game industry to exploit it, with the Merlin game using the higher-memory TMS1100 variant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> Through the late 1970s and 1980s, embedded computing was dominated by microprocessors such as the 8085, Z80, and 6800, because ADCs and other peripherals had to be purchased as separate, expensive chips, and early microcontrollers were themselves costly and difficult to develop for — an 8051 development entry around 1980 effectively required being a business.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-54" title="EEVblog #54 - Electronics - When I was a boy...">54</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> The PIC 16C84 was among the first field-reprogrammable parts, using E²PROM rather than mask ROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup>

Genuinely affordable microcontrollers did not reach hobbyists until the early to mid 1990s, when PIC parts and products such as the BASIC Stamp displaced development kits that had previously cost on the order of $1,000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8Oragx4JJrE" title="Memories of Sydney Electronics Stores &amp; Work Experience at Tandy">•</a></sup> Microchip's own pivot illustrates the shift: facing commodity pricing in EPROM memory, the company applied its EPROM process to the PIC architecture to produce customer-programmable microcontrollers, replacing the mask-ROM model in which code was sent to a vendor such as Motorola with a 16-week wait for parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup>

Microcontrollers are manufactured on trailing-edge process nodes — the advanced bleeding-edge nodes are reserved for high-end processors — which is why an ARM-based microcontroller can sell for around 50 cents: the fabs are already amortized.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> At the extreme low end, Asian manufacturers largely invisible to Western distributors produce 8-bit one-time-programmable parts for under three cents each in quantity, and a 32-bit RISC-V part such as the CH32V003 for around ten cents; this pricing is what enables disposable novelty products and two-dollar toys.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1524" title="EEVblog 1524 - The 10 CENT RISC V Processor! CH32V003">1524</a></sup>

## Architecture and on-chip peripherals

The integration trend has been continuous: a microcontroller of fifteen or twenty years ago might have had a UART and little else, whereas contemporary parts assume ADCs, multiple serial buses, timers, and interrupt capability as standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> UART, SPI, and I²C interfaces are now effectively guaranteed on any candidate part, and on families such as the AVR, nearly every pin offers interrupt capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> A notable feature is that the buses need not be hardware-based at all: I²C can be implemented in [[firmware]] ("bit-banged") on ordinary I/O pins in tens of lines of code, and pull-up resistors can be enabled internally rather than fitted externally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

ADC resolution on mainstream parts is commonly 10 to 12 bits, with higher-performance variants integrating 16-bit sigma-delta converters with internal references, as in the MSP430F2013.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Some parts integrate LCD drive capability; where they do, the module must support both the number of segments and the number of common (backplane) terminals of the target display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> Capacitive touch sensing is likewise integrated into many [[current]] parts, eliminating dedicated touch-controller chips for multi-button designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-510" title="EEVblog #510 - Mailbag">510</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup>

## Selection practice

Selection is characteristically a parametric exercise: distributor databases such as Digi-Key and Mouser list on the order of 19,000 microcontroller parts, filterable by manufacturer, memory size, I/O count, package, peripherals, and price break.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> Requirements are defined first — a typical set being low [[power-consumption|power consumption]] for year-long [[battery-life|battery life]], a specified number of ADC channels, minimum I/O count, a hand-solderable package, and unit cost — and the search is then sorted by price at the expected quantity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup>

For LCD-driving applications the segment and common counts of the chosen glass must be matched exactly; a 23-segment, single-common display dictates a part supporting at least that configuration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> A built-in [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]] with high common counts frequently forces a larger package, more memory, and a substantially more expensive part, in which case a simpler microcontroller plus an external LCD controller such as the Holtek HT1622 is often the cheaper solution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> The same logic applies to memory: a project that must be crammed byte-by-byte into a chip indicates the wrong chip was chosen, since adequately sized parts cost little more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Given the dozens of parts that can satisfy any given application, the practical rule is to stop searching once a suitable candidate is found rather than to exhaustively compare every option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> Graphical vendor configuration tools that generate initialization code for clocks, GPIO, and peripherals remove a common source of early bring-up failures caused by unenabled pins or registers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup>

## Application circuit techniques

A standard power-on reset circuit uses an [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]] feeding a Schmitt-trigger input, holding the reset pin low for a defined interval while the supply ramps so the processor starts cleanly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> Modern microcontrollers use CMOS outputs that swing very close to the supply rail, which makes the supply [[voltage]] itself the reference for [[pwm|PWM]]-derived analog outputs; for precision work, the whole microcontroller can be powered from a precision [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] (2.5 V, 3.3 V, or 5 V, available at 0.1–0.2 % tolerance) provided total load current stays within the reference's rating — some references supply 40–50 mA — remembering that loads driven from I/O pins also draw from that rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

A PWM pin with an [[rc-filter|RC filter]] substitutes for a DAC when setting control voltages. Higher PWM [[frequency]] eases filtering and improves response time, but the achievable frequency is limited by the timer resolution, and the [[datasheet]] must be consulted for the exact maximum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> Resolution is typically selectable, e.g. 8-bit or 10-bit on the same hardware; where better than 10-bit conversion is required both ways, external 12-bit ADCs and DACs are added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Pins can drive moderate loads directly. Seven-segment [[led|LED]] displays are commonly driven with one pin per segment through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] plus a pin for the common terminal, at up to roughly 30 mA without external transistors, though care is needed that the switching does not disturb the ADC in the same chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Bare LCD glass can be driven straight from pins by generating the required in-phase and out-of-phase [[waveform|waveforms]] in software, with attention to port-update timing skew across segments on different ports.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1045" title="EEVblog #1045 - How To Drive an LCD">1045</a></sup> A PWM or clock output can also drive a [[diode]]-[[capacitor]] voltage doubler to produce a higher rail (for example 5 V for an LCD module from a 3 V coin cell), and by feeding the doubler from an I/O pin rather than a fixed rail the boosted output becomes software-switchable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

Every I/O pin carries an ESD protection diode to VCC, with the side effect that a microcontroller can be powered through any driven-high I/O pin: with VCC disconnected, the pin sources current through its diode, leaving VCC about 0.6 V lower (2.65 V from a 3.3 V input), which is enough to keep the chip running.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

Keypads are handled either as a scanned matrix of digital row and column lines — tolerant of several hundred ohms of switch [[resistance]] — or as a [[resistor-divider|resistive divider]] ladder read by a single ADC channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> Where pins are scarce, consolidating peripherals onto a shared I²C bus instead of SPI frees pins, since additional I²C devices cost no new lines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

## Low-power operation

[[battery|Battery]] designs revolve around sleep modes. Quiescent draw ranges from poorly implemented designs at 150 µA in the off state down to well under a microamp; one measured example idled at 0.8 µA between events.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup> Typical low-power operation is a brief wake burst — on the order of 1.4 mA to start up, read a sensor, update a display, and return to sleep — against an average of about 5 µA, so the pulse contributes little to total consumption when the wake interval is one to two seconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> [[oscilloscope|Oscilloscope]] area-under-curve integration over the current waveform yields the per-cycle charge for such duty-cycled systems.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup> Where even that is too much, memory LCDs that need only power and no refresh draw around 2 µA static and can be driven by the cheapest microcontrollers, beating any continuously running LCD-controller solution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1241" title="EEVblog #1241 - Power Up Display Counter Project - Part 1">1241</a></sup> A related discipline is fitting spare test pins or a test button so firmware can be forced into defined active and sleep states during current profiling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Development and debugging

Modern microcontrollers embed in-circuit debug circuitry on the production die, allowing breakpoints and basic debugging through low-cost interfaces, in contrast to the dedicated bond-out emulator hardware historically required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Flash programming is done in-circuit through a header; failure of a chip to respond on its debug interface despite correct power, clock, and reset is diagnostic of a dead processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup> A routine reverse-engineering hazard is deliberate erasure of part markings, common on cost-driven and security-adjacent products; identification then proceeds from package, pin count, and pin-function clues (such as VPP on pin 1 indicating a PIC), aided by the observation that companies tend to reuse a familiar microcontroller family across products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-697" title="EEVblog #697 - Mailbag">697</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kU7zSSuy9WQ" title="How to ID a Mystery Microcontroller">•</a></sup>

## Security

A standard microcontroller without on-die security features is susceptible to [[usb|USB]]-based attacks, power glitching, and supply-chain tampering, since it lacks a hardware device identity; secure-element designs store secrets in a dedicated hardened chip instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uOOGXORqsqQ" title="Trezor Safe 5 Bitcoin Crypo Hardware Wallet">•</a></sup> Basic protection of product firmware relies on the device readout-protection fuses: blowing them makes casual copying impractical even though determined [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]] remains possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-106" title="EEVblog #106 - Top 5 Tips To Bring Your Product to Market (and why Patents are a BAD idea)">106</a></sup> Where a product exposes [[connector]] pins to users, ESD robustness may rest entirely on the microcontroller's internal clamp diodes unless a dedicated protection device is fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1075" title="EEVBlog #1075 - Digital BitBox Hardware Wallet Review">1075</a></sup>

## Microcontrollers versus alternatives

Against [[fpga|FPGAs]], the microcontroller's limitation is architectural: it is a bottleneck in which every operation passes sequentially through one processor core, instruction by instruction, whereas an FPGA implements massively parallel logic and contains no processor at all unless one is configured into it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> The compensating virtues are accessibility and price — a beginner can flash an LED with a bare PIC or AVR on a [[breadboard]], a supply rail, and a cheap USB programmer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-635" title="EEVblog #635 - FPGA&#x27;s Vs Microcontrollers">635</a></sup> The choice of discrete logic over a microcontroller remains a legitimate engineering decision: a 1992 design used GALs specifically because an unrebooted micro could lock up unrecoverably, and niche mixed-signal parts are still preferred where a small fixed function makes a micro unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-833" title="EEVblog #833 - Mailbag">833</a></sup> Conversely, the historic trajectory has been microcontrollers displacing earlier solutions — the PIC family's 8-pin parts effectively ended the era of featureless microprocessors such as the Z80 in embedded roles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-5 - Talking Electronics - Old Hardware - Part 5 of 5">280</a></sup>
