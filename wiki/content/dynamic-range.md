Dynamic range is the ratio between the largest and the smallest signal a system can handle at once, usually expressed in decibels. It matters wherever a single instrument or sensor has to cope with signals of wildly different magnitude without either clipping the large one or burying the small one in [[noise]] — a [[current]] that swings between microamps in sleep and milliamps during a radio transmission<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup>, a spectrum in which a wanted tone sits far below a carrier<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>, or an acoustic field spanning a whisper and a screamer<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup>. It is distinct from resolution: a converter's bit count sets how finely it divides its span, but the [[noise-floor|noise floor]] sets how much of that span carries real information<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>.

## Resolution is not dynamic range

Bits and usable dynamic range diverge because of noise. A converter can offer 24-bit resolution without delivering anything like 24 bits of true dynamic range, because the noise floor swamps the lowest bits and makes them unusable<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>. Working the other way, an 8-bit converter is only good for something around 50 dB, which is why a general-purpose [[oscilloscope]] [[fft|FFT]] cannot substitute for a [[spectrum-analyzer|spectrum analyzer]] with roughly 100 dB of range<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>. The same 8-bit limit shows up directly on screen: pushed down to 500 microvolts per division while measuring supply [[ripple]], the individual quantisation steps of the math result become visible, a disadvantage of the digital scope that the analog scope did not have when large differences and dynamic range were involved<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>. Bode plotting on an 8-bit instrument is constrained for the same reason, and the high-resolution boxcar averaging modes intended to recover range do not sit well with the measurement<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup>.

The trade also runs in the opposite direction. A dynamic signal analyzer can have enormous dynamic range in the sense of accepting inputs of very different magnitudes, and still be limited by the bit count of its ADC when those signals occur at the same time — a 14-bit converter is what stops it from resolving vanishingly small distortion products<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vwAhHz7Zpzk" title="Guest Video - Karl Adams - Audio Distortion Measurement">•</a></sup>.

## Oscilloscope front ends and 12-bit converters

In a 12-bit oscilloscope, the converter is not the whole story: the front end ahead of it is not itself 12-bit, but it must supply the low-noise dynamic range that lets the 12-bit conversion mean anything<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1503" title="EEVblog 1503 - Rigol HDO4000 12bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1503</a></sup>. Front-end design has advanced to the point that an 800 MHz, low-noise, 500 microvolt per division front end with 12-bit dynamic range performance is buildable at consumer price points, and the same board can be shared across products separated by thousands of dollars without changing that front end<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup>.

Unusual vertical range sequences appear to be a consequence of the same optimisation. Where scopes conventionally step 1-2-5, some 12-bit instruments step 1-2-4-5, which is most plausibly explained as maximising the dynamic range performance of the 12-bit converter<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup>.

## Measuring a scope's ADC range

The nominal full-scale span of a scope front end is not what the converter actually digitises. At 1 volt per division across eight divisions the nominal span is 8 volts peak-to-peak, yet a signal that appears clipped on screen proves, when the acquisition is stopped and shifted, to have been sampled outside the visible window — there is extra range beyond the display<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>. The measurement procedure is to feed a triangle wave, which makes clipping easier to see than a sine, establish that the signal survives at one setting and clips at the next, then walk the generator amplitude down until clipping just disappears; the true range lies between those bounds<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>. Not every instrument has much to give: on one 12-bit scope the overrange amounts to a little extra before it clips, and effectively nothing worth exploiting<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup>.

## Preserving the range you have

Since dynamic range is finite, most bench technique is about not wasting it.

Attenuation throws range away, so an active probe is built with the lowest attenuation ratio that the design permits — around 1.3 to 1, almost but not quite unity — specifically to maximise the dynamic range of the signal reaching the scope<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup>. A large [[dc-offset|DC offset]] wastes range in the same way, by consuming span that carries no information. A [[power-rail|power rail]] probe removes the DC offset before the signal reaches the scope input, so the full 14 bits are available to the AC content riding on the rail; an ordinary active probe without [[ac-coupling|AC coupling]] cannot do this, and the burden falls on the scope front end's own offset capability instead<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup>.

[[auto-ranging|Automatic ranging]] is the instrument doing this housekeeping itself. A built-in DVM rescales to maximise the dynamic range of an 8-bit converter<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup>, and a [[frequency-response|frequency response]] analyser auto-ranges on every sample point — and adjusts its [[bandwidth]] as it sweeps — for the same reason<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) Explained &amp; Measured">1521</a></sup>. In audio transmission the equivalent is the compander, a combined compressor and expander that squeezes the source into the channel's available range and restores it afterwards, standard practice in wireless microphones; the art is in lifting low-level material without also lifting the noise<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup>.

## Current measurement

Dynamic range is the central specification for a [[current-shunt|current shunt]] amplifier, because the current being measured may swing between a sleep-mode trickle and an active burst when a device wakes up and transmits<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup>. Supply [[voltage]] sets the ceiling directly: an amplifier running on 2.7 to 5.5 volts has at most 5.5 volts of dynamic range, and if that is insufficient the design is pushed into range switching<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup>. Range switching carries its own penalty — driving an [[op-amp|op amp]] into saturation costs milliseconds of recovery time unless clamping circuitry is added, which means extra parts and extra design work, and the artefact lands squarely in the measurement<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup>.

The asymmetry between the two measured quantities is instructive. In a source-measure style instrument the voltage channel is undemanding, and an 8- or 10-bit converter suffices; the current channel is where the massive dynamic range lives, spread across eight ranges, and where converter quality is worth paying for<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>.

Elsewhere the requirement can be modest. A biopotential front end such as an ECG amplifier has enough dynamic range at 3 volts, and the practical constraint is the upper limit — going beyond about 5.5 to 6 volts risks damaging the op amp through its [[input-protection|input protection]] diodes<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>.

## Audio

Audio is where the largest numbers appear. A well-designed microphone preamplifier measured at 12 dB SPL equivalent input noise and 155 dB SPL at one percent distortion — not at clipping — yields 143 dB of dynamic range from a single circuit, which few semiconductor circuits achieve and which a tube circuit can match<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup>. Converters sold into the audio market quote figures in the same territory: a 24-bit, 192 kHz codec specifies 114 dB of dynamic range for both its ADC and DAC paths, alongside about -100 dB THD<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup>. Competing audio interfaces built on such parts can differ by only a few decibels of dynamic range<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup>.

Bench audio analysis is more constrained than the sources it measures. A 16-bit dynamic signal analyzer specifies 90 dB of dynamic range, rising to 130 dB in an optional swept sine mode<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup>, and an older analyzer tops out at 75 to 80 dB, which is not enough to characterise a low-distortion compound amplifier without an external notch filter ahead of it to suppress the fundamental<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vwAhHz7Zpzk" title="Guest Video - Karl Adams - Audio Distortion Measurement">•</a></sup>.

At the transmission end the requirement collapses. Digitising intelligible voice needs only about 30 dB of signal-to-distortion ratio held over roughly 40 dB of dynamic range, a specification that telephony codecs meet with companding rather than brute-force linear conversion<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup>.

High dynamic range in a transducer has a downside: powered studio monitors combining efficient drivers with wide dynamic range make the inherent noise floor of their own amplifiers audible as tweeter hiss<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>. Speakers nonetheless offer considerably better dynamic range than headphones, though headphones seal out room noise and expose small details during editing<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-169" title="EEVblog #169 - Samson StudioDock 4i USB Monitor Speaker Review">169</a></sup>. Dedicated audio codec silicon is what buys improved dynamic range in a compact recorder, as in the generational step between action camera models<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup>.

## Sensing, imaging and RF

The human eye sets a benchmark that electronic sensors struggle to meet: roughly 90 dB, a bit over 30,000 to 1, adapting quickly enough that scenes from full sunlight to moonlight all appear broadly usable<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-48" title="EEVblog #48 - Solar Power Hope">48</a></sup>. This is why a small [[solar-panel|photovoltaic panel]] that charges a phone in full sun does nothing indoors — the eye's adaptation disguises how far apart the two light levels actually are<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-48" title="EEVblog #48 - Solar Power Hope">48</a></sup>. The same gap explains why camera-only autonomous vehicle perception is weaker than human vision at picking out low-light detail, glints and small movements, and why such systems are supplemented with radar and LIDAR<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1066" title="EEVblog #1066 - Uber Autonomous Car Accident - LIDAR Failed?">1066</a></sup>. Camcorders address the problem with dedicated dynamic range modes, which on some models shift the image sensor and visibly alter the frame<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #650 - Canon HF G30 Camcorder Review - FULL">2</a></sup>.

Physics instrumentation pushes the requirement further. A neutrino detector module must resolve anything from a single photon striking its photomultiplier up to thousands during a large event, and that wide dynamic range has to be achieved at a cost that permits building thousands of identical units<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1gfM_9EdLSs" title="Antarctic Neutrino Detector!">•</a></sup>.

In RF, dynamic range is a system-level result rather than a single component specification: the phase noise and noise figure of a radar receiver add up to determine the dynamic range of the whole system, which is why phase noise [[calibration]] matters so much to radar operators<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1041" title="EEVblog #1041 - Keysight Calibration Lab Tour">1041</a></sup>. Measurement systems built around FFT analysis rather than a conventional D-to-A converter achieve better dynamic range and a lower close-in noise floor<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1041" title="EEVblog #1041 - Keysight Calibration Lab Tour">1041</a></sup>. Spectrum analyzers are specified accordingly — a mixed-domain oscilloscope's RF input is quoted at -60 dBc, adequate to pull low-level content out from under an -18 dBm peak<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> — and dynamic range is one of the axes on which six-figure real-time analyzers compete<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup>. A SAR test chamber achieves very wide dynamic range partly by using fiber optic links, which contribute little noise<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-201" title="Mobile Cell Phone Radiation SAR Testing - EEVblog #201">201</a></sup>.

## Dynamic range as a headline specification

Instruments intended for precision work advertise dynamic range because it bounds what can be trusted. A 24-bit delta-sigma converter running at 4 megasamples per second specifies 103 dB of dynamic range, improving to 111 dB with -107 dB THD at lower sample rates, which is the kind of part that goes into a source measure unit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>. A 16-bit precision instrumentation recorder handling 20-volt ranges devotes substantial effort to [[shielding]] and to generating clean rails from a single [[battery]], precisely to protect that dynamic range from its own power conversion<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup>; the electrical specifications alone were rarely the whole argument for such a machine against a plug-in data acquisition card, but they were the entry requirement<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup>. The practice extends beyond instruments: dynamic range and digit accuracy were specified for a professional personal computer, an unusual disclosure that reflected a calculator heritage in which the numbers coming out were meant to be trustworthy<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-904" title="EEVblog #904 - Hewlett Packard HP85 Professional Computer">904</a></sup>.
