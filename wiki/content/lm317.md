The LM317 is an adjustable three-terminal [[linear-regulator|linear voltage regulator]], and one of the small handful of parts that qualify as true jellybean components — stocked as a matter of course, used everywhere, and worth knowing in detail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> A single part number covers any output from 1.25 V to 37 V, set by two external resistors, which is why it earns a place in a kit where the fixed 78XX series would require a different part for every rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> It carries the same protection features as the fixed regulators — short-circuit [[current-limiting|current limiting]], thermal overload protection, safe area compensation — at a nominal 1.5 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Its weaknesses are as well known as its strengths: it will not regulate below 1.25 V, it needs a minimum load to regulate at all, and it is emphatically not a low-dropout part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

## How it works

Internally there is no mystery and no real difference from a 7805: an error amplifier driving a series pass [[transistor]], usually a Darlington pair, closed around a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] through a feedback divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The fixed 78XX types build the divider into the chip; the LM317 brings the feedback out to an adjust pin so the divider sits outside, and the two work on exactly the same principle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

The internal reference is 1.25 V, developed between the output and the adjust pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The classic configuration puts a fixed [[resistor]] R1 from output to adjust and an adjustable R2 from adjust to [[ground]], giving the standard output formula; rearranged for design work, R2 equals V out divided by 1.25, minus one, multiplied by R1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

That reference [[voltage]] also explains the floor. The output cannot be commanded below 1.25 V, because the part can only hold the reference across R1 — and that limitation is what disqualifies it from a lab supply that must reach 0 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

### R1 and the adjust pin current

240 ohms is the nominal value for R1 in the datasheets, and it is a value worth standardising on: a design that uses several LM317s can share one 240 ohm part across the whole [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]], and that part can often be reused elsewhere in the circuit for parts consolidation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> A 220 ohm is an equally serviceable substitute in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The value should not be pushed far upward — beyond roughly 500 ohms the error terms become significant and the design will no longer meet the [[datasheet]] specs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

The reason the value is low in the first place is the adjust pin's own error [[current]], which flows through R2 and adds a term to the output equation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Typical adjust pin current is 50 µA, but design calculations should use the worst case unless the exact part is known.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> With 100 µA through 363 ohms the added error is about 36 mV, turning a nominal 3.3 V into 3.336 V — not serious for casual work, but something to be aware of when designing LM317 circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The low divider values also serve a second purpose, discussed below: they supply the device's minimum load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

## Minimum load current

The minimum load current needed to maintain regulation is the classic "big trap for young players" with this part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The device needs 3.5 mA minimum, which puts it on par with the 78XX series — the 78XX has no minimum load requirement but carries a higher [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]] instead, so neither family suits micropower design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

In ordinary use the requirement is invisible, because the feedback divider is specified with values low enough to present the minimum load itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> It becomes visible the moment the divider is removed. Driving the adjust pin directly from an external source leaves the output with no minimum load at all, and an explicit load resistor must be added to make the circuit stable — a 1K resistor serves on the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Similarly, an application that genuinely draws only 1 mA needs a resistor on the output purely to establish the minimum current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

The [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]], by contrast, is not required for stability, though it is normally fitted as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

## Dropout voltage

The LM317 is a standard linear regulator with a high [[dropout-voltage|dropout voltage]], not an LDO.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The datasheet input-to-output differential is typically 3 V minimum — more than the 7805 requires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> As a working rule of thumb the dropout is 1.5 to 2 V at full current, against perhaps 0.2 V for an LDO.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

Measurement bears the graphs out closely. Defining dropout as the input differential at which the output falls by 100 mV, a 3.3 V output at 20 mA and 25 °C [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]] drops out at just over 1.5 V of differential; at 200 mA the same part measures 4.958 V in against 3.264 V out, or 1.69 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The transition is not clean — the part does not drop out as smoothly as one would like.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

The practical consequence appears whenever the LM317 is asked to make 3.3 V from a 5 V [[usb|USB]] rail. Characterising such a design worst case means setting the input 5% below nominal, to 4.75 V, then raising the load current until the output falls 5% below 3.3 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> A regulator run at room temperature on this margin gives roughly 150 to 160 mA of usable output before it leaves regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> An LDO in the same socket would deliver 500 mA without difficulty, needs no adjustment resistors if a fixed version is used, and requires only input and output caps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Choosing the LM317 for a 5 V to 3.3 V conversion is, on those numbers, a gamble that trades most of the available current for the convenience of a part already in the junk box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

## Constant current configuration

Shorting the adjust pin to the output and inserting a single resistor turns the regulator into a [[constant-current-source|constant current source]]: the device holds its 1.25 V reference across that resistor, so the current is 1.25 V divided by the resistor value — 1.25 A for 1 ohm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-992" title="EEVblog #992 (Part 2) - How To Clean &amp; Service A Microscope">992</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Both the LM317 and the 78XX family can be used this way, and the whole circuit amounts to the regulator, a resistor and a [[capacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-992" title="EEVblog #992 (Part 2) - How To Clean &amp; Service A Microscope">992</a></sup>

[[grounding|Grounding]] the output of such a [[current-source|current source]] and connecting the input to the supply under test converts it into a constant current dummy load for [[power-supply|power supply]] and [[battery]] testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> The configuration has real limits: the sense resistor must be a very low value and can only occupy a narrow range if the circuit is to stay stable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> At more modest currents the arrangement is unremarkable and works well — an LM317 current source dissipating about a watt is a routine solution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-992" title="EEVblog #992 (Part 2) - How To Clean &amp; Service A Microscope">992</a></sup>

Because a linear regulator passes essentially all of its [[output-current|output current]] through from the input, the input current in such a load equals the output current, which makes the drain on a battery under test genuinely constant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>

## Use in adjustable and programmable supplies

The LM317 with a voltage knob and a current knob is the basis of hundreds of published bench supply designs, and a supply built from one plus a couple of knobs is among the classic self-build projects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> The device is robust, low [[noise]], relatively stable, and works in both constant voltage and constant current configurations, which can be cascaded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

It falls down when software control is wanted. Driving the adjust pin from an external source works — whatever voltage is fed into the adjust pin appears at the output plus 1.25 V, so 1.25 V in gives 2.51 V out and 5 V in gives about 6.25 V — but that fixed 1.25 V offset must be carried through every calculation, and it can be compensated in [[firmware]] without elegance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> The other objections are that the output cannot reach 0 V, and that the adjustment pot behaves poorly at the very bottom of its range, so setting an output current as low as 1 mA is not something the arrangement does well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> One route around the floor is to generate a negative rail so the 1.25 V offset can be pulled down to zero; the offset itself is at least fairly consistent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

A design that needs to go lower must leave the part behind. A regulator with a 0.8 to 0.9 V minimum operating voltage and a single programming resistor is a better starting point than the LM317's 1.25 V and two-[[resistor-divider|resistor divider]], at the cost of relying on the load to supply the minimum current below that point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> The alternative used in a full custom design is to keep the topology — error amplifier plus series pass transistor — and replace the internal 1.25 V feedback path with circuitry that injects an external 0 to 1 V control signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

## Thermal behaviour and failure

Being a linear pass element, the LM317 dissipates the full input-to-output differential times the load current, and thermal design is where it is most often abused. Its operational temperature range extends to roughly 120 to 125 °C, and it has built-in thermal overload protection, so an overheated part shuts down rather than fails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

The Rigol DP832 supplied its isolated 5 V logic rail from an LM317 that ran extremely hot — around 90 to 95 °C on the tab early on, with [[heat-sink|heat sink]] readings of 110 to 120 °C after ten minutes of operation, approaching a 100 °C rise above ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> The junction is hotter still, and must be computed from the datasheet's junction-to-case [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] rather than guessed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Running a regulator at that temperature to supply a nominal 5 V digital rail leaves no design margin whatsoever, and the resulting thermal shutdown was the identified cause of the unit resetting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> The part fitted also had a conspicuously thin TO-220 tab compared with a properly specified device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

The revised board changed nothing but the LM317 supply and its heat sink, which was enlarged to a part that fitted the existing footprint; the fix brought the tab down to just over 70 °C in one measurement and just over 81 °C in another with no [[airflow]], against well over 110 °C before.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> The [[transformer-tap|transformer tap]] was not changed, so a high [[mains-voltage|mains voltage]] still delivers more input voltage into the regulator than is necessary — a lower tap would have been preferable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup>

Similar arithmetic applies elsewhere. Tab temperatures around 70 °C on a TO-220 in a Stanford Research SR650, and 72.4 °C on the analog LM317, indicate no gross overload but poor thermal design in a chassis that should not have needed a fan at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup> Note also that a dropout figure quoted at 25 °C is a junction temperature, not an ambient one; serious characterisation requires a probe on the case, and even that understates the junction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

## Relatives and equivalents

Several variants share the type number. The plain LM317 is the 1.5 A part; the LM317M is a 500 mA version; the LM317L is a low power version limited to 100 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup> The 117 designation denotes the military-qualified equivalent of the 317, and in a hermetically sealed welded ceramic package it can command hundreds of dollars against a jellybean's cents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup>

The 1117 is a different part despite the similar number — the jellybean [[low-dropout-regulator|low dropout regulator]], available in fixed and adjustable versions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Its adjustable version has an identical pinout to the LM317 and an identical divider formula, so it can be treated as a low dropout equivalent of the LM317 or 7805.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-371" title="EEVblog #371 - Universal Programmer Teardown">371</a></sup> For negative rails the complementary part is the LM337, and split analog supplies are commonly built from a matched 317 and 337 pair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup>

Second sourcing is not automatic even within the family. A new die released under a modified part number — for example a 317M variant qualified for automotive use — changes the specifications, and a regulator that has been requalified may drop out differently or oscillate with different loads and bypass capacitors than the legacy chip did.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup>

## As a voltage reference

An LM317 trimmed to a chosen output can serve as a crude voltage reference, with initial accuracy in the region of 1 to 1.5%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> That is sometimes good enough, but precision work — ADC references, [[comparator]] threshold detection — generally needs better than the couple of percent a [[voltage-regulator|voltage regulator]] offers, along with the [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] specification a regulator does not provide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The part can also be combined with a series pass transistor and a precision reference to build a more accurate fixed regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

## In practice

The LM317 turns up constantly in teardowns, typically as the low-current housekeeping regulator in an instrument whose main rails are handled elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Examples span a Rigol DSA815 spectrum analyser, whose TO-220 devices are simply LM317s and 337s; a Siglent [[oscilloscope]] where an LM317 is the only secondary-side regulation; an HP 35670A analyser using adjustable 317s and 337s alongside fixed 7800-series parts; a universal programmer; a low-cost process meter; a Rigol DP832; and a monitor board where it supplies the low-end rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-371" title="EEVblog #371 - Universal Programmer Teardown">371</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1162" title="EEVblog #1162 - Little British Monitors">1162</a></sup> It also appears in retro equipment and in field bodges — one homemade modification mounted an LM317 with heatsink compound directly against a case used as a heatsink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-839" title="EEVblog #839 - Mailbag">839</a></sup>

For anyone specifying a fixed 3.3 V rail, the sensible default is a [[parametric-search|parametric search]] on current, package, dissipation, price and availability, with the LM317 plus two resistors as the ready alternative when a suitable fixed part is not to hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Where the requirement is more demanding, better regulators exist, and a low dropout part is the right choice whenever the input-to-output margin is tight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>
