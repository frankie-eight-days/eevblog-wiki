Power consumption is the rate at which a device or circuit draws electrical energy, expressed in watts (or amps at a known [[voltage]]), and it is one of the primary constraints in electronic design alongside cost, size, and performance. It determines [[battery-life|battery life]], heat dissipation, [[power-supply|power supply]] sizing, and running cost, and it can vary by orders of magnitude between operating modes of the same product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-80" title="EEVblog #80 - Nokia E71 + Garmin Mobile XT = Embedded Hell">80</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Because consumption depends so heavily on [[firmware]] behaviour, peripheral use, and operating conditions, [[datasheet]] headline figures are unreliable predictors of real-world draw; the dependable method is to build the circuit and measure it in the actual application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup>

## Measuring power consumption

Dedicated instruments exist for the task: power analysers such as the Voltech PM300 measure consumption to around 0.1% on most ranges, with specialised modes for ballasts and pulsed loads, whereas cheap plug-in energy meters give only a rough indication.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> A [[battery]] simulator such as the Keithley 2302 can additionally source with programmable output [[resistance]] and measure the [[current]], voltage, and pulse current of a device under test, replacing the improvised combination of a microcurrent probe and an [[oscilloscope]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup>

For battery-powered devices that sleep and wake, average consumption is dominated by the [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]], not the peak. Oscilloscope integration of the current [[waveform]] gives the true average: a [[microcontroller]] drawing a 1.4 mA startup pulse for 4 ms every 2 s against a 5 µA sleep floor averages only 5.35 µA, so a visually dramatic pulse can be a negligible fraction of total consumption.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup> Development boards that provide a removable jumper in the supply rail make this kind of measurement practical, though [[multimeter]] [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]] inserted in the rail can itself stop a board from functioning.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup>

Mains-powered products require care with real versus [[apparent-power|apparent power]]. A well-specified product states consumption in VA rather than watts when it lacks [[power-factor-correction|power factor correction]], and measured power factors on bench equipment are frequently poor: 0.54 on a Rigol DS1054Z drawing 22 W and 40 VA, 0.5 on a Tektronix TDS220 at 9 W and 18 VA, and 0.33 on a small monitor speaker at 8 W and 24 VA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-690" title="EEVblog #690 - TDS220 Oscilloscope Autopsy">690</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-169" title="EEVblog #169 - Samson StudioDock 4i USB Monitor Speaker Review">169</a></sup>

## Software as a power variable

Firmware has a major effect on hardware power consumption, responsiveness, and reliability, and must be tested for it. A smartphone case is illustrative: a navigation package installed a hidden utility at boot that continuously drew excess power even when the application was never run, halving real-world battery life until removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-80" title="EEVblog #80 - Nokia E71 + Garmin Mobile XT = Embedded Hell">80</a></sup> At the silicon level, neural-network accelerators can exploit the error tolerance of their algorithms to relax numerical precision — for example reducing GPU floating-point accuracy toward four or five bits — and thereby cut system power consumption.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tjdae8oqYMQ" title="The Signal Path Discussion - Engineering &amp; RF (3/3)">•</a></sup> [[processor|Processor]] consumption can also be reduced by exploiting diffusion [[capacitance]] effects in driven junctions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1384" title="EEVblog 1384 - Halve Your Processor Power Consumption!">1384</a></sup>

## Architecture and component choice

[[fpga|FPGAs]] are not optimised for power: the routing fabric, configuration memory, and process technology needed for flexibility mean an FPGA implementation can never match the consumption of a dedicated chip performing the same function, and FPGAs can draw heavily at startup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> FPGA vendors supply power estimator tools that take gate counts, family, switching speeds, and I/O usage and produce accurate consumption estimates — garbage in, garbage out — and core consumption can be trimmed by operating the device at a lower core voltage, for example 1.2 V instead of 1.5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> The engineering trade-off is between hardware performance, power consumption, and field upgradeability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

Microcontroller selection shows the same spread: a PIC32-class part can draw roughly five times the power of an STM32L alternative, while a PIC24F with nanoWatt XLP offers much lower consumption with a built-in [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> Clock rate directly scales draw; an Energy Micro Tiny Gecko drops from 5.92 mA at 32 MHz to microamps at 32 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> Even logic families differ: 74HC devices specify 20 µA maximum ICC, while old 74ALS parts and 1970s-era Intel 8212 latches (around 90 mA each, static) run hot enough to exceed 60 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3zAWgNjx5Ew" title="Intel MCS-85 Design Kit THERMAL IMAGING">•</a></sup> Aggregation matters at scale: thousands of op-amps at 5–10 mA each forced a large mixing console to use an external rack-mount supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-840" title="EEVblog #840 - Yamaha M3000 Mixing Console Teardown">840</a></sup>

## Displays

Display technology dominates portable-device budgets. A dot-matrix [[lcd|LCD]] draws a couple of milliamps where a comparable [[led|LED]] display draws orders of magnitude more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-243" title="EEVblog #243 - Vintage Brick Mobile Phone Teardown">243</a></sup> Sharp memory LCDs achieve 6 µW static consumption — 2 µA at 3 V — making them suitable for watches, calculators, and meters, and at a 1 Hz update rate an entire microcontroller-plus-display system can run at about 6 µW.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> For a three-year coin-cell watch, the entire budget is roughly 4 µA, since 100 mAh over 26,280 hours permits about 3.8 µA average.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Transmissive LCDs eliminate the option of removing the [[backlight]], which then always draws power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Plasma televisions sit at the opposite extreme, with around 380 W for a 42-inch set, where an 86-inch LCD draws about 220 W and older LCD sets about 170 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ia4xB9wk80o" title="Dumpster Diving 86&quot; LG 4K LCD TV">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup>

## Standby power

Standby draw separates good designs from poor ones. Instruments with true hard power switches draw zero when off, while soft-switched designs have been measured at 6.5–7 W standby on bench oscilloscopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> Better examples include 300 mW standby on the Owon XDS3202A oscilloscope and roughly 9 mW on the Sony D50 Discman, whose microcontroller stays awake to watch the buttons while the DC-DC converter remains off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup> Consumer computing gear ranges from 0.4 W standby on a desktop PC to 0.6 W on a small embedded x86 board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-462" title="EEVblog #462 - Video Editing PC Assembly">462</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sCPfEPSDh40" title="How Badly Can I Screw Up a Home Assistant Install?">•</a></sup> A claimed "low power mode" that still draws 19–20 W is not meaningfully low power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Fx3YixoQS6E" title="Improving the Rigol MHO98 Boot Time + Low Power Mode">•</a></sup> Long-endurance standby is achievable with discipline: a 50 mW receiver load drains a 36 Wh battery in about a month, but multi-year standby from the same pack is possible with aggressive power reduction, and safety devices achieve it by sampling sensors only twice per minute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1136" title="EEVblog #1136 - Banksy Artwork Shredder - Part 2 (Still Trolling!)">1136</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1165" title="EEVblog #1165 - Cypres Parachute Safety AAD Teardown">1165</a></sup>

## Household and system-level measurement

Whole-home consumption monitoring with current clamps exposes behaviour invisible in utility billing: 5-minute resolution reveals individual refrigerator compressor cycles, while 15-minute systems smooth them away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vDtxmikcYuc" title="Who Screwed With The Solar Analytics Code?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k2_mJtAeaog" title="SNEAKY Enphase Solar Monitoring Modification">•</a></sup> Measurement configuration errors produce characteristic artefacts — a consumption trace that mirrors [[solar-power-generation|solar production]] and goes "negative" by day indicates two measuring systems monitoring the same quantity, and a hybrid [[inverter]] reporting 84 W of phantom load was contradicted by its own battery [[current-clamp|current clamp]] at 1.15 A and 53.6 V (about 62 W, with no other path available).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k2_mJtAeaog" title="SNEAKY Enphase Solar Monitoring Modification">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kCtGoymiShU" title="Deye Hybrid Inverter Gen Port Troubleshooting">•</a></sup> Analogue induction watt-hour meters are themselves engineered for minimal self-consumption because they operate under legal metrology requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1446" title="EEVblog 1446 - Analog Watt Hour Meters are AMAZING!">1446</a></sup>

## Consumption claims in marketing

Power figures in crowdfunding and product marketing routinely fail verification. A "graphene" heater rated at 300 W was shown by the promoter's own test data to average 440 W, and resistive heaters are in any case all 100% efficient at turning electricity into heat, so a 200 W panel cannot heat a room like a 1500 W one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1186" title="EEVblog #1186 - Solus Graphene Heater Kickstarter BUSTED!">1186</a></sup> An atmospheric water generator's implied 10–15 W solar operation became 120–200 W over 18–24 hours per 1–2 litres in its own update reports.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1121" title="EEVblog #1121 - Fontus goes BANKRUPT!">1121</a></sup> A wireless-charging demonstration powered a speaker that draws only about half a watt from its AA cells, far below the claimed 5 W delivered-power capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1092" title="EEVblog #1092 - Wi-Charge IR Wireless Charging - Fact or Fiction?">1092</a></sup> A boost-converter battery extender draws progressively more current from the battery as its voltage falls, to hold output constant, while delivering negligible performance gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-963" title="EEVblog #963 - Batteriser / Batteroo Passive Toy Test!">963</a></sup> Excess consumption is also a diagnostic sign in its own right: a network chip dissipating 8 W against a 1 W specification, and a board drawing 44 W while producing no picture, both indicate faults.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-E1IqcGcZKHE" title="RPi CM4 Compute Module FAILURE Part 2: Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-378" title="EEVblog #378 - Dumpster Diving Teardown Repair">378</a></sup>
