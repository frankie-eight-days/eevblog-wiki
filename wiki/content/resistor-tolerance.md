Resistor tolerance is the manufacturer's guaranteed bound on how far a part's actual [[resistance]] may deviate from its marked nominal value, quoted as a percentage — a 100 ohm [[resistor]] at plus minus 1% may legitimately be anywhere from 99 ohms to 101 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> It is the parameter that sets the achievable accuracy of any circuit whose behaviour depends on a resistance ratio or an absolute resistance: [[current]] shunts, dividers, amplifier gain setting, and instrument reference chains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Tolerance is a guarantee about the limits of a population, not a statement about where inside those limits any individual part will land, and the difference between those two readings underlies most of the practical consequences.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup>

## What the number actually guarantees

The specification is a bound, not a distribution. Measuring the deviation of a large batch from nominal and plotting it produces a roughly Gaussian bell curve centred near the nominal value, with the great bulk of parts inside plus minus 0.1% or plus minus 0.2% and the count falling away steeply from there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> In a batch of 400 nominally 1% metal film resistors, no part deviated by more than about plus 0.5% or minus 0.6%, and by the 0.5% bin there was almost nothing left — only a handful of parts in the outer bins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> Getting nothing near the claimed plus minus 1% limit out of 400 samples is the expected result of a process whose capability is far tighter than the grade being sold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup>

That gap between capability and marking is a deliberate commercial arrangement. A manufacturer whose process is geared around a plus minus 0.5% spread can bin the same production as 0.5% parts and sell the remainder as 1% parts, confident they will fall well inside the looser limit, losing only a small percentage at the extremes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> The older folk belief that cheap 1% parts are simply 5% parts that happened to test well is at best a historical artefact; modern processes are targeted and progressively tightened rather than sorted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup>

## The distribution is not guaranteed to be centred

The centring of the distribution is not part of the specification, and it varies by manufacturer. A batch of 1,000 low-cost 1% 50 ppm metal film resistors showed a spread comparable to the higher-quality brand — around 0.6% wide — but the whole distribution was offset roughly minus 0.35% from the nominal 1k, with not a single resistor measuring above nominal, plus a few stray outliers out at minus 1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup> Every one of those parts was inside its plus minus 1% specification and therefore entirely conforming.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup>

The practical consequence is that averaging tricks cannot be relied on. Paralleling ten resistors in the expectation that high and low parts will cancel to yield an effectively tighter part assumes the manufacturing mean sits on nominal; when the population is offset, the parallel combination simply lands on the offset mean.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup> Hand-selecting matched parts out of a bin with a [[multimeter]] is the reliable version of the same idea, and can bring nominally 1% parts to within a couple of least significant digits of each other — far better matching than the marking promises, though only for that hand-picked set.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

## Combining resistors in series

Tolerances of series resistors do not add. A common beginner error is to assume four 100 ohm plus minus 1% resistors in series give 400 ohms plus minus 4%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Taking the worst case, four parts each at their 101 ohm limit total 404 ohms, which against a 400 ohm nominal is 4 ohms out, or 1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> The same arithmetic holds for a hundred resistors in series: the total remains plus minus 1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> This is why a decade resistance box built from 1% parts can honestly be specified as plus minus 1% overall, and why commercial boxes built from 0.1% parts are specified at plus minus 0.1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup>

Resolution and accuracy are separate properties in such a box. A decade box spanning 10 ohms to 10.1 megohms in 10 ohm steps built from 1% resistors will increment reliably by 10 ohms when the lowest decade is switched in, but the absolute value at the 1 megohm setting carries a 1% uncertainty that dwarfs the step size — the fine steps are relative, not absolute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup> Commercial boxes also vary their tolerance by range, with the higher decades quoted at 1%, 2% or 3% and the mid ranges tightening toward 0.1%; one instrument meets 0.1% only over the stated 10 ohm to 10 megohm span.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup>

## Tolerance versus temperature coefficient

Absolute tolerance and [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] are distinct specifications, and which one matters depends on whether the circuit is trimmed. Where a range is calibrated by a trimmer, initial tolerance is largely irrelevant and tempco governs the result; where there is no trimmer, the resistor's own tolerance sets the accuracy directly, which is why 0.1% parts appear on untrimmed ranges of a multimeter while the trimmed shunt path can use ordinary 1% parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Precision decade boxes frequently omit temperature coefficient from their published specifications, and the omission matters more than it appears: the resistors inside self-heat above ambient according to the current passed through them, so the drift is driven by dissipation, not merely by room temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Precision parts are consequently specified on several axes at once — a 10 ohm reference resistor at 0.05% and 10 ppm, or laboratory-grade parts described by ppm class, where a 0.01% 1k and a 0.005% 10k correspond to roughly 2 ppm and 5 ppm classes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup> Low aging is a further separate property of genuinely precision parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup>

## Grades, cost and availability

The available grades span several orders of magnitude, and a single manufacturer series may be offered anywhere from 1% down to 0.01%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> Historically, discrete carbon resistors were plus minus 10%, with plus minus 5% parts marked by a gold band and sold at a premium; carbon composition was eventually displaced because that precision was too crude for modern requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup> Consumer retail stock of the era was 5% carbon film sold in small packs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1240" title="EEVblog #1240 - Tandy In The 1980&#x27;s">1240</a></sup> Even parts explicitly sold as precision are not automatically tight by modern standards: circa-trim 10k precision resistors from an earlier generation carry a plus minus 5% marking, their value being the very low temperature coefficient rather than the initial tolerance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup>

Cost climbs sharply with grade, and this shapes design decisions. 0.1% resistors are cheap enough to specify freely for a general instrument accuracy target of roughly 0.1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> At the 0.01% level, individual parts run about $20 each from a distributor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup> Cost also drives bill-of-materials consolidation: reusing one expensive 0.1% value in series and parallel combinations elsewhere in a design is preferable to stocking many distinct precision values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Some values are simply hard to obtain at any grade. A 0.1% 10 milliohm [[current-shunt|current shunt]] is difficult and expensive to source, which forces trimming as the alternative route to accuracy on low-current shunt ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> At the other extreme, very high value resistors are frequently out of specification unless bought at top prices, commonly landing at plus minus 10% or 20% figures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-673" title="EEVblog #673 - Mailbag">673</a></sup> Availability can also gate a project outright — a precision 10 ohm 0.05% part was unobtainable worldwide at one point, requiring a committed order of 1,000 pieces up front.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup>

## Where the number does and does not matter

Not every resistor in a design needs a tolerance specification worth paying for. High-value [[input-protection|input protection]] resistors feeding a high [[impedance]] meter input need no precision at all and can be ordinary 1% parts — just Joe Blog's resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> An [[led|LED]] dropper setting current in a [[battery]] gauge does not need to be precise, so the nearest convenient value serves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> Conversely, where the tolerance is the accuracy, it must be bought: the microamp and nanoamp ranges of a current adapter derive their specification directly from 0.1% resistors, as does the gain-setting pair around the [[op-amp|op amp]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

Instrument design frequently accepts resistor tolerance as one contributor within a looser overall budget. 1% resistors are adequate inside a meter with a 2% total specification, particularly where trimming brings the result into range, whereas a higher-accuracy instrument uses half-percent low-aging parts throughout, including on the high values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup> Where the tolerance stack is relied on directly, [[calibration]] can be skipped entirely: units built from a single batch of parts and verified against one fully tested example can be expected to be in specification, though outliers do occur.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-197" title="RAW! Testing µCurrent&#x27;s, Keithley Current/Voltage Sources, DIY Pick And Place - EEVblog #197">197</a></sup> A meter calibrated only on its 100 mV range and relying on internal resistor tolerance for the remaining ranges can still be a little out on those ranges while staying within specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1084" title="EEVblog #1084 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout Part 2">1084</a></sup>

Resistor tolerance also sets the [[noise-floor|noise floor]] for interpreting measurements. Discrepancies of a few percent between nominally identical circuits can be attributable to the tolerance of the resistors and of whatever other impedances share the node, ruling out more alarming explanations such as contamination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup> Asynchronous timing between nominally identical 555 oscillators derives from the same source, combined with internal [[threshold-voltage|threshold voltage]] variation and [[capacitor]] tolerance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup> In metrology, tolerance ceases to be the dominant term at the extremes: a 100 gigaohm leakage path in parallel with a 100 megohm reference resistor contributes a 0.1% error, pushing an instrument outside a 0.05% specification, so teraohm-order insulation becomes the constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup>

## Verification against the marking

Tolerance is a claim that can be checked, and checking it occasionally exposes a supply chain failure. Parts ordered against a distributor listing of 0.1% in 0805, with the manufacturer [[datasheet]] confirming that the part number's B suffix denotes 0.1%, measured 10.08 ohms on a nominal 10 ohms under four-wire measurement — 0.8% out where 10.01 or better was expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> Other parts from the same brand-new batch measured 10.23 ohms, and assembled boards were as much as 3.5% out, consistent with a 10.35 ohm part in a 10 ohm position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> The parts were evidently not the specified series at all; buying 0.1% and receiving something several percent out is a failure of the part, not of the technique, and testing before shipping product is the defence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup>

Ordinary functional checks give a coarser but useful confirmation. A 10 by 10 grid of 10k 1% resistors used to approximate an infinite resistor grid returned 6.41k and 6.42k against a theoretical 2/pi times R, or about 6.37k, at several points — within the 1% tolerance, and consistent across directions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup> Further out on the grid the finite edges dominate and readings move outside 1%, showing that the deviation there is a limitation of the model rather than of the parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25" title="EEVblog #25 - The Infinite Resistor Puzzle">25</a></sup> A resistor cube built from unmatched 4.7k 1% parts decades old measured 3.517k against a predicted 3.525k, an error of 0.23% and well inside what the parts guarantee; a full minus 1% deviation on the 0.75 scale factor would have produced 3.489k.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>

Where a transfer standard is needed rather than a check, a factory 0.01% metal foil resistor serves: 0.01% of 1k is 0.1 ohms, which is the least significant digit of the display, so absolute values transfer essentially spot on even though the procedure is not a genuine calibration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup>

## Tolerance in design tools

Tolerance is a first-class parameter in the design flow rather than something considered only at the bench. Simulators accept a tolerance field on the resistor element alongside the power rating, feeding later analyses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-516" title="EEVblog #516 - LTSPICE Tutorial - DC Operating Point Analysis">516</a></sup> Monte Carlo analysis exercises that field, spreading component values across their 1% or 5% bands and re-running the simulation to establish whether a marginal value choice still works — an appropriate step when a chosen value sits near the edge of its margin, alongside prototype verification over temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Distributor [[parametric-search|parametric search]] likewise treats tolerance as a filter field on equal footing with resistance, power, composition and temperature coefficient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup>
