A vacuum fluorescent display (VFD) is an emissive display in which electrons boiled off a heated [[cathode]] filament strike phosphor-coated anodes inside an evacuated glass envelope, causing them to fluoresce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup> It is functionally a vacuum tube shaped into a display: the same filament heating, the same electrode structure, and the same dependence on an intact vacuum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup> VFDs dominated consumer and instrument front panels through the 1970s and 1980s — calculators, audio gear, cash registers, test equipment — and remained in production long after LCDs became the default, because they are bright enough for high ambient light yet dim smoothly to very low levels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-641" title="EEVblog #641 - Dumpster Cash Register Teardown">641</a></sup>

## Construction and operation

Three elements sit inside the vacuum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup> At the bottom is the anode, a phosphor-coated element of the same family used in fluorescent lighting tubes; electrons striking it fluoresce and produce the light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup> Above it is the grid, and above that the cathode filament, run at a negative potential relative to the anode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup> An AC [[voltage]] on the filament heats it so that electrons burn off its surface; a positive voltage on the anode relative to the filament then attracts them across, with the grid interposed to gate which regions light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup>

The filament wires run the length of the display and terminate at pins on each end, so a typical pinout puts one cathode connection at each side, commonly the outer corner pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> Filament connections are readily identified because the two pins at each end are shorted together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> Cathode wires are usually visible as a string running across the face, with the grid structure beneath and each grid segment separate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> Filaments are relatively fine and it is common to see two running across a display; taller displays may use three.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> Some later panels abandon the visible cathode-wire-and-grid appearance entirely, presenting continuous traces to segments rather than individually addressable dots.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup>

Every VFD carries the evacuation port — the small glass nipple where the air was pumped out and the envelope sealed off — usually on the back or at one end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup> Nearby sits the getter, a barium seal whose job is not to hold the vacuum in but to trap gases that would otherwise contaminate the envelope; its typical failure signature is dark spots on the display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1665" title="EEVblog 1665 - Keithley VFD REPAIR 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1665</a></sup> A getter that has turned white indicates the vacuum has been lost, and its normal appearance is evidence that a dead display may still be intact and the fault elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1665" title="EEVblog 1665 - Keithley VFD REPAIR 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1665</a></sup>

## Drive voltages

VFDs cannot run from logic rails. Anode and grid drive require substantially more than the 5 V TTL supply that runs the surrounding logic, so instruments feed 5 V into a dedicated [[boost-converter|boost converter]] and generate the display rail from it — often identifiable on the board by a 50 V [[capacitor]] on the output side against a 10 V part on the input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1665" title="EEVblog 1665 - Keithley VFD REPAIR 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1665</a></sup> Probing a live segment line on a calculator-class display shows a swing of roughly 28 V at around 287 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup> Purpose-built supplies for driving salvaged VFDs cover a comparable span: a step-up module taking about 12 V in and producing 32 V to 54 V adjustable was built specifically for old Soviet VFDs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup>

The rail is not always positive. Consumer audio equipment commonly generates a negative pull-down supply for the display — a −35 V VP rail in one receiver, described in the general case as tens of volts negative.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1395" title="EEVblog 1395 - Onkyo Repair SUCCESS">1395</a></sup> Filament supplies are low-voltage AC, on the order of 4.5 V to 5 V, and are usually derived externally rather than from the display driver chip itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup>

Larger salvaged modules typically want a system-level supply: a 12 V input is a reasonable starting point where a 16 V rated capacitor is present on the board, though some run from 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup> [[current|Current]] draw at 5 V can reach 0.8 A on a large dot-matrix module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup>

## Power consumption

Current consumption is the principal engineering objection to VFDs. A dot-matrix module drawing 212 mA is dissipating about a watt while displaying nothing, orders of magnitude more than the few milliamps of a comparable [[lcd|LCD]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> Even a small eight-digit calculator display draws 114 mA at 2.4 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup> This rules the technology out entirely for [[battery]]-powered instruments targeting long runtimes; a design aiming at thousands of hours on a set of cells must use an LCD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup>

Pin count is kept manageable by [[multiplexing]], which is why displays with many segments emerge on comparatively few connections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-851" title="EEVBlog #851 - Mailbag">851</a></sup> High-speed capture of a bench instrument display at 1000 frames per second resolves the multiplex sequence stepping around the display in quadrants — a rate at which 100 or 200 frames per second is insufficient to freeze the scan.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup>

## Drivers and modules

High-voltage drive is normally handled by dedicated silicon. Serial-input VFD drivers such as the PT6315 are used in pairs on instrument front panels, fed over a [[ribbon-cable|ribbon cable]] from the [[microcontroller]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup> Older equipment used dedicated display processors — the Fluke 45 front panel uses a µPD7512-series driver alongside a four-bit microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup> Cost-reduced consumer designs integrate everything: a single LSI can contain the [[processor]], the VFD driver and the high-voltage generation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup> Discrete potted high-voltage bricks appear in 1970s equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup> Larger instrument modules break out the filament and high-voltage driver on the same board as the display, sometimes with more than a dozen driver chips, part of them hidden beneath the display itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup>

Character and dot-matrix VFD modules are made to be electrically interchangeable with the standard Hitachi LCD interface, making them drop-in replacements for a conventional character LCD apart from the current budget.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> In practice the initialisation sequence may differ enough that LCD [[firmware]] does not bring one up directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> Manufacturers encountered across equipment include Futaba, IEE, Samsung and Babcock, the latter producing custom modules to order.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-851" title="EEVBlog #851 - Mailbag">851</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup>

## Aging and failure

VFDs fade. Dimming with age is the characteristic end-of-life behaviour across calculators, bench multimeters and audio equipment, and it is progressive rather than binary — a display can be functional and displaying correct information while being almost unreadable without shading it from ambient light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1012" title="EEVblog #1012 - Best Bargain Ebay Bench Meter? - Fluke 8842A">1012</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-749" title="EEVblog #749 - Mailbag">749</a></sup> This matters diagnostically: a VFD fault is often not a go/no-go condition, and a panel that looks dead may simply be very dim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> Character burn-in from static content is also visible on heavily used units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup> The other failure mode is loss of vacuum, after which the display is unrecoverable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup>

Because the electronics driving a VFD are comparatively robust, a blank display in old equipment is far more likely to be the tube than the driver board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup> The diagnostic sequence is to verify the supplies before condemning anything: check the AC filament voltage at the end pins, then the anode and grid drive rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> Absent filament voltage is conclusive, since neither vacuum tubes nor VFDs work without filament heating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> A dim display traced through the drive chain can turn out to be an anode drive voltage fault rather than a failed tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup> The VFD position found on some multimeters is unrelated — it selects variable [[frequency]] drive measurement, not vacuum fluorescent display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup>

Physical handling is its own hazard. The glass envelope is fragile, displays are readily broken in transit, and a socketed instrument-sized module requires enough force to extract that removing it risks breaking the glass top; levering from one end is not viable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1601" title="EEVblog 1601 - How VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent) Displays Work">1601</a></sup> Flexible display substrates tolerate only a few bends before fracturing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup>

## Replacement strategies

Two substitution paths are practical. Where the interface is a standard 16-pin inline character connection, an ordinary LCD module can be fitted in place of the VFD, though the original front-panel filter — often a dark contrast filter matched to an emissive display — may absorb a non-emissive replacement almost completely, requiring a [[backlight]] and even then giving poor results.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup> Purpose-built [[led|LED]] replacement displays exist for instruments with a history of VFD failure, such as the Agilent 53131A counter, using blue LEDs chosen to approximate the original VFD colour and a segment current set low enough not to glare.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1669" title="EEVblog 1669 - Agilent 53131A VFD to LED Display Upgrade MAILBAG">1669</a></sup> Such a swap is effectively a preemptive repair, trading the original appearance for a display that will not fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1669" title="EEVblog 1669 - Agilent 53131A VFD to LED Display Upgrade MAILBAG">1669</a></sup>

## Design trade-offs

The choice against a VFD in newer instruments is driven by cost, capability and consumption rather than by any deficiency of the display itself. Replacing a VFD front panel with a colour LCD gives histograms, trend charts and statistics that a segment display cannot show, and became an expectation for instruments released around 2010.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Cost is close: one instrument front-panel assembly costing about $65 as built was matched by a VFD-based design at about $59.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Isolation cuts the other way — keeping a floating front panel safely isolated is easier with a VFD than with a flat panel display, which must connect directly to the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>
