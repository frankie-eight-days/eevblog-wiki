A transformer is a passive component comprising two or more magnetically coupled windings that transfers AC energy between circuits, changing [[voltage]] and [[current]] levels according to its turns ratio while providing [[electrical-isolation|electrical isolation]] between primary and secondary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> It operates only on alternating current — which is why every switch-mode converter first chops its DC input back into AC before feeding it through the transformer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> Transformers appear at nearly every scale of electronics, from pole-mounted substation units down to [[pcb|PCB]]-planar magnetics, and serve two fundamentally different roles: bulk power conversion and signal coupling or isolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2vJ0c0ioAXY" title="Electronex 2014 - Walkaround">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1104" title="EEVblog #1104 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Teardown">1104</a></sup>

## Operating principles

The ratio of primary to secondary turns sets the voltage transformation: a 1:1 winding fed 1 V AC delivers 1 V out, while a large step-up ratio can multiply a signal enormously — an electrostatic speaker driver measured 1.1 Ω on its primary against roughly 1.5 kΩ on its secondary, a step-up of about a thousand times.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1150" title="EEVblog #1150 - Electrostatic Speaker Teardown">1150</a></sup> The inverse also holds for measurement: in a step-down transformer the secondary carries the higher current and is therefore wound with visibly thicker wire, which is a quick way to identify which side is which.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup>

Transformers are rated in VA, not watts, because winding and conductor sizing must account for [[apparent-power|apparent power]] — the product of volts and amps regardless of phase difference between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Real power is dissipated only in resistances: in a transformer that means the winding [[resistance]], since the ideal reactive (magnetic) component dissipates nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> This winding resistance is also a practical diagnostic — a healthy small mains transformer primary typically measures in the tens of ohms, with measured examples of 45 Ω and 54 Ω on bench gear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> A flyback primary may read much lower, around 2.2 Ω, with a secondary reading nearly a short because its turns ratio is small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup>

## Isolation

Physical and electrical separation between primary and secondary windings means there is no direct electrical path between the two sides — only a small amount of interwinding [[capacitance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> This property, traditionally called [[galvanic-isolation|galvanic isolation]], is exploited both for safety and for [[noise]] control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-bg6QsTT0Plw" title="Laser Driver PCB &amp; Circuit Review">•</a></sup> A two-pin (unearthed) supply with an isolated transformer secondary is electrically equivalent to a [[battery]] supply from an [[oscilloscope]]-[[grounding]] standpoint, which is why checking for a short between output [[ground]] and [[mains-earth|mains earth]] with an ohmmeter determines whether a supply is isolated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup>

Precision instruments use transformer isolation deliberately to break ground loops: the Keithley DMM7510 uses a custom-wound toroidal transformer delivering ±6 V AC solely to isolate the analog board's grounds from the noisy switching supply grounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Similarly, multi-channel lab supplies route each channel through separate transformer secondaries and rectifiers so the channels are galvanically isolated from each other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup> Wideband injection transformers — essentially 1:1 transformers spanning 1 Hz to 10 MHz with CAT-rated isolation — exist specifically to inject disturbances into control loops for Bode-plot measurement without a DC connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1104" title="EEVblog #1104 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Teardown">1104</a></sup>

## Mains-voltage configuration and taps

Because transformers are wound for a specific [[mains-voltage|mains voltage]], equipment without a switch-mode front end is generally sold as fixed-voltage models; 115/230 V operation, where offered, is implemented either as a voltage-selection tap on the primary or as a split primary whose two coils are paralleled for 115 V or placed in series for 230 V — the series configuration roughly doubling the measured primary resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> Multiple secondary taps supply different rails or ranges from a single core: vintage instruments tap the transformer for each AC range, and linear supplies tap the secondary to feed separate regulators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup>

A center-tapped secondary provides a ground-referenced midpoint, used, for example, to generate a differential high-voltage drive in electrostatic speakers without a separate high-voltage generator, and historically as the injection point for microphone phantom power, where the DC supply connects [[via]] a [[resistor]] to the audio transformer's center tap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-616" title="EEVblog #616 - How Microphone Phantom Powering Works">616</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1150" title="EEVblog #1150 - Electrostatic Speaker Teardown">1150</a></sup>

## Switch-mode and power-conversion roles

In switch-mode supplies the transformer is driven by a switching [[transistor]] at frequencies far above mains — a measured example ran at about 27 kHz — allowing much smaller magnetics than a 50/60 Hz transformer of equivalent power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> The sharp rise and fall times and high breakdown capability of gallium-nitride switches have pushed this further, enabling extremely small, efficient converters such as 60 W adapters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o2NxHu5Bsnk" title="Discussion with Shahriar from The Signal Path">•</a></sup>

Transformers in UPS inverters are driven by an H-bridge of MOSFETs from the battery, with the secondary producing the AC output; in some topologies the same transformer works backwards, with the H-bridge pushing power back through it to charge the batteries, eliminating a separate charger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup> Bidirectional hybrid solar inverters similarly pass multi-kilowatt power both ways through a single large toroidal transformer on its own [[heat-sink|heatsink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> In resonant-mode converters such as the LLC, the transformer's magnetizing and leakage (resonant) inductances become deliberate circuit elements that must be designed in matched ratios, parasitics included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup> Series-input, parallel-output (ISOP) multi-level topologies exploit the property that paralleled transformer secondaries force the series-connected inputs to share voltage equally, giving automatic balancing across devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

A transformer secondary is also the usual source for voltage-multiplier chains: a Cockcroft-Walton ladder multiplies the transformer's peak AC by two per stage, so a 1 kV secondary can be stacked to 10 kV or more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup> For low-voltage doubling, however, a transformer is unnecessary overhead; a Dickson charge-pump doubler driven directly from a [[microcontroller]] pin replaces it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

## Signal, sensing, and RF uses

Many transducers and interfaces are functionally transformers even when not called one:

- Ribbon microphones produce such low voltage at sub-ohm [[impedance]] that a step-up transformer with an extreme turns ratio is required to get a usable signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-602" title="EEVblog #602 - Introduction to Microphones">602</a></sup>
- Contactless payment cards and their readers couple through air-core coils that behave as a transformer, not an RF [[antenna]] — which is why placing a passive load coil nearby corrupts the 847.5 kHz load-modulated data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-889" title="EEVblog #889 - Credit Card RFID/NFC Theft Protection Tested">889</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-890" title="EEVblog #890 - ArmourCard Active RFID Jamming Teardown">890</a></sup>
- A [[current-transformer|current transformer]] is a toroidal core with many turns on the secondary and the measured conductor passing through as a single primary turn — 1 A in yields roughly 1 mA out — and multiple current transformers can be paralleled to sum currents from separate feeds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup>
- A metal detector's drive and sense coils on a shared ferrite rod form a transformer; nearby metal draws energy from the coupling and drops the sense-coil amplitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup>
- Ethernet ports use one small magnetics transformer per channel pair for isolation and common-mode rejection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-I-9dGvk3BW8" title="TP-Link AX6000 Router Teardown">•</a></sup>
- Small step-up transformers generate the high voltages for [[vacuum-fluorescent-display|vacuum fluorescent display]] filaments and photon-counter outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup>

## Failure modes and practical bench notes

Loose laminations vibrate at the mains [[frequency]], producing the classic 50–60 Hz hum; switch-mode transformers can whine at audible frequencies for the same reason if their [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] or a subharmonic falls in the audible band.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> Magnetizing current is sometimes palpable as vibration through the chassis of a receiver even before any secondary rail comes up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup>

Overvoltaging a fixed-voltage transformer burns the enamel insulation off the windings, releasing a large volume of smoke in seconds; even if the unit still works afterward, the compromised winding insulation means it should not be trusted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup> Thermal fuses buried in the windings are a common protection, and their absence — together with a missing primary [[fuse]] — is a recognised safety defect in cheap [[soldering]] stations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1160" title="EEVblog #1160 - Weller Responds!">1160</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup>

On the bench, transformers foil some measurements: a transformer winding acts as a low impedance that wrecks in-circuit [[capacitance-measurement|capacitance measurement]] below about 10 µF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> In simulation, a transformer's galvanically isolated secondary has no DC path to ground, causing a singular-matrix error unless a ground reference is added on the secondary side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> Mechanically, heavy parts such as transformers contribute to board flexure on drop, which can crack nearby ceramic capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1037" title="EEVblog #1037 - Solving Ceramic Capacitor Cracking">1037</a></sup> A deliberate PCB spark gap between primary and secondary provides a controlled air-gap breakdown path — tested examples held 1,000 V and sparked by 2,500 V — diverting surges across the isolation barrier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup>
