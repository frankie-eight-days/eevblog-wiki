The tip is the consumable working end of a [[soldering-iron|soldering iron]], and it is the component that decides whether heat actually reaches the joint. Two stations of nearly identical wattage can perform very differently because of tip geometry, tip [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] and how the tip couples to the heating element and [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> The judgment that follows from this is blunt: "Tips are everything on your soldering iron"<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup>, and an iron purchase is really a purchase of the tip ecosystem behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5vbg8QEZXfY" title="iFixit FixHub Soldering Iron Review EXPLAINED + More Testing">•</a></sup>

## Geometry

The chisel or wedge tip is the general-purpose shape for both through-hole and surface-mount work, and is available in a range of widths such as 2 mm, 2.5 mm and 3.2 mm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup> A wedge tip is useful in roughly 95% of [[soldering]] applications, and if only one tip can be had it should be a wedge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup> The conical or round tip supplied as standard with most irons is of little use: a fine point does not have the surface area to transfer heat into the component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> A fine point is still worth keeping for the occasions where a chisel physically cannot fit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup>

A chisel only works if it is used flat. Tilting the iron so that only an edge touches means the flat of the chisel is not in contact with the pad and little heat enters the joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup> The chisel point should touch the pad and the pin at the same time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Where a pad sits at an awkward angle, rotating the board rather than the iron is the way to keep the tip flat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup>

Well or wave-base tips hold a reservoir of [[solder]] whose surface tension drags solder away along a row of pins, which is the basis of drag soldering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> A well tip will also wick excess solder back out of a bridged row.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> Drag soldering does not require one; a standard chisel does the same job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Well tips are also used for reflowing a fine-pitch package in place, where the self-wicking action cleans up as it goes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup>

At the other extreme, a very large chisel spanning most of a package lets an entire row of leftover pins be scraped off at once after chip removal, and can be laid on an exposed [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] to heat a whole chip from the copper outward.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup> For lifting SMD chips, a large thermal mass iron with a decent chisel is required; a conical tip will not get the heat through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> Thinner tips earn their place in rework by fitting between tall components without cooking the parts around them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup>

## Thermal mass and power

Thermal mass and power are separate properties, and "don't confuse thermal mass with power" is the operative rule: within one tip line, the high-thermal-mass premium tips and the standard tips can carry the same 130 W rating, and the difference is only what heat is retained in the physical tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> The premium tips are visibly thicker with a bulge in the shank; retaining more heat means the tip temperature dips less when it is applied to a joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup>

A physically thin tip is also a bottleneck for power delivery. A large-capacity tip can accept essentially 100% of the station's output because there is no thin section in the path, while a small bevel or conical tip cannot, even when the base unit is rated at 100 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5vbg8QEZXfY" title="iFixit FixHub Soldering Iron Review EXPLAINED + More Testing">•</a></sup> On one such 100 W-rated portable, the supplied small bevel tip appeared to reach only around 50 W peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5vbg8QEZXfY" title="iFixit FixHub Soldering Iron Review EXPLAINED + More Testing">•</a></sup> Where the heating element sits behind the tip rather than inside it, the shortfall is worse still.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>

The consequences appear at the bench as failures on high-thermal-load work: TO-220 tabs, D-pack packages, large resistors and any pad tied to a [[ground-plane|ground plane]] are where small tips run out of capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> A miniature cartridge with almost no thermal mass, rated 14 W per tool, is excellent for 0201 and fine-pitch work but will not solder [[ground]] planes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QbeTv41l2EQ" title="JBC Nano Soldering Iron">•</a></sup> A $5 [[usb|USB]] iron with a very thin fine-point tip has virtually no thermal mass, and its limit on heavy wire is set by the shape of the tip as much as by the wattage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup> Where a station offers no larger-capacity tip in its range at all, that ceiling cannot be raised by the user.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>

Comparative thermal testing is therefore done with identical tips fitted to both irons — for example the same 3.2 mm chisel at the same set temperature, heating identical pads on a board with a ground plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup> Tests of that kind show that a 5 W difference in iron rating counts for far less than the design of the ceramic heater element, its heat path and the tip design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup>

## Cartridge tips versus separate tips

In traditional construction the tip is only a tip: it is physically separate from the heating element and the temperature sensor, so heat must cross a contact interface and whatever air gap the fit leaves, adding [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> In cartridge or direct-heat construction the element and the temperature sensor are built into the tip itself, right at the tip, so power can be delivered faster and the control loop is much tighter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> Such a tip carries multiple contacts — an outer, a middle and a tip contact — to serve the heater and the sensing element through one connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Because the tip is the heater, the shaft stays cool enough to hold, heat-up is a matter of seconds, and tips can be changed quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup>

Traditional tips are the cheapest option but may not last as long, because without a setback system that cools the tip between joints they sit at full soldering temperature; integrated tips cost the most but can last longer precisely because setback works with them, and recovery from the reduced temperature takes only a couple of seconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> Some stations add a sensing stand that cuts power when the iron is docked, and the same sensing can be used for hot tip changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

## Plating and tip life

The working surface of a tip is a plating, and its loss is what ends the tip's life. With use the plating pits and wears away, and once it is gone the tip only produces bad quality joints and should be discarded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> The plating must be matched to the solder: a tip that is not a lead-free tip should not be used with the corresponding solder, because the wrong combination eats the plating away very quickly and can eat holes in the tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

Plating thickness is a deliberate design trade-off. Thicker iron plating buys longer tip life at the cost of thermal performance for a given power input, while thinner iron plating gives better thermal performance and a correspondingly shorter tip life — the difference between the Pace and JBC tip philosophies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup>

Temperature is the other lever. Holding a tip at full soldering temperature for ten or twenty-four hours a day shortens its life and the plating lasts a great deal less, which is why decent stations implement automatic temperature setback on the stand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup> Some implementations reduce power after a delay; others switch the element off entirely and fall to room temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup>

Routine care is cleaning and tinning. Wiping on a wet sponge while rotating the tip leaves it clean for the next joint, and brass or steel wool cleaners serve the same purpose in a stand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup> A freshly tinned tip is kept tinned as a matter of habit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1281" title="EEVblog #1281 - Garmin GPS Repair">1281</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup> A film of solder on the tip also improves thermal contact, and briefly applying solder to the iron before touching the joint heats that joint faster than a dry tip does — though this must not become the only technique, since solder is properly fed to the far side of the joint rather than to the tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup>

## Ranges, compatibility and cost of ownership

Because tips are consumables bought repeatedly, their price and the breadth of the range dominate long-run cost. Standard tips for one 130 W station start at about $10–11 and its high-capacity ultra performance tips run about $14–17, roughly a third of the price of the equivalent JBC tips; combined with longer life from thicker plating this gives a much lower cost of ownership.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> Tips for one portable station cost about $20 each with no high-capacity option in the range at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> One station ships without any tip in the box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup>

Cross-compatibility is common and worth checking. T12-style Hakko tips are available in genuine and copy versions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup> The FX951-pattern tip also appears on TS100- and TS80-class irons and their clones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> A successor portable iron may be fully backward compatible with the previous model's tips, protecting an existing investment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup> Several stations and [[desoldering]] tweezers are built around JBC-compatible cartridges, in the tweezers' case clamped by grub screws that should not be over-tightened.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OvGdE5hC1Ro" title="Bakon BK212 Soldering Station - URGH">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1650" title="EEVblog 1650 - Sequre HT-140 SMD Desoldering Tweezers REVIEW">1650</a></sup>

Tip quality should be matched to the iron. On a decent station only genuine tips are worth trusting, and cheap marketplace tips are not; but on a $16 clone iron the cheap tips are the sensible choice, since one or two genuine tips cost more than the whole iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup> A four-dollar assortment for a budget lab will not last long but will do the job, and should include chisels, a well type for drag soldering and larger tips for bigger work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> A working bench keeps at least two irons and a range of tips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-51" title="EEVblog #51 - A tour of the EEVblog Electronics Lab">51</a></sup>

Tips have also been made by hand: a thin piece of copper tubing pushed over the element, flattened and filed to a point, with the solder taken up between the tubing and the element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-874" title="Extended Cut: EEVblog #874 - Ted Wale">874</a></sup>
