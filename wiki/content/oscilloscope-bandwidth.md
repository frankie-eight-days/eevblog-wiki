Oscilloscope bandwidth is the [[frequency]] at which an instrument's analog front end has fallen 3 dB below its low-[[frequency-response]], and it is the single most important specification of a scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup> The figure printed on the front panel is not a limit beyond which nothing is seen and below which the response is flat; it is the point at which a [[sine-wave]] of constant amplitude at the input reads 0.707 of its true value on screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> Everything else about an acquisition system — [[sample-rate]], [[memory-depth]], vertical sensitivity, and the probe on the end of the cable — is either subordinate to the analog [[bandwidth]] or capable of quietly destroying it.

## Definition and measurement

Because the specification is a minus-3 dB point, the practical test is to feed in a constant-amplitude sine wave, sweep the frequency upward, and find where the displayed amplitude reaches 0.707 of the low-frequency reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> A 4 V peak-to-peak signal should therefore read about 2.83 V at the rated bandwidth, and a 5 V peak-to-peak signal about 3.53 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> The measurement is only meaningful when the signal path is properly terminated: a 50 ohm feedthrough at the input, a correctly compensated ×10 probe, or a direct 50 ohm load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-978" title="EEVblog #978 - Keysight 1000X Oscilloscope Hacked!">978</a></sup>

Where no sufficiently fast generator is available, bandwidth can be inferred from step response instead. Feeding a 1 kHz square wave with a fast edge and measuring the displayed rise time gives the bandwidth as roughly 0.35 divided by the rise time, a method that works on analog instruments where no frequency source of the right range exists.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup> The relationship inverts freely, so a measured rise time also yields a bandwidth figure: a 520 ps edge corresponds to about 769 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Different constants apply depending on the front-end response — 0.4 and 0.45 are both in use — and the result is a lower bound rather than an exact value, because the cabling and the finite rise time of the source both contribute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup>

A rise-time check is also the standard confirmation that a bandwidth change has actually taken effect in hardware rather than only in a menu. On the Rigol DS1000E series, a 3.5 ns displayed rise time and a 2 ns/div minimum [[time-base]] indicate the 100 MHz mode, while the 50 MHz mode returns a slower edge and a 5 ns/div floor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-77" title="EEVblog #77 - Rigol DS1052E DS1102E Oscilloscope Hack Update">77</a></sup>

## Bandwidth against sample rate

Analog bandwidth alone does not make a signal usable. For single-shot work the governing rule of thumb is that the real-time sample rate should be about ten times the analog bandwidth, so a 300 MHz instrument running at 500 MS/s has a usable single-shot bandwidth closer to 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> That ratio became the industry norm with the Tektronix TDS200 series, which paired 100 MHz of bandwidth with 1 GS/s of real-time sampling and established the 10-to-1 expectation for the digital scopes that followed; earlier instruments sampled at around 20 MS/s and relied on equivalent-time sampling to fake the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-690" title="EEVblog #690 - TDS220 Oscilloscope Autopsy">690</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup> Where sine(x)/x interpolation is applied, a lower multiplier of roughly four times is treated as the minimum acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup>

The mismatch appears most often in low-cost four-channel instruments, where a single ADC is shared and the aggregate sample rate divides down as channels are enabled. A 1 GS/s converter shared four ways yields 250 MS/s per channel — technically acceptable for a 100 MHz model with interpolation, and inadequate for the 200 MHz version of the same chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> At 500 MS/s a 200 MHz front end is effectively reduced to around 125 MHz of usable bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup> The same reasoning applies at the top of the market: a 500 MHz four-channel instrument dropping to 1 GS/s per channel meets Nyquist and still fails to perform as a genuine 500 MHz scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> Where the advertised sample rate cannot support the advertised bandwidth at all — 200 MS/s behind a claimed 110 MHz front end — the specification does not hold together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1375" title="EEVblog #1375 - Mailbag: IEEE">1375</a></sup>

Not every distorted [[waveform]] is a bandwidth problem. A square wave reconstructed from too few memory points can be badly wrong at very low frequencies, and reducing the bandwidth limit to 20 MHz changes nothing, because the fault lies in memory depth and interpolation rather than in the front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup>

## Bandwidth and noise

The higher the bandwidth, the greater the inherent noise of the input amplifier and the rest of the front-end circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup> This is the mechanism behind the long-standing impression that analog scopes are quieter than digital ones: an analog instrument of modest bandwidth and short phosphor persistence simply does not display the high-frequency content that a wideband digital scope with deep memory captures and paints on screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup>

The effect is directly observable. Engaging a 20 MHz limit on a 100 MHz instrument visibly drops the trace thickness, and stepping a 1 GHz scope down through 250 MHz to 20 MHz reduces it further at each step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> On a 200 MHz scope the [[noise-floor]] at the most sensitive range falls to under one division once the 20 MHz limit is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup> Comparative noise figures track bandwidth closely: at 1 mV/div, roughly 94 microvolts RMS from an instrument restricted to 200 MHz against 96 microvolts from a 350 MHz instrument running at full bandwidth, and around 323 microvolts from a wider front end under otherwise identical settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1223" title="EEVblog #1223 - Oscilloscope Standard Deviation Noise Measurement">1223</a></sup>

Critically, the noise penalty is baked into the hardware and cannot be undone in software. A 350 MHz front end software-limited to 200 MHz is still measurably noisier than a native 200 MHz front end at the same setting, because a bandwidth limit filters the noise rather than preventing it from being generated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1612" title="EEVblog 1612 - Siglent SDS1000X HD 12 bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1612</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-nO09bc5ozng" title="NEW Tektronix 2 Series MSO Oscilloscope FIRST REACTION">•</a></sup> The same logic underlies the near-universal presence of a 20 MHz bandwidth limit on modern scopes, and the convention that power-supply output ripple and noise is specified over a 20 MHz bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup> Measuring supply noise at the full 200 or 400 MHz bandwidth of the instrument is not the industry standard and produces figures that cannot be compared against a [[datasheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup> Reduced bandwidth also buys resolution in high-definition acquisition modes, where dropping from 500 MHz to 200, 100 and finally 10 MHz raises the effective resolution from 14 bits to roughly 18.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup>

## How bandwidth is set in hardware

In instruments up to about 500 MHz, both the switchable gain ranges and the bandwidth limiting are typically handled inside a programmable gain amplifier — commonly the TI/National LMH family — which then drives a differential output to the ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> Register writes over an [[spi]] bus to that amplifier select the bandwidth, and the 20 MHz limit is implemented there with no other hardware involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> On one instrument family the base unit ships at 50 MHz and is raised as far as 300 MHz entirely by SPI commands to the front-end amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wWymcaiaQXM" title="What happened to my Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202?">•</a></sup> Probing that bus reveals settings well above the model's rating — a 750 MHz code on a 300 MHz unit — with the actual ceiling imposed elsewhere in the front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup>

Above that range the front end becomes a custom part. A single-chip [[asic]] covers 70 MHz to 500 MHz in one Tektronix design, and a comparable custom ASIC provides a 1 GHz front end at the sub-$1000 price point, drawing enough power to require heat-sink contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> Not every manufacturer shares hardware across a model range: some series use genuinely different analog front ends at 200/350, 500, 1000 and 1500 MHz, which requires a board swap at the factory to change bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup> Analog bandwidth is generally expensive to obtain, and pricing reflects it — a four-channel 200 MHz instrument at around $4,000 against $7,500 at 350 MHz and $10,000 at 500 MHz within one series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup>

## Software-limited models and bandwidth hacking

A large proportion of the market ships the full-bandwidth front end in every model of a series and restricts it by licence key or configuration setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> One compact instrument carries no bandwidth marking on the front panel at all, precisely because the figure is a software property; the base 100 MHz unit contains the same 300 MHz vertical amplifiers as the top model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> The pattern repeats across manufacturers: a 70 MHz model containing a 300 MHz front end, a 50 MHz model containing the full 100 MHz hardware, a 70 MHz model containing a 300 MHz front end with no upgrade offered for sale, and a series spanning 70 to 500 MHz in five field-upgradeable steps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup> Where upgrades are sold, the path may be constrained by the model purchased, or may allow a jump from 100 MHz directly to 1 GHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> Some series are deliberately not upgradeable, with separate 70 and 100 MHz models and no licence route between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-522" title="EEVblog #522 - Rigol DS1000Z Oscilloscope Quick Look">522</a></sup> More recent designs push the software span further, expanding a base 350 MHz model to 800 MHz or 1 GHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup>

Because the hardware is present, the restriction is frequently defeatable. Changing the model number over the remote interface converts a 50 MHz instrument to its 100 MHz sibling, and the change survives a [[firmware]] upgrade provided the modification is performed on a sufficiently old firmware version first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-77" title="EEVblog #77 - Rigol DS1052E DS1102E Oscilloscope Hack Update">77</a></sup> On another instrument, setting two product-configuration resistors to zero changed the reported bandwidth; measurement then showed the front end was not 3 dB down even at 200 MHz, implying an actual analog bandwidth beyond 200 MHz on a unit sold at 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-978" title="EEVblog #978 - Keysight 1000X Oscilloscope Hacked!">978</a></sup> Where the setting lives in an SPI register, a small [[microcontroller]] intercepting the bus can rewrite it in transit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup>

A software-set bandwidth is also useful in its own right. Being able to set a 500 MHz instrument back to 200 MHz allows side-by-side comparison against a 200 MHz unit without the extra noise of the wider setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-nO09bc5ozng" title="NEW Tektronix 2 Series MSO Oscilloscope FIRST REACTION">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1612" title="EEVblog 1612 - Siglent SDS1000X HD 12 bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1612</a></sup>

## Where the rated bandwidth is not available

Full bandwidth is commonly withheld at the most sensitive vertical ranges. One manufacturer's datasheet carries a double-asterisk footnote stating that below 1 mV/div the bandwidth is limited to 200 MHz — so the 500 microvolts per division range advertised alongside the bandwidth figures cannot deliver it — and on the 500 MHz and 1 GHz models the restriction applies below 2.45 mV/div, meaning four or five mV/div is required before the purchased bandwidth appears.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> The same trap appears elsewhere: a 300 MHz instrument that cannot use its 1 mV/div range at full bandwidth, a 300 MHz instrument that automatically engages its bandwidth limit at 2 mV/div, a 12-bit instrument that forces a 20 MHz limit on its 500 microvolt range, and a 1 GHz instrument on which a 1 GHz signal simply is not present at low volts-per-division settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> The consequence is that a noise figure quoted at 1 mV/div may have been taken at 200 MHz on an instrument sold as 1 GHz — "a bit of a trap for young players".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1223" title="EEVblog #1223 - Oscilloscope Standard Deviation Noise Measurement">1223</a></sup> Instruments that provide a true, non-zoomed 1 mV/div range at full bandwidth are the exception rather than the rule.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup> Analog instruments have an equivalent behaviour: engaging ×10 vertical magnification on a 50 MHz scope drops the bandwidth to roughly 5 MHz, an order of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup>

The probe is the other common ceiling. A switchable ×1/×10 passive probe in its ×1 position has a bandwidth of about six and a half megahertz, so the ×1 setting reduces a 100 or 200 MHz instrument to that figure regardless of the front end behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> Passive probe technology runs out at around 500 MHz; above that, fixed 10:1 and then active probes are required, and the probing solution can cost more than the scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ln_XJDPKJlc" title="eevBLAB 103 - Is an $800 Oscilloscope OVERKILL?">•</a></sup> A 1 GHz passive probe is essentially unobtainable, which caps what a 1 GHz instrument can usefully be pointed at.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> Supplying 500 MHz passive probes across an entire model range avoids the probe becoming the limiting element and preserves a later bandwidth upgrade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> When a signal on screen looks smoothly sinusoidal because sine(x)/x interpolation is filling in between too few samples, the signal is beyond the scope's bandwidth regardless of how good the probe is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup>

## Claimed against measured bandwidth

Low-cost portable instruments are where claimed and measured bandwidth diverge most sharply. A tablet scope advertised at 100 MHz measured about 22 MHz at the 3 dB point with a direct 50 ohm input, and its own manual conceded 5 MHz on the ×1 profile; the claimed 1 GS/s sample rate was likewise not delivered, so the 100 MHz figure could not have been met in any case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup> Restricting such an instrument to 20 or 30 MHz makes it usable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup> A $123 pocket instrument technically reached its claimed 180 MHz, but behind only 500 MS/s falling to 250 MS/s with two channels — a well-designed 50 MHz front end would have suited the price class better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup> An $80 instrument claiming 50 MHz visibly falls apart by 40 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup> Claims are not always inflated: one $70 single-channel instrument measured 107 megahertz with proper [[50-ohm-termination]] and a compensated ×10 probe, after an initially poor result was traced to the physical ×1 attenuator in the supplied probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup>

## Choosing bandwidth

For general-purpose bench work the practical floor has been around 20 MHz for a secondhand dual-channel analog instrument, with 50 MHz preferable and 100 MHz comfortable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> For modern digital work a couple of hundred megahertz covers fast edges, serial decoding and mixed-signal analysis, and 200 to 350 MHz is regarded as sufficient for almost all general-purpose requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ln_XJDPKJlc" title="eevBLAB 103 - Is an $800 Oscilloscope OVERKILL?">•</a></sup> Above roughly 500 MHz the probing problem dominates, and buying additional bandwidth that will never be usefully probed is treated as wasted money for anything short of a specific niche requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ln_XJDPKJlc" title="eevBLAB 103 - Is an $800 Oscilloscope OVERKILL?">•</a></sup> Bandwidth requirements are also application-dependent in the other direction: power-conversion work at hundreds of kilohertz to a few megahertz was well served by 7.5 MHz instruments, and field service work is well served by 5 to 10 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup>

## Historical and high-end range

The bandwidth available at a given price has moved by orders of magnitude. A 1965 dual-channel instrument offered 0 to 10 MHz, a 1975 portable combining scope and [[multimeter]] offered 1 MHz, and 10 MHz was a respectable bench figure in that era.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup> The 300 MHz analog instruments of the mid-1980s were raised to 400 MHz in their revised versions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> At the far end, real-time instruments reach 2 GHz at 3.2 GS/s, 13 GHz at 40 GS/s, and 32 GHz halving to 16 GHz when all four channels are enabled at 80 GS/s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-984" title="EEVblog #984 - World&#x27;s Best Dumpster Find! ($300k!)">984</a></sup> Above a few gigahertz the [[input-impedance]] becomes 50 ohms as a matter of necessity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup>
