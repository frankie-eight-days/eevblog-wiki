Current limiting is the deliberate capping of the [[current]] a source will deliver, either by design inside a circuit or as a user-set threshold on a bench [[power-supply|power supply]]. Its purpose is to bound the energy available to a fault: a supply that limits at a set current can have its output shorted indefinitely without damage, and a circuit fed from a limited source cannot draw enough current to destroy itself before the fault is noticed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Absent any limit, the only thing setting the current is the [[impedance]] of the load and the wiring, which in a fault condition is close to nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

## Constant current mode in a bench supply

A regulated supply with an adjustable limit behaves as a constant [[voltage-source|voltage source]] until the load demands the set current, then crosses over into [[constant-current-mode|constant current mode]] and lets the [[output-voltage|output voltage]] fall to whatever value sustains that current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> With 2 A set and a 3 A load connected, the output collapses to around 0.12 V while still delivering the full 2 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> Shorting the output of a supply limited to 0.5 A produces exactly 0.5 A and no more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The same behaviour appears as a diagnostic display: a 1 A limit into a faulty 3.3 V rail holds 1 A while the rail [[voltage]] drops far below nominal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

In constant current mode the supply is functioning as a constant current generator, dialled to whatever value is wanted, even though it is normally thought of only as a protective ceiling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> That protective framing is the usual one, because most of the time the circuit under test is being run in constant voltage mode and the limit exists only for when something goes wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

Limiting is not the only possible response to an overcurrent. [[overcurrent-protection|Overcurrent protection]], where implemented separately, switches the output off entirely instead of entering current limiting mode, which is the appropriate choice when continuous operation at the limit would itself be damaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> Without it, a supply simply sits in [[current-limit|current limit]] continuously as a normal supply does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> Some designs make the limiting behaviour selectable between several modes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> The limit and the load state are usually indicated on the front panel, and a limiting supply may or may not disturb its own upstream source — a [[usb|USB]]-powered instrument can current limit its outputs without collapsing the USB supply feeding it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-337" title="EEVblog #337 - HSC School Electronics Projects">337</a></sup>

The crossover is not instantaneous. On a supply set to a 1 A limit driving a 20 V range, the output can ramp toward the full set voltage for several milliseconds before the control loop determines that limiting is needed and pulls the output back, producing visible [[overshoot]] on turn-on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup>

## Bringing up unfamiliar hardware

The standard bench procedure for powering an unknown or repaired board for the first time is to set a current limit well below what would cause damage and only then apply power. The margin is chosen against a rough estimate of expected consumption: 20 V at a 0.25 A limit is about 5 W into a board that should draw nothing like 5 W, which is an acceptable margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup> Typical working values are 100 mA for small logic and display boards<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-573" title="EEVblog #573 - Mailbag">573</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qmcSEJehqZM" title="Energizer Battery Leak Fluke 3000 Multimeter &quot;Repair&quot;">•</a></sup> and around 1 A for a complete portable consumer device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-863" title="EEVblog #863 - Sony D50 Discman Teardown - World&#x27;s First Portable CD Player">863</a></sup> A first power-on of a repair candidate at 0.1 A rather than a more generous 1 A means that if the board is faulty there is not enough power behind the supply to do any harm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup> The same reasoning motivates substituting a current-limited bench supply for a board's mains supply during fault-finding, both for safety and because the exposed mains supply is itself hazardous to have on the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> Where the required rail is high voltage, the ability to current limit is the main thing lost when a bench supply cannot reach the needed voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1657" title="EEVblog 1657 - DUMPSTER Samsung 75&quot; QLED One Connect TV">1657</a></sup>

A limit set too low creates its own failure mode. Power-on surge currents can exceed the steady-state draw by a wide margin, and if that surge trips the limit, the supply drops its voltage in response, which can in turn upset the converter or circuit being powered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Choosing a limit for a finished product being tested is therefore a different judgement from choosing one for a design under development, where a tight limit such as 1.1 A per channel is a reasonable safety margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

## Current limiting inside circuits

The simplest implementation is a [[series-resistor|series resistor]]. Placing one in series with a chip's supply will not prevent CMOS SCR latchup, but it prevents the excess current of a latchup event from destroying the chip; the penalty is degraded high-[[frequency]] performance, which confines the technique to DC and low-frequency parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16" title="EEVblog #16 - CMOS SCR Latchup Tutorial">16</a></sup> The same idea protects instrument inputs, where high-value resistors ahead of a chipset's pins limit the current during an overload without affecting accuracy, since the [[input-impedance|input impedance]] is high enough that 1% tolerance parts suffice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

LEDs are the canonical case for series limiting, because their forward characteristic is steep enough that a small voltage error produces a large current error and there is nothing in the device itself to restrain the current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> Driving LEDs directly from a chip output with no [[dropper-resistor|dropper resistor]] and no current limiter is a design fault, and one that would be expected to fail a design review.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup> The consequence of omitting the limit entirely is destructive: 15 V across a small red [[led|LED]] lights the bonding wire itself, produces flame over the wire, and destroys the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup> Where an LED driver is only a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] switch rather than a constant current stage, the limiting must be done externally in the [[led-strip|LED strip]], and provision on the board for a current limiting [[resistor]] — ideally with DIP-switch selection between two or four resistor values — is a worthwhile addition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup>

Many integrated parts include limiting. A high-voltage op-amp intended for power supply service can accept 0 to 150 µA on a dedicated pin and translate that to a 0 to 700 mA adjustable [[output-current|output current]] limit, making it close to a complete supply in one package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> Switching regulators offer an externally programmed limit set by a resistor<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup>, LED driver controllers implement cycle-by-cycle current limiting alongside short-circuit protection and undervoltage lockout<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup>, energy-harvesting parts limit a switched auxiliary output to 300 mA internally<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup>, a nixie driver's totem-pole high-voltage output can be current limited through its bias input<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup>, and a regulator such as the LT3080 provides internal current limiting and thermal shutdown as standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> A linear supply design can be simplified by using the regulator's own adjust pin to control current as well as voltage, rather than adding a separate limiting stage downstream, though a [[current-sense-resistor|current sense resistor]] is still needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Host ports are commonly limited as well, with local regulation providing current-limited 500 mA to a front-panel USB [[connector]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> Protection of this kind — current limiting together with overvoltage clamping — is what distinguishes industrial-grade I/O from ordinary I/O.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-979" title="EEVblog #979 - Mailbag">979</a></sup>

Some products omit it. A supply may be sold with voltage and current displays but no current limiting at all<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-272" title="EEVblog #272 - Manson 9400 40A 3-15V Switchmode PSU Teardown">272</a></sup>, and a toothbrush charger may connect the rectified output of its coupling coil straight to the cell with nothing in series, relying instead on the maximum current the coil can deliver as the effective charging limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup>

## Interactions and side effects

Interrupting current to an inductive load causes the voltage to fly in one direction or the other, so when VI limiting engages in an amplifier driving a heavily inductive load, catch diodes to the positive rail and to [[ground]] are required to absorb it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>

Current limiting also confounds diagnosis. In a supply whose rails are of the current limiting type with series limit resistors, excessive loading anywhere causes the affected rail to read 20 to 30% low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> That signature can be misread: if one board on the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] were faulty, the limiting should pull down only that rail, so all rails sagging together points to a mechanism other than a single board fault — though limiting further downstream can also drag rails down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup> Confirming that a supply is not entering current limit rules the supply out as the cause when a system fails to boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup> A supply's own limit can intervene unexpectedly and slow an unrelated measurement, particularly with a modest wattage source and long leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1254" title="EEVblog #1254 - Mailbag">1254</a></sup>

Limiting works alongside, not instead of, other protection. An output [[fuse]] would not normally be expected to blow because the channel's current limiting stops it first, but it is worth having as backup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> When a supply rated to current limit at 3 A instead blows a pass [[transistor]] and its 5 A output fuse under a short applied through [[relay]] contacts, that is a defect rather than expected behaviour: a supply of that grade should current limit at 3 A and handle the fault properly, and a good supply should survive repeated dead shorts on its output, which is what the current limiting is for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup>
