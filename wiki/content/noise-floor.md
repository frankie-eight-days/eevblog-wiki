The noise floor is the residual signal an instrument or circuit produces with no input, and it sets the smallest thing that can usefully be measured: a signal buried beneath it cannot be recovered, and a signal near it is contaminated by it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> Because it is the limit rather than a nuisance, it is the first specification worth checking on any measuring instrument, and it is checked by measuring the instrument against itself — a shorted, terminated, or disconnected input — before any device under test is connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-529" title="EEVblog #529 - HP 35660A DSA Upgrade Investigation">529</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Noise floor is not a single number: it depends on measurement [[bandwidth]], on front-end design, on converter resolution, and on how much post-acquisition processing is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

## Bandwidth dependence

The noise floor changes with the bandwidth over which it is measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> For an [[oscilloscope]] this means the higher the instrument's bandwidth, the higher its residual noise floor, all other things being equal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup> Engaging a [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]] visibly drops the trace: switching a 200 MHz front end from full bandwidth to a 20 MHz limit brings the [[noise]] under one division with a floating input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup>

The limit belongs to the physical front end, not to the setting. A 350 MHz analogue front end software-limited to 200 MHz is still noisier than a front end built for 200 MHz — 57 µV standard deviation against 45 µV in a direct comparison — because the noise is baked into the hardware design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1612" title="EEVblog 1612 - Siglent SDS1000X HD 12 bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1612</a></sup> Two scopes of different bandwidth are therefore only comparable once both are constrained: with both set to a 20 MHz limit, one instrument's mean fell from about 65 µV to 38 µV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1223" title="EEVblog #1223 - Oscilloscope Standard Deviation Noise Measurement">1223</a></sup>

Bandwidth is also the lever on [[current]]-probe noise. The I-Prober 520's toroid attachment specifies 6 mA RMS over its full DC-to-5 MHz bandwidth but 1.5 mA RMS at the minimum 2 Hz bandwidth setting, which puts its practical floor at a few milliamps and rules out microamp work entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

## Converter resolution and processing gain

Vertical resolution and noise floor move together. A 12-bit converter with a low noise floor produces a visibly cleaner trace than an 8-bit instrument on the same 50 Ω terminated signal at the same bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup> Going further, a true 14-bit ADC halves the noise floor relative to a 12-bit competitor; in one such instrument the floor sits around −109 to −110 dB, low enough that a 15 µV signal passed through a 12 dB attenuator is still visible poking out of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> High-resolution modes trade [[sample-rate|sample rate]] for depth by high-order averaging of the bits, giving a lower noise floor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-223" title="EEVblog #223 - Agilent Oscilloscope High Res Mode">223</a></sup> The distinction between a genuine converter and a computed one matters: a 14-bit ADC reaching 16 bits in high-definition mode is a different proposition from a 12-bit ADC averaged up to 18.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup>

Digital signal processing after the converter buys a great deal. A plain 8-bit converter gives only about 50 dB of noise floor, whereas even an entry-level spectrum analyser is expected to deliver around 100 dB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> Digital down conversion followed by discrete Fourier transforms produces processing gain, which lowers the effective noise floor because the result is computed over a much smaller bandwidth; in one documented case this improved signal-to-noise by roughly 57 dB and yielded a 107 dB noise floor from an 8-bit converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> The same mechanism appears in a scope [[fft|FFT]], where increasing the transform length lowers the noise floor as more data becomes available, and where enabling high-resolution acquisition drops it further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup> [[calibration|Calibration]]-grade instrumentation exploits the same effect, using an FFT rather than a conventional converter for better [[dynamic-range|dynamic range]] and a lower noise floor, particularly close in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1041" title="EEVblog #1041 - Keysight Calibration Lab Tour">1041</a></sup>

Averaging attacks the same problem in the time domain, pulling periodic content out of the noise: 512 averages will lift a peak that is otherwise invisible, and at very low signal levels a [[sine-wave|sine wave]] becomes recognisable only after tens of averages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup> Relating the noise floor to the full-scale input is what gives effective number of bits, and hence usable rather than nominal resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>

## Apparent versus real noise floor

A displayed trace can misrepresent the underlying noise. One scope appearing to have a lower noise floor than another turned out to differ only in update rate and [[memory-depth|memory depth]]; changing the memory depth changed the apparent noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> The remedy is a numeric measurement — standard deviation of the acquired samples — rather than a visual judgement of trace thickness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1223" title="EEVblog #1223 - Oscilloscope Standard Deviation Noise Measurement">1223</a></sup> At coarse vertical settings the floor may be visible as nothing more than the last few bits refusing to settle: three bits oscillating at 10 mV/div is the noise floor of that instrument, and it can equally be expressed in the [[frequency]] domain, around −74 dBV for the same hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup>

Logarithmic display exists largely because of this dynamic range problem. With a noise floor at 10 µV against a 1 V full scale, a linear axis would need to be divided into 100,000 parts before the noise became visible at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-49" title="EEVblog #49 - Decibels (dB&#x27;s) for Engineers - A Tutorial">49</a></sup>

## Measuring low noise requires a lower noise instrument

Characterising noise demands a measurement chain quieter than the thing being characterised. A dynamic signal analyser used for op-amp [[voltage]] noise had its own floor of 31.3 nV/√Hz at 1 kHz — around 22 nV/√Hz by a later reckoning at the same frequency — which is not spectacular, and that floor was documented before anything else was attempted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-529" title="EEVblog #529 - HP 35660A DSA Upgrade Investigation">529</a></sup> Measuring at these levels also requires [[shielding]]: a Faraday cage, typically a diecast alloy box, is the standard method.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup>

When the device under test is quieter than the analyser, the noises still sum, so the quieter device registers as a bump above the instrument floor rather than a reading in its own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> Conversely a genuinely good op-amp measured 33.7 nV/√Hz — only a couple of nV above the analyser's own floor — meaning the instrument, not the part, is what is being measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> The way out is external gain: a low-noise preamplifier of about 4 nV/√Hz placed ahead of the analyser lifts the signal well above the floor so the absolute performance of the part can be resolved, and characterising state-of-the-art op-amps requires exactly this, with the front-end amplifier itself carefully designed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> The same reasoning applies to a scope-based EMC probe: rather than buying a low-noise scope front end, a higher-gain preamplifier — 20, 30 or 40 dB — is fitted so the scope's own noise floor stops mattering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup>

Cascading two measurements does not multiply their dynamic ranges. Notching a −80 dB-capable source into a −80 dB-capable analyser does not yield 160 dB of range, because the noise floor and residual analogue distortion of the second instrument reassert themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vwAhHz7Zpzk" title="Guest Video - Karl Adams - Audio Distortion Measurement">•</a></sup>

## Spectrum analysers

For a spectrum analyser the noise floor is the reference against which everything is judged, and it is set jointly by hardware and [[resolution-bandwidth|resolution bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> A direct comparison of two entry-level analysers at identical attenuation, detector and reference settings over a 1.5 GHz span showed −65 dBm against −85 dBm at 1 MHz RBW, a full 20 dB advantage, narrowing to 15–20 dB at 100 kHz RBW.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup> A better noise floor combined with a 10 Hz minimum resolution bandwidth against 100 Hz makes the difference between resolving detail and not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup> Narrowing RBW lowers the floor, but there is a point beyond which reducing it will not expose an artefact caused by internal coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup>

Absolute figures at the higher end run far lower: −156 dBm for one instrument against −161 dBm for a better-specified sibling, a difference traceable to the hardware rather than the [[firmware]], while phase noise between the two was practically identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> A typical working figure with an unterminated input at 10 kHz RBW sits just above −115 dBm, rising with frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> The input attenuator and preamplifier blocks are the parts that determine this, and are given their own shielded can inside an already shielded block for that reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> A dedicated RF input, as opposed to a general-purpose analogue channel pressed into service, is what gives a mixed-domain instrument its low noise floor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

In EMC pre-compliance work the inherent noise floor of the analyser and its cabling is established first, with the vertical scale referenced to 1 µV, and pass/fail limit lines are set above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> A misbehaving transmitter that generates broadband noise raises the noise floor across the band rather than producing discrete spikes, which is distinguishable from the natural floor simply by disconnecting the offending unit and watching the elevated region vanish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup>

## Probes and signal conditioning

Attenuation ahead of the instrument costs noise floor. Using a 10:1 probe to gain bandwidth divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope input, raising the effective noise floor — an explicit trade-off when working at 50 mV/div.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> The same effect appears within a probe: switching a differential probe to its ×100 range raises its own noise floor to the point that a common-mode rejection plot goes flat and featureless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Dez9KG6whb0" title="CMRR Followup Micsig DP10007 vs HVP70">•</a></sup> Measurements taken below the noise floor become visibly jagged and unreliable, which is the signature that the plot has run out of dynamic range rather than that the device has a real response there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Dez9KG6whb0" title="CMRR Followup Micsig DP10007 vs HVP70">•</a></sup>

Where the gain stage sits determines the result. Placing gain ahead of the attenuating network, rather than a ×10 stage afterwards, buys a lower noise floor at the cost of an extra part, and selecting a quieter differential receiver — an AD8129A at 200 MHz in place of a 270 MHz alternative — trades a little bandwidth for lower noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> An active probe compared side by side against a competitor at the same 20 mV/div with the inputs shorted shows the difference plainly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> An optically isolated probe reached roughly −80 dBm across its 200 MHz bandwidth, with roll-off beginning at the bandwidth limit as expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

## Analogue circuits and audio

In analogue equipment the noise floor is often a property of one part rather than of the layout. Audible hiss from studio monitor speakers was isolated to the output power amplifier chip, and is inherent in that chip's noise floor irrespective of input termination — a reasonably common characteristic of powered monitors, some of which fit an auto-mute to cut the output entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1128" title="EEVblog #1128 - Studio Monitor Speaker Noise - Part 2">1128</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> A microphone's noise floor is likewise a selection criterion, an instrument microphone being chosen over a lapel type partly for its lower floor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a5g7-YKhveA" title="Rode Microphone Comparison - Lapel vs M5">•</a></sup> Acoustic instruments have the same limit expressed in dBA: a sound level meter with an 18 dBA noise floor is still not quiet enough to be irrelevant when the ambient reading is 33 dBA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1745" title="EEVblog 1745 - Continuity Tester Spectral Probing">1745</a></sup>

Noise floor is also one of the deliberate trade-offs in a measurement front end. In one power meter a residual offset of about 1.8 µA was left uncorrected because the design prioritised a lower noise floor and wider bandwidth over minimum [[dc-offset|DC offset]]; trimming the offset digitally would have degraded both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>

Device-to-device variation in a parameter such as an op-amp's noise floor follows the same Gaussian manufacturing distribution as [[resistor-tolerance|resistor tolerance]], so a [[datasheet]] figure describes a probability of falling within a range rather than a guarantee for the individual part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup>

## Practical significance

The distinction between a bench instrument and a cheaper alternative frequently comes down to noise floor alongside [[input-protection|input protection]], vertical amplifier performance, [[waveform]] processing speed and triggering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 2 of 2 - Comparison of PC Based Oscilloscopes">13</a></sup> The same is true when choosing between a modern low-cost analyser and secondhand professional equipment, where RF performance means noise floor and bandwidth first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> On any instrument the practical procedure is unchanged: measure the floor with no input, at the vertical setting and bandwidth actually to be used, before trusting anything measured above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup>
