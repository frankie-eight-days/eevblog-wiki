A virtual ground is a circuit node that behaves as [[ground]] — sits at the reference potential, sinks and sources [[current]], and shows no signal — without being physically connected to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup> The term covers two distinct things that share a name: the inverting input node of an op-amp in a feedback configuration, held at the reference potential by op-amp action alone<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>, and a deliberately generated mid-rail reference that serves as the circuit's ground when only a single supply is available<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup>. In both cases the node is a ground by behaviour rather than by wiring, and understanding it is the usual barrier to analysing inverting amplifiers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup>

## The inverting input as a virtual ground

The mechanism follows from two op-amp rules: no current flows into or out of the inputs, and the amplifier drives its output to whatever [[voltage]] is required to make the two input voltages equal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup> In an inverting amplifier the non-inverting input is tied to ground and cannot move, so the amplifier must adjust its output until the inverting input also sits at zero volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> That node is the virtual ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup>

The effect is directly measurable. Probing the inverting node of a working inverting amplifier shows no signal at all — the input [[waveform]] is present on the far side of the input [[resistor]] and vanishes at the summing junction, leaving a trace that is flat as a tack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> This is the point that confuses beginners, who probe around a circuit they have just built and cannot account for where the signal went.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup> The signal has not disappeared and neither has the current: current still flows through the input resistor obeying Ohm's law, and at the junction current in equals current out — Kirchhoff's current law applied at the virtual ground node is the standard way to analyse the configuration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

The gain follows from the same picture. Because the junction between the input and feedback resistors is pinned at the virtual ground, a small input movement forces a proportionally larger output movement to hold that node in place, the ratio being set by the two resistor values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup> Hence the inverting gain is RF/R1, with no plus one, and with a negative sign because the output is inverted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

The clarifying point about the potential itself is that zero volts is a position in a chain, not an absence of anything: the two inputs are equal at zero volts simply because that is the same voltage as wherever the measurement reference has been placed, not because nothing is there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup>

The node also does not have to be at zero. Feeding another voltage into the non-inverting pin offsets the virtual ground to that voltage — commonly half rail, generated by a [[resistor-divider|resistive divider]] from V+ to ground — and the node is still called a virtual ground, because the property that names it is the forcing action, not the potential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

## Consequences for the surrounding circuit

Because the inverting input is held at the reference potential and no current flows through the input resistor when the input is grounded, the configuration is a clean setting in which to observe [[input-bias-current|input bias current]], which persists regardless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup>

Whatever voltage the non-inverting pin is given is transferred exactly to the virtual ground, so [[noise]] on that reference is amplified along with the signal and appears on the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The standard remedy is heavy bypassing at the non-inverting reference — a 1 or 10 µF [[capacitor]] across it — to make the virtual ground quiet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

The virtual ground is also where non-ideal behaviour becomes visible. With a 1 kΩ input resistor and a 10 kΩ [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]], the output waveform carries small disturbances, and probing the virtual ground point resolves them into spikes that correspond one-for-one with the bumps in the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

## Generated virtual grounds for single-supply circuits

Where split rails are wanted from a single [[battery]], a virtual ground can be synthesised: divide the supply with two resistors, buffer the midpoint with a unity-gain voltage follower, and declare the buffer output to be the circuit's ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup> The op-amp output is not being shorted, because it is not referenced to anything else — it becomes the reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> From a single 3 V cell this yields ±1.5 V rails from one cheap op-amp with no [[switching-noise|switching noise]], and leaves a single battery to monitor for low-voltage detection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

The defining property of such a reference is that every node in the circuit is referenced to it, so the entire circuit shifts with it. Applying 1 V to the generated ground moves the whole circuit up by 1 V, and the op-amp output that read 1 V with respect to that ground still reads 1 V with respect to it after the shift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup> This is what makes it a true virtual ground, usable as the sole ground in a topology such as a Howland current pump, where the circuit already has only one ground node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup>

## Stability of buffered virtual grounds

A generated virtual ground is an op-amp output, and it inherits every op-amp output's sensitivity to capacitive loading. In the µCurrent, a single coin cell is split by an LMV321 to produce the reference ground output, giving ±1.33 V about it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup> The reference is derived from 200 kΩ dividers R6 and R7 feeding the op-amp, and the output reaches the negative output terminal through a 270 Ω [[series-resistor|series resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup>

Bypassing that virtual ground to both supply rails — 0.1 µF ceramics either side of the divider capacitor, effectively across the battery — stops [[oscillation]] in the problematic LMV321 variants.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup> It is not, however, a clean fix, and the reason it was omitted from the original design is that this arrangement was found to cause stability problems in earlier testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup> The caps are being added to the op-amp's own output, and the 270 Ω series resistor does not isolate the amplifier from that [[capacitance]]; it shifts the pole rather than removing the load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup> Adding capacitance to an op-amp output is generally a bad idea even when, as here, testing shows it working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup>

Solving the problem on the input side is technically preferable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup> Placing a 0.1 µF ceramic across either divider resistor — R6 or R7, the choice makes no difference — also stops the oscillation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup> But with no bypassing on the virtual ground pin and bypassing on the input instead, the pulse response departs slightly from the ideal, so bypassing the virtual ground with two caps gives the better result in this particular case despite being the less principled approach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup>

Diagnosing such a circuit is complicated by where the capacitance actually sits. In the µCurrent the MAX439 bypass caps are across V+ and V− — directly across the battery rather than on the output — which makes the effective capacitance on the virtual ground relative to either rail hard to establish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup> The behaviour is also strongly part-dependent: the Texas Instruments part of identical specification never oscillates with any capacitive load and is completely robust, while the 321T and 321S variants are the troublesome ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup> When [[troubleshooting]] a suspected split-rail fault, measuring the battery from the reference ground output confirms whether the splitter itself is at fault before looking elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup> For bench work generally, a generated virtual-ground split supply and a laboratory split bench supply give the same measurement results, as would be expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-476" title="EEVblog #476 - Opamp Offset Voltage Measurement">476</a></sup>
