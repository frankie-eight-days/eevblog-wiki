Measurement accuracy is the degree to which an instrument's reading corresponds to the true value of the quantity being measured, and it is the property that determines whether a bench result means anything at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup> It is distinct from resolution — the number of digits or counts a display can show — and the two are confused constantly, including by people who know better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup> An instrument can display far more digits than its accuracy justifies, in which case the trailing figures carry no information and exist only for show.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup>

## Accuracy versus resolution

Resolution is the fineness of the display: counts, digits, decimal places. It has nothing to do with accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup> Describing a meter as having an accuracy of four and a half digits, or an accuracy of 10,000 counts, is simply wrong; those figures are resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup> A [[current-measurement|current measurement]] front end may resolve down to 100 nanoamps while being specified nowhere near that well in absolute terms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> The same split appears in decade [[resistance]] boxes, where a box specified to 0.1% at 1 kΩ can still be set with 0.1 ohm resolution — the resolution is finer than the accuracy of the value it produces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup> On a benchtop [[power-supply|power supply]], an option that unlocks an extra digit of readback resolution does not change accuracy at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup>

## How accuracy is specified

A meter's accuracy is expressed as a percentage of the actual reading, not of full scale, plus a fixed number of counts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup> A good handheld might be 0.1% plus two counts, which on a 1 volt measurement corresponds to an uncertainty of about one millivolt from the percentage term before the counts are added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup> The counts term is where cheap instruments lose. A Fluke 87 V is quoted at 0.05% plus one count on basic DC volts, and the plus-one is the significant half of that figure — lesser meters advertise a similar 0.05% but pair it with plus four or plus five counts, which must be folded into any real uncertainty calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10" title="EEVblog #10 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 87 V Multimeter Review">10</a></sup> The same pattern holds for LCR meters, where a 0.5% plus one count headline can beat more expensive instruments quoted at 0.5% with a higher count adder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup> The counts term dominates when the reading has no decimal places to spare: a tweezer [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] specified at 5% plus three digits can read visibly wrong on a low-value part and still be inside its specification once the digits are counted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> A meter that looks 0.04% out of a 0.025% class spec may likewise fall back inside once the plus-five-counts allowance is included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup>

Accuracy specifications are also rarely a single number. LCR meters carry whole tables of figures broken out by range and test [[frequency]], and the headline basic accuracy is met only in the middle ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> A 0.7% plus three digit basic spec can degrade to 1% plus five counts at the low end and 5% plus counts on a 10 millifarad range, and this range dependence is near-universal across the category.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup> Bridges show the same spread from the opposite direction: 0.01% in the comfortable middle, rising to about 1.5% out in the teraohms, where sufficient measuring [[current]] only exists if a high bridge [[voltage]] is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup> [[temperature-coefficient|Temperature coefficient]] is part of the specification too; older instruments sometimes quote it per 10 kelvin rather than per degree Celsius.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-482" title="EEVblog #482 - Retro Iskra Multimeter Teardown">482</a></sup> Warm-up matters as well — an instrument switched on and used immediately is not yet in a state where its specification can be fairly judged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-FgkD8K0Ssdc" title="Fluke 287 Repair - My Arse">•</a></sup>

## Verifying accuracy

Checking one instrument requires a better one. The working rule for [[calibration]] is a reference an order of magnitude — ten times — better than the device under test, so a 0.1% meter needs a 0.01% reference and comparing two identical meters against each other proves nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1496" title="EEVblog 1496 - Winning Mailbag">1496</a></sup> That ratio of ten is a test uncertainty ratio of 10; four is treated as the floor in the calibration industry rather than the target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> At the extreme metrology end the ratio can collapse to a factor of a few, at which point the exercise becomes considerably harder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1496" title="EEVblog 1496 - Winning Mailbag">1496</a></sup> Once the reference's own accuracy is folded in, agreement between two good instruments quickly becomes a question about the reference rather than the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-377" title="EEVblog #377 - Mailbag Monday">377</a></sup>

Spot checks against a reference source are the ordinary bench form of this. They are informative but weak evidence, since a single unit says nothing about a production population.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup> Testing a whole batch of the same model against a reference generator addresses that directly, characterising how close a given design lands out of the box across ranges rather than how one favourable sample behaves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup>

## Improving accuracy in practice

Several techniques buy accuracy at the cost of speed or convenience. Integration time in a bench [[multimeter]] is set in number of power line cycles; more cycles slow the measurement but increase accuracy and reject 50/60 Hz [[noise]], because the integration happens in the ADC rather than as smoothing applied afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Pushing to 100 power line cycles is very slow and considerably more accurate, and can gain a digit of usable reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> On an [[oscilloscope]], filling more of the vertical range with the signal reduces the error standard deviation of a measurement and changes how many decimal places the instrument is willing to report.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup> High resolution or boxcar averaging mode differs from mathematical averaging in ways that range from negligible to large enough to change measurement accuracy and the apparent shape of the [[waveform]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> Analogue ohm scales are non-linear, so readings taken well into the range are more accurate than those near the crowded end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> Clamp meters have a defined sweet spot for conductor position that is not always the jaw centre — on a 600 amp range it is central, while a lower current range may specify an offset position marked by arrows, and meeting the accuracy specification on that range requires using it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup>

## Fitness for purpose

Required accuracy follows from the measurement, not from what is available. Characterising a [[battery-discharge-curve|battery discharge curve]] is served perfectly well by 1% absolute accuracy in voltage and power, so a 0.05% class instrument adds nothing to the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Conversely, some measurements carry legal weight: electricity metering falls under legal metrology, where accuracy underpins a commercial transaction in the same way a post office scale does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1446" title="EEVblog 1446 - Analog Watt Hour Meters are AMAZING!">1446</a></sup>

At the low end, a quoted figure can be honest and still misleading. A cheap meter quoted at 0.05% may meet that specification on one range out of the box and drift away from it, because the components chosen to hit the price cannot hold the figure over time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 2 of 2 - Why cheap multimeters suck">6</a></sup> This is the reason for keeping a second meter to cross-check anything important.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 2 of 2 - Why cheap multimeters suck">6</a></sup> Marketing sometimes abandons the pretence altogether: one crowdfunded multimeter advertised "rockuracy" and its makers later conceded the measured performance was worse than initially claimed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> Instruments do fail their published numbers under test — a wrist-worn meter specified at 0.5% measured around 3% on two DC volt ranges, an [[electronic-load|electronic load]] specified at 0.05% plus 0.05% of full scale read several tenths of a percent out, and a handheld's [[capacitance]] ranges came in 5 to 7% out against a 1% plus counts claim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1706" title="EEVblog 1706 - K8 Wrist Worn Multimeter REVIEW">1706</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> A microamp range reading 1.4 to 1.5% error where 0.1 to 0.2% was expected is roughly an order of magnitude wrong and indicates a fault rather than a tolerance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup>

At the top end the figure becomes the selling point: a high-end real-time oscilloscope is marketed as "Engineered for unmatched real-time measurement accuracy." on the strength of its low jitter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-342" title="EEVblog #342 - Agilent 90000 Oscilloscope Teardown">342</a></sup> Elsewhere the numbers are simply the class of the tool — 0.008% basic accuracy on an [[impedance]] analyser<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup>, 0.1% across the ranges of a power analyser<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup>, roughly 0.005% one-year accuracy on a 6½ digit bench meter<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup>, 0.04% basic DC volts on an older microvolt DMM<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup>, 0.5% DC volts on an entry-level handheld<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>, 0.8% DC volts on a pocket meter<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup>, about 2% typical on a [[thermocouple]] front end<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup>, and 3% or so on an insulation resistance tester, which is more than adequate for what insulation testing asks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup> Thermal stability is a separate axis: a handheld holding better than 0.01% across a 32 °C delta is doing well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Wwz_fdU17aQ" title="121GW Multimeter Thermal Test">•</a></sup>
