A comparator is a differential amplifier operated without negative feedback, so that its output slams to one supply rail or the other depending on which of its two inputs is more positive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> It is the boundary device between the analog and digital worlds: it takes a continuously varying [[voltage]], compares it against a reference, and emits a one or a zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> Because it is the simplest way to turn a threshold into a decision, it appears wherever a circuit must notice that something has crossed a limit — a [[battery]] has run flat, a rail has collapsed, a [[current]] has hit its ceiling, a signal has crossed zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

## Operating principle

With no external feedback, the open-loop gain of a differential amplifier is so large that any practical input difference drives the output into saturation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> An input difference of a millivolt, or of one microvolt, is enough; the device does not amplify the difference, it merely reports its sign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Feeding 1 V into one input and 1.001 V into the other sends the output hard to the positive rail, and reversing the two sends it slamming down to the negative rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The switching threshold is set by whatever reference sits on the other input, commonly a [[resistor-divider]] from the supply — two 1 K resistors from a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] giving a 2.5 V reference is the textbook arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> With the reference at ground, the circuit becomes a zero-crossing detector.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

Internally a comparator is built as a differential pair, the same topology as an amplifier input stage, distinguished only by the absence of external feedback.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Crude differential pairs work reasonably well in this role, but they need current sources to behave decently; a typical comparator may contain three or four constant-current generators scattered through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## Op-amps used as comparators

An op-amp run open-loop is a comparator, and this substitution is common enough that spare sections of a quad op-amp routinely serve as threshold detectors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup> An LM324 quad op-amp is described in circuit terms as a quad comparator when that is the job it is doing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> The practice is legitimate but compromised: an op-amp makes a very crude comparator, usable in a pinch, and is not as good as a purpose-built part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> Op-amps are not designed for the output saturation and slew demands of comparison, and lack the open-collector output and switching speed of a real comparator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>

The distinction is visible in circuit topology. A comparator connection is recognisable by positive feedback, or by no feedback at all, where an amplifier would have negative feedback.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> In a lab supply current limiter, the op-amp has no feedback around it and is therefore working as a comparator; because its input is high [[impedance]], the driver stage ahead of it can be deleted entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Left genuinely open-loop, the same device switches hard: disconnecting the base of the pass [[transistor]] removes the loop and the output snaps like a true comparator, whereas with the loop closed the device still does the job of a comparator without cleanly going between one and zero, because it is operating inside the feedback path in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> The practical consequence is that hanging an [[led]] directly on such an output does not work, and a separate transistor driven from the current-limit comparator is the better arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

## Noise, hysteresis and the Schmitt trigger

The defining weakness of an ideal comparator is that its threshold is infinitely sharp. The input needs to be only a fraction — "half a bee's dick" — above or below the reference, by a microvolt or a nanovolt, and the output will switch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Real signals carry noise, so a slowly moving input crossing the threshold produces a burst of oscillation on the transition rather than a clean edge; this is a genuine trap, distinct from metastability, with which it should not be confused.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Slow input slew rates on digital circuitry are the companion problem, and both have the same cure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

The cure is positive feedback, which converts the fixed reference into one that toggles between two values depending on the output state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> With a 9 K and 1 K divider from an output that swings to ±10 V, the divider ratio of 0.1 places the upper threshold at +1 V and the lower at −1 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> The formula is the ordinary voltage-divider relation applied to the maximum output swing, with the caveat that a real output may not reach the rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> An open-collector part such as the LM311 complicates the calculation slightly, because the low output level sits above ground by the drop across the pull-down path, but in practice a 10 K or 20 K feedback [[resistor]] is simply added and tested.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Hysteresis appears in working designs for exactly this reason: a 10 M feedback resistor around the comparator in a voice-recognition front end supplies it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup>

## Output stages and drive

A large open-collector output driver is characteristic of the classic comparator, and exists because the load is often not digital logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> An open-collector output can pull a [[relay]] coil, a solenoid, or a high-current LED or LED string directly, and can withstand an output voltage well above the chip's own rated supply — 50 V at up to 50 mA in the case of the jellybean parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> The trade-off is that open-collector devices dominate the older jellybean families and bring with them non-rail-to-rail inputs and limited speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>

## Common-mode input range

Common-mode input range is a key specification for a comparator, as it is for an op-amp, and violating it is a classic failure mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> A circuit that appears to work may be operating outside the specified range and succeeding only because the practical part is better than its [[datasheet|data sheet]] guarantees — which is not a basis for a design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Rail-to-rail comparators solve the problem at a cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Parts rated from 2 V upwards, such as the LM393 and the 74HC14 Schmitt [[inverter]] used as an alternative, are chosen for low-voltage split-supply work on this basis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup>

## Jellybean parts

The comparator category is less clear-cut than op-amps or regulators: a few parts are definite jellybeans, and a few oddballs earn their place by necessity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> The industry-standard comparator, the one used everywhere, is the LM393, dating from October 1979 — roughly equivalent to the LM311 but in the same SO8 package with two comparators instead of one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> The LM311 is the classic single jellybean part and is the reference point in tutorial circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The LM339 quad comparator turns up constantly in production hardware, from Amiga mouse encoders to source-measure units, battery emulators and mailbag teardowns, sometimes as a TLC-prefixed CMOS version.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> A step above the open-collector jellybeans is the TLV370x family — the 3701, 3702 and 3704 from TI, second-sourced from ST as the TSR3702 — not jellybean parts but the next useful tier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>

The TL431 shunt reference can also be pressed into service: its reference input is uncommitted, so the internal amplifier acts as a comparator whose output switches a series pass transistor fully on or fully off according to whether the external reference input sits above or below the internal 2.5 V reference, which is what makes it useful for power-supply feedback.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

## Threshold detection and supervision

Low-voltage battery detection is the archetypal application, and every implementation is essentially the same thing: a precision [[voltage-reference]] and a comparator, which switch on an output when the supply drops below a set level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> For a product designed to cut out at about 2.7 V from a 3 V supply, or at a typical 1 V per cell for a modern active product, the comparator and its reference drive a low-battery LED, shut the product down, or curtail functionality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> A second-order effect of such circuits is that the extra current drawn when the indicator LED turns on pulls the terminal voltage down through the battery's ESR, dropping it back below the comparator threshold and producing a flashing indicator as an unintended side effect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-576" title="EEVblog #576 - Advantest R6142 Current Voltage Generator">576</a></sup>

Power-supply supervision works the same way at a larger scale. A quad comparator such as the LM339 takes in the +5, +12, −12 and −5 rails, compares each against a reference on its input pins, and the results are summed to produce a power-good signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> A machine whose negative rails have failed will therefore be held in reset by a signal-bad output, preventing the processor from booting at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> The same pattern shows up as rail checking on old power supplies with a 339 alongside a few op-amps, potentially serving a supervisory role over an entire system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1420" title="EEVblog 1420 - Mailbag">1420</a></sup> Dedicated overload and half-rail detection circuitry in test instruments is likewise built from op-amps and comparators operating entirely in the analog domain, independently of the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> A simple overload indicator can be nothing more than a comparator built from an LM741 driving an overload lamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

Window comparison — testing whether a signal lies between two limits rather than above one — is a recurring variant, whether done with rough op-amp sections or by using all four comparators in a quad package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-275" title="EEVblog #275 - PIR Sensor Teardown &amp; Tutorial">275</a></sup> Level detection in alarm and metering circuitry may drive relays that physically disable an output rather than merely flagging the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup>

## Signal conditioning and digitisation

Converting an analog [[waveform]] into something a [[microcontroller]] can read without an ADC is a standard comparator job: the signal is squared up and clipped into a clean digital level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup> In a voice-recognition front end the chain is a microphone, a high-gain amplifier with a bandpass filter, and a comparator feeding the recogniser chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup> Gain setting becomes critical in such a chain — too much gain and the comparator output is garbage even with no one speaking, too little and the signal never reaches the comparator threshold and events are missed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup>

The same trick converts a [[sine-wave]] into a pulse train for [[frequency]] counting, the comparator producing one pulse per positive excursion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> Timer-counters are, at heart, a microprocessor with input differential amplifiers, comparators and dividers, gating the result against a reference oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-265" title="EEVblog #265 - Philips PM6672 Timer Counter Teardown">265</a></sup> Function generators traditionally exploited the reverse: rather than synthesising square waves with a DAC, they generated a triangle wave and ran it into a comparator, an approach that constrained the design to two or three selectable slew rates and a 10% minimum duty cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 3 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

Time-domain conversion of a [[resistance]] uses comparators against a voltage ramp. In an analog PC joystick, a JFET ramp generator feeds four comparators; as the ramp rises past each axis [[potentiometer]]'s voltage the corresponding comparator flips, and the host reads the result as a time period — functionally identical to a 555-based implementation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> A comparator can also replace an ADC outright: an instrument with DACs but no ADC anywhere on the board is presumably generating a reference with the DACs and comparing measured voltages against it to detect end-of-charge levels, rather than measuring them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup> Hardware [[auto-ranging|autoranging]] in a meter works the same way, with comparators setting the thresholds at which range changes occur — there being no reason to fit the comparators at all if the ranging were done in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

## Comparators inside other devices

The 555 timer is internally two comparators, a flip-flop, an output stage and a reset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The internal resistor ladder of three 5 K resistors sets the threshold comparator at 2/3 of the supply, so on a 9 V supply the timing [[capacitor]] charges toward a 6 V threshold; the control-voltage pin connects directly to one of those ladder taps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The two comparators are the threshold and trigger comparators, sometimes called the upper and lower comparators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

The LM3914 bar/dot display driver is a buffer and a [[diode]] input clamp driving a bank of comparators fed from an internal resistor ladder of 1 K sections, whose outputs drive the LEDs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> For a battery gauge the cell voltage must be brought down by a divider to fall within the workable range of those internal comparators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> The built-in comparators make the part usable for centre-or-not indication in equipment where discrete comparators would have been the expected choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-709" title="EEVblog #709 - EDC 4601 AC Voltage Standard Teardown">709</a></sup>

Battery charger ICs contain a reference generator and a bank of comparators for the different charge modes — preconditioning, termination, end of charge — alongside the pass transistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Uncommitted logic arrays of the early 1980s bundled an ADC, comparators and an R-2R DAC into a single custom part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-501" title="EEVblog #501 - Sinclair C5 Electric Car Teardown &amp; Test Drive">501</a></sup> PIR motion detectors follow a JFET-buffered pyroelectric sensor with an amplifier and a comparator, sometimes a full window detector using all four comparators of a quad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-275" title="EEVblog #275 - PIR Sensor Teardown &amp; Tutorial">275</a></sup> A metal detector filters the oscillator waveform out of the sense-coil signal before a comparator that senses amplitude relative to half rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> An optical mouse encoder feeds its phototransistor outputs through a quad comparator straight to the controller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup>

## Microcontroller peripherals

Comparators are near-universal on-chip peripherals, including at the very bottom of the market. A three-cent microcontroller in a five-pin SOT23 offers a comparator and an internal voltage reference while having no ADC at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup> A ten-cent RISC-V part carries an op-amp comparator alongside sleep modes, a programmable voltage detector and a 64-bit unique ID.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1524" title="EEVblog 1524 - The 10 CENT RISC V Processor! CH32V003">1524</a></sup> Larger parts pair comparators with wake-up capability so they can run independently of the core: two ultra-low-power comparators with wake-up on one ARM device, two low-power comparators operable in sleep mode and independent of the micro on a programmable-analog part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> Configurable mixed-signal arrays offer comparators, DACs and a little logic for cases where dedicated functionality is needed without committing a microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-833" title="EEVblog #833 - Mailbag">833</a></sup>

The recurring trap with such peripherals is pin multiplexing: comparators share pins with [[lcd]] drive, timers, ADCs, I²C and [[pwm]] outputs, so competing functions often cannot be used simultaneously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> This makes device selection between families genuinely difficult, since LCD drive, timers, ADCs and comparators interact in ways that only test [[firmware]] exercising each peripheral will reveal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup>

## High-speed comparators in instruments

[[oscilloscope]] trigger circuits and logic-analyzer front ends are comparator-dense by nature, since each channel needs an adjustable threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> Their presence is a reliable structural clue: a dense bank of comparators near the mixed-signal connector marks a capable [[logic-analyzer]] with settable threshold levels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> Digital inputs typically pass straight through comparators into the acquisition [[fpga]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> External trigger inputs are usually nothing more than a comparator feeding the FPGA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-521" title="EEVblog #521 - Picoscope 5000 USB Oscilloscope Teardown">521</a></sup>

The parts used are fast. The ADCMP562 is a dual high-speed PECL comparator with 1 ns propagation and 500 ps rise time, and is exactly what belongs in an instrument front end that needs a settable threshold per channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Other examples include the MAX9202 fast quad comparator, the ultrafast AD CMP567, and the CMP401.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Xg_niU86bhI" title="Wens 540 Handheld Oscilloscope Multimeter Teardown">•</a></sup>

Speed imposes power-supply requirements. Fast devices of any digital nature take large gulps of current when switching, so a fast pulse-response [[voltage-regulator]] is fitted locally specifically to drive the input comparators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> Layout matters equally: on a fast ECL board the [[ground-plane]] is deliberately carried unbroken beneath the comparator inputs rather than split, and decoupling at each chip is far more critical there than in the slower buffer sections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup>
