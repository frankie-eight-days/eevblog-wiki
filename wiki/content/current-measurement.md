Current measurement is the third of the three basic [[multimeter]] functions, alongside [[voltage]] and [[resistance]], and the only one of the three that cannot be performed by touching two probes to a live circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Because a meter measures the [[current]] flowing *through* itself, the circuit has to be opened and the instrument inserted in series — a link cut, a trace broken, a jumper pulled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> That single structural fact drives almost everything else about the discipline: the [[burden-voltage]] the meter inserts, the shunt resistors that set its accuracy, the separate input jacks and fuses, and the entire family of clamp, [[transformer]] and field-sensing techniques that exist to avoid breaking the circuit at all.

## The series constraint

A meter placed across a circuit reads voltage; there is no probing sequence that yields current in-circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> To measure the current in a link, the link is cut and the probes placed either side of the gap; to measure it in a board trace, the trace is cut.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> The positive lead must also be moved to the dedicated current jack, which is why meters that measure current are usually identifiable by a third or fourth jack marked A, mA or µA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Some two-jack meters do measure current, but many pocket meters and basic instruments deliberately omit the function — the Fluke 101 has no current ranges at all, while the otherwise similar 107 adds a 10 A range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup> Omitting current is a defensible choice for a general-purpose or household meter, since breaking a circuit is not something a casual user typically needs to do, and some pocket meters are better for leaving it out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup> One meter was advertised with a current capability that was subsequently withdrawn, leaving a 100 mA glass [[fuse]] guarding an input that cannot measure current at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>

The current position is also the dangerous one. A rotary switch turned to a current range and then applied to the mains shorts the supply through the meter's shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> Meters that omit current ranges entirely still have to survive [[mains-voltage]] on every remaining range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> Instruments increasingly detect a lead plugged into a current jack while a voltage function is selected and warn about it, and some warn every time a current range is entered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-FgkD8K0Ssdc" title="Fluke 287 Repair - My Arse">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup>

## Burden voltage

Every multimeter on the market inserts a [[voltage-drop]] when measuring current, developed across its [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] and its fuse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The effect is easy to demonstrate: 1 V across a 100 Ω [[resistor]] gives 10 mA by Ohm's law, and the meter reads 10 mA — until the circuit is broken and the meter inserted in series, at which point the meter's own drop reduces the current actually flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> The consequence is not merely an error in the reading; it can prevent the circuit from working. An attempt to measure the charge current of a fast NiMH charger in the 10 A range failed because the combined drop of the leads and the meter's burden voltage stopped the charger from charging at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

Burden voltage is therefore a specification worth reading. One handheld claims less than 40 mV on its 300 µA range and measures 30 mV in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Redesigning the current front end to lower the drop across both the shunt and the fuse is a recognised design goal for a better multimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

## Shunts, ranges and accuracy

Current is measured by developing a voltage across a shunt resistor, and accurate shunts are hard to make — particularly at high current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup> Meters consequently specify worse DC current accuracy on the 10 A range than on the milliamp and microamp ranges, with the microamp ranges usually the most accurate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A current specification of one percent or one and a half percent is poor; the target is to get as close as possible to the meter's basic DC volts figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A Fluke 28 Series II reaches 0.05% plus one count on DC volts and 0.2% on both ohms and current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> A Fluke 45 specifies 0.05% on current, which is unusually good, though it lacks a microamp range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup> Most meters do not do current particularly well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup>

Shunts are physically trimmed as well as designed. In one meter, heavy copper bands are soldered at an exactly chosen point to set the value of the highest [[current-shunt]], with progressively thinner wire gauges forming the lower ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> Older instruments use a ten-turn trimmer alongside the 10 A jack shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup>

Which ranges an instrument carries matters more than its headline accuracy. A 60,000-count insulation-resistance multimeter with 0.09% accuracy and a 6 µA range has no 10 A jack and tops out around 600 mA, which is a showstopper for anyone doing high-current work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup> A bench THD multimeter offers only 1 A, 100 mA and 10 mA ranges to a 3 A maximum, with no microamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup> A clamp-oriented handheld whose lowest current range is 60 mA is adequate for electrical work and inadequate for electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> A 6½-digit bench meter with milliamps as its lowest range gives only 0.1 µA resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> The most damaging gaps are in the middle: a cheap handheld missing both the 100 mA and 10 mA ranges leaves 9 mA — an everyday electronics value — with essentially no resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup> Absent low ranges are felt too; a 600 µA range that reads about half a percent out is tolerable at a low price point, but the missing 60 µA range is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>

Range and jack handling is a common failure. Two related [[oscilloscope]]-multimeters read 115 mA and 110 mA respectively when fed 1 mA into the correctly chosen milliamp jack with the amps function selected, and one raised its 10 A over-range beeper while on the milliamp jack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup> A bench supply reads a phantom 30 mA with nothing connected whenever its [[current-limit]] is set below 10 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Cheap process calibrators sourcing and measuring current are unreliable enough to be a lottery, one reading over 24 mA at maximum and another producing nonsense — including a negative reading — when sourcing 30 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1257" title="EEVblog #1257 - MORE! $9 0.02% AIMO Process Calibrator">1257</a></sup>

## Designing measurement into a product

Because current requires a broken circuit, well-designed boards provide the break in advance. Development boards commonly fit a jumper in the supply rail specifically for current measurement, so the jumper can be removed and a meter or a microcurrent adapter inserted to read the processor's consumption directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-290" title="EEVblog #290 - Mailbag">290</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup> Vintage instruments do the same at the rail level: link wires along a [[power-rail]] exist so they can be cut and the current on that rail measured during servicing, and jumper links with removable inserts allow currents to be measured and circuits broken into for debug.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup> Where no provision exists it must be improvised — desoldering a [[series-resistor]] and fitting a two-pin header, or tacking a pair of pins onto a pad so an ammeter can be clipped in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> The point of insertion has to be the real return path: measuring only 20 to 30 mA on a supply wire turned out to be an artefact of a [[ribbon-cable]] carrying the return current, and breaking into the correct conductor revealed roughly 700 mA after boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

## Low currents and wide dynamic range

Low-current work is a distinct specialty. The microcurrent adapter uses a MAX4239 [[chopper-amplifier]] to keep offset low, and outputs a scaled voltage — 1 mV per mA on one range — so that an ordinary voltmeter reads current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-24" title="EEVblog #24 - Chopper Operational Amplifiers">24</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> Bench instruments now reach comparably low: one bench supply design measures down to microamp output current with a circuit capped at 10.24 mA, switching to a coarser path above that, giving continuous coverage from a few microamps to a couple of amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> A programmable supply offering 1 µA resolution on its 200 mA range devotes many display digits to current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup>

At the extreme, electrometer-class instruments convert current to voltage across very large fixed resistors — a 250 GΩ resistor serves a 2 pA range — with the input node kept off the [[pcb]] and routed directly to standoffs to control leakage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup> Such gear reaches down toward attoamps and is expensive enough that its owners describe metrology as a path they choose not to go down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-s2KkgI-kyK0" title="The Signal Path Lab">•</a></sup> A femtoammeter of this class ships with its current input disabled by default and must be enabled in the connection settings before it measures anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

Dynamic range, not resolution on any single range, is the design driver. In a combined voltage-and-current instrument the voltage channel can be served by an 8- or 10-bit converter because nobody cares about the last digit there; the current channel is what needs the high-resolution converter spread across eight ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> Residual offset is the other limit: a power meter shows about 1.8 µA of residual offset on its 5 mA range, the consequence of a deliberate choice to prioritise a low [[noise-floor]] over low offset in the input amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>

## Product consumption and battery life

Estimating [[battery-life]] rests on measuring current in each of a product's operating modes — sleep, wake and active — rather than on long-term endurance testing, which is impractical for products rated for hundreds of hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> [[datasheet]] figures should be checked: a device specified at 8 milliamps measured 5 milliamps in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Purpose-built profilers both supply the product and measure it, sourcing up to 500 mA through screw terminals so no separate supply is needed, with a trigger input for correlating measurement with product events.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> A [[battery]] simulator displays voltage and current together, captures pulse current, and integrates current over a settable interval.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup> Cell capacity measurement ideally uses a four-terminal arrangement with the cell voltage taken differentially into one amplifier and the current into another, both logged; a three-terminal meter with the current section inserted in the cell's negative lead is the compromise, driving a constant-current load of, for example, 250 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>

Routine measurements of this kind are quick: a display module drawing 4.25 mA, most of it the [[led]]; a meter drawing 0.3 mA on DC volts; an upgraded instrument display pulling roughly 150 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1669" title="EEVblog 1669 - Agilent 53131A VFD to LED Display Upgrade MAILBAG">1669</a></sup> A fault investigation distinguished 1.25 mA at power-on from about half a milliamp in sleep.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f-LTv1GqCMw" title="Brymen BM786 Power On Fault Investigation">•</a></sup>

## Measuring without breaking the circuit

Where the circuit cannot be opened, current is sensed through its [[magnetic-field]]. A non-contact probe of this type covers milliamps up to the amps range with a 5 MHz [[bandwidth]] and can display current in a PCB track, with a toroid adapter supplied for conventional clamp-style measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> Its limits are worth stating plainly: ±2.5 mT maximum field, equivalent to 2000 A/m, and ±10 mA to ±10 A at ±5% accuracy through the toroid, with a nominal 1 A per volt output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> Accuracy in track mode depends on the user's own [[calibration]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> Such a probe is the practical way to view isolated current [[waveform|waveforms]] on live equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

Field-sensing outputs are voltages, so the scope must be scaled deliberately: the probe is set to ×1 because the output is direct, the manual consulted for the scale factor, and the reading converted — 240 mV at 1 A per volt is 240 mA, 230 mV at 1 V per amp is 230 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup> Most digital scopes can be told to display the channel in amps directly rather than leaving the conversion to the operator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup>

Current transformers serve the same purpose permanently installed. A UPS passes a single mains wire through a small transformer to get an isolated tap on the input current, with a second transformer on the output, so that input and output power can be computed alongside the tapped voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup> A three-phase smart meter passes each phase through its own sense element and measures each phase voltage relative to neutral.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> An EVSE uses a [[current-transformer]] or Hall-effect sensor for its current channel, giving it enough information for real and apparent power even though its display shows only voltage and current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup> Household energy monitors use clamps to read solar production, house consumption and grid export separately — and these clamps carry a direction arrow that must be respected on installation, though a negative consumption reading is not by itself proof that a clamp is reversed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> Clamp instruments have their own error modes: one exhibited an offset that tracked correctly with 3 A applied, identifying the fault as offset rather than sensor or amplifier failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Yk_T-uCbg10" title="Returned BM036 Clamp Meter Investigation">•</a></sup>

## Inferring current from a voltage

Much practical current measurement is a [[voltage-measurement]] across a known resistance. Triac conduction is observed by scoping the voltage across the load resistor, which is proportional to the current through the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup> Mains-referenced coil current is obtained by reading 218 V RMS across a 43 kΩ resistor and dividing, giving about 5 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-425" title="EEVBlog #425 - Mailbag">425</a></sup> The leakage of MOV-protected meter inputs is characterised by putting a current meter in series with a 10 MΩ [[input-impedance]] at 100 V, where 10 µA confirms the expected 10 MΩ, and watching the current rise faster than linearly as the applied voltage climbs toward 1000 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup> [[heat-sink|Heatsink]] work uses the same arithmetic in reverse: 10 V across a [[mosfet]] at 1 A is 10 W dissipated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

The same principle underpins LCR measurement. A resistor is placed in series with the device under test, the voltage and current and their relative phase are measured, and from those three quantities [[inductance]], [[capacitance]], reactance, series resistance, dissipation factor and quality factor all follow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup>

Current also shapes what a voltage reading means. A [[diode]] does not sit at a constant 0.6 V drop; the diode curve gives low drops at low currents, so 0.2 V across a protection diode indicates a small processor current rather than a fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

## Transients

Averaged current is a poor guide to peak current. Motor drives produce spikes significantly higher than expected, so a circuit measured at about 1 A and protected with a 3 A fuse will trip unexpectedly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HBLKxC4JzTA" title="Space Electronics Circuit Protection">•</a></sup> Characterising current transients requires appropriate test gear rather than a handheld ammeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HBLKxC4JzTA" title="Space Electronics Circuit Protection">•</a></sup> Charging circuits show the same behaviour: a 15-minute NiMH charger delivers roughly 7.6 A pulses rather than a steady current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-35" title="EEVblog #35 - Inside the Varta 15 minute NiMH Battery Charger">35</a></sup> A cyclically loaded circuit reads 64, 88, 91 and 65 mA in succession as its phases interleave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup>

## Current in fault-finding

Measuring supply current is a cheap way to avoid chasing the wrong fault. Where all rails in an instrument sagged together, checking the current first ruled out the overload the [[service-manual]]'s troubleshooting guide would have pointed to, and identified a [[bridge-rectifier]] instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Current draw serves as a power indicator on boards with no status LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1493" title="EEVblog 1493 - MacGyver Project - Part 2">1493</a></sup> A microamp range in series establishes whether a suspect module draws anything at all, and whether what it draws is a residual [[capacitor]] charge or genuine operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup> Watching for a current change while freezing a suspect component with inverted air duster is a standard test — a current that does not move disconfirms the theory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Current mode also finds unexpected uses, such as forcing a pin low through the meter while observing the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>

Free-energy claims are tested the same way: measuring 12 mA through the load LEDs and about 24 mA at 1.5 V into the circuit gives roughly 36 mW input against the claimed output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup>

## Verification and calibration

Current can be derived rather than sourced. Given a trustworthy voltage standard and a trustworthy resistance standard, current follows from Ohm's law with no separate current standard required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup> In practice a constant-current source is used: 350 µA fed to several meters at once, or 50 mA, exposes the spread between them — one comparison found only three least significant digits of spread on current, tighter than expected, and another found about 5 counts across fourteen units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 2of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1051" title="EEVblog #1051 - 121GW Multimeter Mass Turbulence">1051</a></sup> A source good to 30 mA is more than sufficient to calibrate a 4000-count meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup> Verification catches both gross faults and marginal specifications: a bench meter that would not measure current at all turned out to have its input terminals physically detached, while a 1 mA reading on a 100 mA range that looked fine calculated out to 0.26% against a specification of 0.05% plus five counts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup>
