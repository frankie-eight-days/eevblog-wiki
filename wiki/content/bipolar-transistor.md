The bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a three-terminal semiconductor device whose collector [[current]] is set by the current forced into its base, making it the current-controlled counterpart to the [[voltage]]-controlled field effect [[transistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Its terminals are named base, collector and emitter, in contrast to the gate, source and drain of a [[mosfet|MOSFET]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Z_c4wjBpSzE" title="Grokipedia vs Wikipedia">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> Before the power MOSFET became commercially viable, bipolar transistors were the dominant power device, used for everything from driving motors to building power supplies, despite being limited in speed, fairly delicate, burdened with a safe operating area problem, and limited in gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> They remain in wide use where ruggedness, low-voltage operation and linear performance matter more than switching speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

## Operation and gain

A BJT and a MOSFET are physically different devices that operate by different mechanisms at the silicon level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup> In a bipolar transistor the gain, or beta, is set by the physical construction inside the device — the doping of the lightly doped region, how thin it is, and the rest of the internal geometry — and that beta is what multiplies base current into collector current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup>

Because the device is current driven, current must actually flow into the base for the transistor to turn on; there is no equivalent of simply raising a gate voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> In switching service this makes the base current a permanent load on whatever drives it. A discrete BJT switch therefore needs a base [[resistor]], and a design can shed that resistor entirely by substituting a MOSFET, which also removes the base-emitter drop from the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup>

The base current is small but never zero, and in high-[[impedance]] circuits it is a real error term. Hanging a bipolar transistor off an RC timing node loads that node with base current and corrupts the charge time, which is why a proper high-impedance buffer is used instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> The same effect makes resistor ratios critical in circuits such as soft-latching power switches, where the base pull-up must be high — 100 k and above, with a 1 M and 22 µF timing pair — and the balance between values becomes delicate unless the design moves to MOSFETs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup>

## Darlington and compound configurations

A single bipolar transistor does not necessarily have high current gain, and the classic remedy is the Darlington pair, which cascades two devices to multiply their betas.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup> A 1964-vintage laboratory supply used a Darlington pass element precisely because the available bipolar transistor had low gain on its own, and the extra stage was needed to get usable performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup> The Sziklai pair is the alternative compound transistor for the same job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Darlington pairs also appear in trivial applications: a small continuity tester built around two bipolar transistors in Darlington configuration, three 1.5 V cells and an incandescent bulb has enough gain to conduct through the [[resistance]] of human skin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Uk5F3rQNUkU" title="ESD Explained &amp; Demonstrated + History of the Pink Polyethylene ESD Bag">•</a></sup>

The [[capacitance]] multiplier exploits the gain directly. An emitter follower fed from an [[rc-filter|RC filter]] passes the input voltage through to the output while drawing only a fraction of the load current through the filter resistor, so the effective capacitance seen by the load is the physical capacitance multiplied by the transistor's beta.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Kilohm to tens-of-kilohm resistors with modest [[capacitor]] values are sufficient, and the pass element can be a single BJT, a Darlington, a Sziklai pair or a MOSFET — the principle is unchanged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

## Junction breakdown ratings

The base-emitter and base-collector junctions have reverse breakdown voltages that appear in the absolute maximum ratings of a [[datasheet|data sheet]] rather than in the electrical characteristics table: VEBO for emitter-base and VCBO for base-collector, the emitter-base figure sometimes written as VBR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup> Driven in reverse, the base-emitter junction breaks down and behaves as a zener at roughly 5 to 10 V; because of the construction of the device the base-collector junction breaks down far higher, typically in the 20 to 100 V region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup>

VEBO is a poorly served specification. Typical parts sit around 6 to 12 V, anything past 20 V is rare, and Digi-Key offers no [[parametric-search|parametric search]] on it at all — Mouser and OctoPart do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup> Filtering for VEBO above 20 V returns on the order of 16 matches from roughly 50,000 transistors, many of them the same part, and some of the survivors are impractical packages such as TO-3.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup> The 2SC3326 is one of the few usable devices, and the Rohm IMX25 dual general-purpose transistor happens to carry a 25 V VEBO without advertising it as a feature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup>

## The transistor as a bidirectional clamp

Two NPN transistors with their collectors joined and their bases joined, emitters facing outward — one device effectively flipped — form a bidirectional clamping zener usable against impulses, overloads and ESD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1000" title="EEVblog #1000 - Fundamental Mailbag Retro Teardown Shootouts are Bullshit">1000</a></sup> The circuit works by deliberately abusing the junction breakdowns just described: in each direction one base-emitter junction sits forward biased as an ordinary [[diode]] drop while the other reverse-breaks down as a zener.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup> It is a fast clamp, it appears in countless multimeters, and it is difficult to find documented in textbooks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup> Fluke used the arrangement extensively and it was widely copied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup> Its practical constraint is the scarcity of high-VEBO parts: an instrument with a 15 V [[diode-test|diode test]] mode needs a reverse emitter-base breakdown around 20 V, which is exactly where the available selection collapses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup>

## Comparison with the MOSFET

MOSFETs are voltage driven, need no steady base current, and have lower on-resistance — RDS(on), against VCE(sat) in a bipolar — which translates into lower [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] and more current and power handling in a given package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> They are also faster: the bipolar transistor exhibits a storage time, a delay of the order of microseconds between the drive being removed and the device actually turning off, and this is what limits the [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] of BJTs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> Storage time is not always specified in the data sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> For megahertz-region converters MOSFETs are the only practical choice, and an LLC resonant converter cannot reasonably be built with bipolars because their drive requirements are too heavy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup>

Bipolars retain concrete advantages. They are more robust and hard to damage electrostatically, having no gate oxide to puncture; they tolerate overcurrent better; and they operate at ultra-low supply voltages where a MOSFET's gate threshold and biasing become awkward.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> A 2N3055 in a [[power-supply|power supply]] is close to unkillable, whereas an equivalent MOSFET such as a 150 V CEP60N15 is a plausible failure suspect in the same position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> BJTs also remain preferred in linear applications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Package habits differ too: a BJT is generally not available in SO8, so an SO8 device with several pins tied together is almost certainly a MOSFET.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>

The insulated gate bipolar transistor merges the two. Structurally it is a FET variant in which the low-resistance N+ substrate is replaced with a P substrate, after which the device behaves partly like a bipolar and partly like a MOSFET and achieves a lower effective on-resistance at high voltage — combining MOSFET drive with bipolar ruggedness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>

## Jellybean parts

A jellybean bipolar is one that has been available essentially forever — some date from the late 1950s — is stocked everywhere, and is cheap enough that the choice barely warrants thought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup> The 2N2222, 2N3904, BC547, BC549 and ST25C fall into this category for small-signal switching, with the 2N2222 the reflexive choice for an open-collector NPN where almost any NPN would serve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup> At the power end the 2N3055 and its complementary 2955 are the standing recommendation, though such parts move in lower volumes than medium- and low-power transistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup> Higher-voltage jellybeans exist for specialised supply rails: a 2N5401-class 120 to 150 V device gives usable gain and low [[noise]] in a condenser microphone front end running from a 120 to 150 V rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-609" title="EEVblog #609 - Condenser Microphone Design Tutorial">609</a></sup>

Selecting a non-jellybean part is a parametric search exercise. Searching Digi-Key's discrete BJT category for NPN devices still leaves around 9,000 candidates before a collector-emitter breakdown filter of 200 V and above narrows it for a high-voltage application such as [[nixie-tube|Nixie tube]] driving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> The catalogue is large — roughly 22,500 BJTs at one distributor — but skewed by package and by which parameters are searchable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup>

## Applications

In a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] the error amplifier compares the output divider against a reference and drives a pass element that may be a BJT or a MOSFET; the topology is indifferent to which.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> The same op-amp-plus-transistor pattern builds a [[constant-current-source|constant current source]], with an NPN as the pass device on the low side or a bipolar on the high side, the [[op-amp|op amp]] forcing its two inputs equal across a sense resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

Bipolar transistors also constitute the internals of many analogue ICs. Bipolar comparators draw supply currents of a few hundred microamps and are characterised by input bias and offset currents that follow directly from the input technology, unlike their CMOS equivalents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> Low-noise audio op amps are often entirely bipolar, reaching figures such as 1.5 nV/√Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup> Conversely, a CMOS op amp built on a silicon-gate process substitutes MOSFETs for the traditional bipolar input devices in exchange for much lower power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> At high [[frequency]], bipolar devices fabricated in silicon germanium are combined with CMOS on the same wafer in a SiGe BiCMOS process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o2NxHu5Bsnk" title="Discussion with Shahriar from The Signal Path">•</a></sup>

Where matching matters, discrete bipolars are inadequate and monolithic transistor arrays are used instead, because devices on the same die track one another; differential front ends typically use five or six transistors with their emitters tied together, sometimes with JFETs ahead of the bipolar stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Scope Bandwidth Hack Investigation">879</a></sup> The cascode — a common-emitter stage stacked with a common-base stage — is used for output stability in precision instrumentation, and can be built as a hybrid with a bipolar and a MOSFET.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup> As a follower the bipolar is more solid than a JFET, which manages only about 0.7 to 0.8 of unity gain in a phase splitter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-616" title="EEVblog #616 - How Microphone Phantom Powering Works">616</a></sup>

The bipolar pair also appears as an equivalent circuit rather than as real silicon: a thyristor is modelled as a PNP and an NPN cross-connected, so that gate current entering the NPN base turns it on, which in turn conducts through the base of the PNP and latches the structure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup>

## Identification and failure

A functional NPN shows a diode drop from base to emitter, and the absence of that drop is direct evidence that a suspect part is either not a bipolar or is destroyed; comparing against a known-good unit settles which.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> Package alone is not diagnostic: the same SOT-23 footprint may hold a 2N2222 or an N-channel MOSFET such as an IRLML2803, and only tracing and analysing the surrounding circuit reveals which one the design intends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup> Part numbering is equally treacherous, with the same numeric suffix appearing on both a high-voltage NPN and a MOSFET from different manufacturers, so the [[schematic]] symbol in the data sheet is the reliable discriminator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup> A cheap component tester will identify a surface-mount bipolar directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1020" title="EEVblog #1020 - Is A $7 LCR / Component Tester Any Good?">1020</a></sup>

Power bipolars fail in the ways power devices generally do. Repeated thermal cycling works the [[solder]] joints of a heat-sinked device loose over time, producing intermittent faults in amplifiers and power stages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup> Undersized heatsinking on cheap output transistors such as an A1695 guarantees the same outcome sooner.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-671" title="EEVblog #671 - White Van Speaker Scam Teardown">671</a></sup> Bipolar power transistors are still found bolted to heatsinks in precision equipment such as AC voltage standards, and in unusually wide packages like the 2SC3858.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-709" title="EEVblog #709 - EDC 4601 AC Voltage Standard Teardown">709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hVIo7vmIExw" title="Mystery Dumpster Teardown - 2020-06-12">•</a></sup> A hot small-signal bipolar such as a BD136 is not necessarily itself faulty — it may simply be sinking current dumped into it by a failed pass transistor elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup>
