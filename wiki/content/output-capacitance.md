Output capacitance is the [[capacitance]] placed across the output terminals of a regulator, converter or reference. It does three jobs at once — it filters [[ripple]], it supplies charge during load transients faster than the control loop can respond, and it forms part of the loop's [[frequency]] compensation, so its value and its ESR determine whether the circuit is stable at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> Because those jobs pull in opposite directions, and because stored energy in the [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] escapes the control of any [[current]]-limiting circuit upstream of it, choosing the value is a trade-off rather than a case of more being better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup>

## Stability: a minimum, and sometimes a maximum

Regulators with an internal error amplifier — linear regulators, low-dropout regulators and [[voltage]] references alike — can oscillate if they are not loaded with the correct amount of capacitance, the correct type of [[capacitor]], and the correct ESR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> LDOs are the classic case, since the PNP or P-channel pass element they use to achieve the low [[dropout-voltage|dropout voltage]] is inherently more unstable, and a minimum output capacitance is required to hold the loop together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> Data sheets state that minimum explicitly; one part specifies 2.2 µF as the minimum for stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

The requirement is not always one-sided. Some parts specify a *maximum* output capacitance rather than a minimum, going unstable above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-827" title="EEVblog #827 - Mailbag">827</a></sup> The TL431 shunt reference is stable only over part of the capacitive-load range: it has a stable region and an unstable region, so a capacitor cannot simply be attached to the output on the assumption that it will help.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Stability is a property of the whole loop, not of the capacitor alone — adding an op-amp buffer inside the feedback path changes the poles enough that a circuit which needed a 470 µF output capacitor to behave becomes stable with that capacitance removed entirely, at the cost of somewhat more [[noise]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

In practice the data-sheet minimum is a guideline rather than a hard threshold. A design specifying 2.2 µF minimum of ceramic has been run stably on 1 µF of a non-ceramic type; removing the output capacitance altogether left the rail working but noticeably noisier, with a 0.47 µF part enough to clean it up again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

## Ripple, transient response and the sizing calculation

Greater output capacitance improves load transient response: the droop is smaller, though recovery takes longer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> The effect is directly measurable — a design with 1 µF of output capacitance showed a 50 mV step for a quarter-amp load change, and adding 47 µF shrank the transient substantially.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

For a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]] the output capacitor is sized from the ripple specification. With an [[output-current|output current]] of 100 mA, an on-time of 7.3 µs and a target of 100 mV peak-to-peak ripple, the standard expression gives 66 µF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> The controller [[datasheet|data sheet]] for an [[led|LED]] driver, working the same calculation in reverse, needs roughly 470 µF on the output to hold ripple current to 30% — itself a large figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup>

Charge-pump converters make the dependency awkward. The ripple curves published for the 7660 family assume a nominal output capacitance of 10 µF and a switching capacitor of 10 µF, so reading a figure such as 50 mV peak-to-peak at 10 mA off the graph and expecting it in a real circuit is a trap: the result depends on the capacitance actually present, on the ESR of the capacitor chosen, and on the derating of that capacitance under DC bias.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

Not every converter needs much. A DC-DC converter tested with no output capacitance at all held 4.93 V from a 4.9 V input right up to its maximum load, needing only a [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> At the other extreme, a physically tiny [[boost-converter|boost converter]] has extremely limited output capacitance and peak current capability by virtue of its size, leaving the load's own internal decoupling to handle pulse currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

## Lab power supplies: keep it as low as possible

The strongest practical constraint appears in bench supplies with a constant-current mode. The output capacitor sits after the current-sensing and regulation circuitry, so the charge stored in it is not current-limited.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> When the supply transitions from constant voltage into constant current, that energy dumps into the load before the loop has time to respond, delivering a current spike above the set limit — precisely at the moment the [[current-limit|current limit]] was supposed to be protecting the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> A 2200 µF capacitor added for transient performance therefore buys better regulation and a worse current limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> The design goal for any supply with constant-current capability is to minimise output capacitance while still keeping ripple low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

Teardowns show the principle applied with varying discipline. One bench supply carries a single small ceramic across the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup> Another uses 22 µF, with some surface-mount ceramics on the reverse side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Against these, a supply found with 1000 µF on the output is very high for the class, though whether that value was forced by a stability requirement cannot be determined without analysing the loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> Excessive output capacitance is visible from the front panel: the output terminals continue to read voltage for a long time after switch-off as the capacitors discharge, particularly with the load disconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> The converse also shows — a supply whose voltage setting steps essentially instantaneously has little output capacitance doing anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Slow, curved edges when the voltage setting is swept down are the expected signature of the output capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup>

## Secondary effects

Output capacitance shapes several behaviours that are not obviously about the capacitor. In a current-limit circuit that works by pulling the regulator's adjust pin low, the [[output-voltage|output voltage]] cannot drop instantly because of the output capacitance, so the loop servos around an average value rather than snapping to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Measurements of a supply under repair start out low and rise as output capacitance somewhere in the circuit charges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup> In an energy-harvesting regulator the value must be split between rails: a small minimum-value capacitor on the rail that must come up quickly, and a much larger one on the rail expected to supply pulse currents, since anything oversized has to be charged before the output is usable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-945" title="EEVblog #945 - Thermal Powered Smartwatches Are GIMMICKS!">945</a></sup>

Output capacitance also appears as a parameter well outside power conversion. Logic-gate [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]] depends on it, and data sheets that quote transition times without specifying the load capacitance leave the figure incomplete.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> [[mosfet|MOSFET]] data sheets list output capacitance alongside input and reverse transfer capacitance among the dynamic characteristics — figures that matter for switching converters and are largely irrelevant when the part is used for logic or simple power switching.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> The presence of an [[inductor]], a [[diode]], a controlling element and output capacitance together is enough to identify an unknown circuit block as a switching converter during [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup>

Sizing claims on commodity supplies are worth checking against the rating on the box: a unit claiming 384 W of continuous output power with a token amount of output capacitance is unlikely to deliver anything close to its stated figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-893" title="EEVblog #893 - Mailbag">893</a></sup>
