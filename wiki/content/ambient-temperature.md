Ambient temperature is the temperature of the air surrounding a component, board or instrument, measured away from the heat the device itself produces. It matters because almost every thermal number in electronics is a *rise* — a differential added on top of ambient rather than an absolute figure — so the same circuit that runs comfortably on a 20 °C bench can be destroyed in a 45 °C rack without a single component changing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup> Ambient is also the one term in a thermal calculation that the designer does not control: it is set by the environment the product is deployed into, and must be assumed at its worst credible value rather than at bench conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

## The reference point in thermal design

In the standard electrical analogy used for heat-flow calculations, [[power-dissipation|power dissipation]] is treated as [[current]], [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] as [[resistance]], and temperature as [[voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> Ambient temperature enters that circuit as a fixed [[voltage-source|voltage source]] at the bottom of the chain — the thermal equivalent of a [[battery]] sitting above thermal [[ground]] — with, say, 22 °C or 20 °C representing room temperature in the lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> Every thermal resistance in the path then adds its own rise on top: junction to case, the [[via]], the insulating pad, the heat transfer block, the case, and finally the [[heat-sink|heat sink]] to air.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> The quantity actually wanted at the end, the [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]], is therefore ambient plus the sum of all the rises, and no amount of heat sinking can bring a junction below the air around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup>

Heat sink thermal resistance is normally specified into free air, though a figure for a specified [[airflow]] over the sink may also be given.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Anodising a heat sink black brings its emissivity closer to that of an ideal black body radiator, improving the radiative part of the transfer, since all objects share the same properties of absorbing and emitting radiation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

A worked example: a power [[resistor]] in a constant-current dummy load dropping 11 V at 1 A dissipates 11 W, and the heat sink used was calculated to rise about 50 °C above ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> With the lab at about 20 °C the predicted heat sink temperature was about 70 °C, and a reference temperature probe on the heat sink read just a couple of degrees over 70.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup>

## Component ratings are ambient ratings

Resistor power ratings are quoted at a specified ambient, not at a specified body temperature. A typical quarter-watt part is rated at its full wattage up to 70 °C ambient, which is why the derating curve in the [[datasheet]] runs flat and then breaks downward at 70 °C on the horizontal axis; beyond that point the allowable dissipation falls linearly to zero, in one datasheet at 155 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> The distinction is easy to miss: 70 °C is the surrounding air, not the resistor itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> Running a quarter-watt resistor at its full quarter watt is legitimate and continuous, but the part will sit roughly 35 °C above whatever ambient it is in — 55 °C in a 20 °C lab, 65 °C for the same product used outdoors on a 30 °C summer day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> Provided the rise plus the ambient stays inside the component's limits, operation is within specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup>

The same structure appears in IC datasheets, where a family of parametric curves is drawn with ambient temperature as the parameter distinguishing one curve from the next — a nominal curve at room temperature and further curves for other ambients, on which the same operating current can produce markedly different behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Battery-life estimation tools follow suit, taking ambient temperature alongside supply voltage, clock [[frequency]] and enabled peripherals as an input to the current calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup>

[[temperature-coefficient|Temperature coefficient]] makes ambient stability, rather than absolute ambient, the relevant quantity for precision work. A decade resistance substitution box built with 0.1 % resistors and a tempco of 0.025 % per degree Celsius is barely affected by lab drift, because an air-conditioned lab holding roughly 20 °C ± 5 °C, and in practice varying only a couple of degrees, moves the resistance by far less than the initial tolerance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup>

## Enclosures, racks and design margin

The gap between bench ambient and deployed ambient is where thermal designs fail. Equipment mounted in a rack can see its ambient rise by 20 or 30 °C above room temperature, and a design with no thermal margin will simply shut down when its regulators hit their thermal cutout — a failure that variation in junction-to-case resistance only makes less predictable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> The correct design target is an operating capability above the maximum ambient the product will ever see, which for rack-mounted gear might be 45 or 50 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup> Instruments built for that environment state it explicitly: a rack-mount [[oscilloscope]] specified to operate in 45 °C ambient carries enough headroom to ramp its fans to full speed and hold its internal temperature at the calibrated operating point, [[noise]] being unimportant inside a server rack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1624" title="EEVblog 1624 - Electronex: Liquid Instruments Moku Oscilloscope">1624</a></sup> Automotive environments impose a comparable burden, with ambients up to around 70 °C before any self-heating is counted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup>

Datasheet limits for embedded controllers are quoted the same way. A lighting controller rated to reach roughly 90 °C case temperature at 40 °C ambient is comfortable in a roof space that peaks at 25 or 26 °C even in an Australian summer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1091" title="EEVblog #1091 - LED Studio Light Repair">1091</a></sup>

## Measured differentials

Because rises are quoted relative to ambient, characterising a product means measuring both numbers. A [[digital-oscilloscope|digital oscilloscope]] left running overnight settled at 39.4 °C with the lab ambient about 24 °C, a differential of 15 to 16 °C, at a roughly constant 35 to 36 W drawn regardless of how many channels or which math functions were in use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pwS6HnM9PDo" title="The Rigol DHO800 Fan(boys) Go Wild">•</a></sup> An 80 W [[led|LED]] luminaire measured 45 °C in its core against about 24 °C ambient, matching its advertised 20 °C rise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup> A high-power LED mounted on a decent heat sink sits only 5 to 10 °C above ambient at the sink, remaining cool to the touch even while the LED die itself is hot enough to burn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup>

Small differentials still carry design consequences. Thermal imaging of electrolytic capacitors in parallel showed the loaded single 100 µF part reaching about 27 °C while its neighbours sat around ambient, a degree or two above it — only about 5 °C of spread at the case, but the core temperature inside the can is higher still, being separated from the surface by the [[capacitor]]'s own thermal path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Since electrolytic degradation is self-reinforcing, a high ambient compounds the effect directly; free air in a lab is a benign case, and even the small airflow from an air conditioner measurably lowers case temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

Inlet air temperature for forced-air cooling is set by ambient only when the intake is placed to draw from outside the hot zone. A CPU cooler taking air through a top grille pulls in room air at 23 to 24 °C, whereas an intake beside the graphics card ingests already-warmed air; the difference between the two arrangements was easily 10 °C of inlet temperature, which propagates directly into the component temperatures downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kvI7vlvzrQo" title="Dark Rock 2: CPU Fan Boogaloo">•</a></sup>

## Ambient tracking and compensation

Some instruments treat ambient as a signal to be tracked rather than a constant. The Lepton microbolometer in a compact [[thermal-camera|thermal camera]] performs a shutter-based flat-field correction whose frequency is driven by ambient change, not by a timer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> Held at a steady lab ambient of about 23 °C — air conditioning cycling within roughly half a degree — the sensor can go several minutes without operating its shutter at all, since nothing inside the array is drifting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> Placed in a thermal chamber and taken through a ramp, correction intervals shorten dramatically and then lengthen again as the sensor's own body approaches equilibrium with the chamber and the internal rate of change falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup> The sensor has an internal [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]] and decides for itself when compensation is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup>

Ambient measurement is common enough that many multimeters carry an internal temperature readout and display ambient without any probe attached, a convenience absent on models that only measure through an external [[thermocouple]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup> Ambient is likewise what an unloaded thermal energy-harvesting demonstrator reports: an ARM [[microcontroller]] powered from a Peltier module held between a hand and a heat sink displayed 23.7 °C, the temperature of the board itself rather than of the heat sink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup>

## Environmental and energy systems

Outside the enclosure, ambient becomes one variable among several. [[solar-panel|Photovoltaic panel]] output depends on ambient temperature, [[solar-irradiance|solar irradiance]] and airflow together, and a cold day can coincide with high irradiance; wind, wind chill, the standoff height of the mounting and the roof construction all modify how far the panels heat above ambient, so a hot day does not by itself prevent a panel reaching its full rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup> In a winter installation with ambient not much above 20 °C, a battery bank showed effectively no temperature rise while the [[inverter]] peaked near 60 °C at its 5 kW maximum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vNKcRs3zDBI" title="We&#x27;re Gonna Need a Bigger Solar Battery...">•</a></sup>

Heat pump water heaters are similarly ambient-driven: the daily run time and the point at which the unit switches off depend on both hot water consumption and ambient temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Uq553ckJ0uQ" title="MURPHY! - Reclaim Hot Water Heat Pump FAIL">•</a></sup> Analysis of such an installation logged daily maximum and overnight minimum ambient temperatures — taken from the weather bureau rather than measured on site — alongside tank temperatures at top and bottom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1517" title="EEVblog 1517 - Heat Pump Hot Water Install &amp; Analysis">1517</a></sup>

Ambient also sets the baseline for thermoelectric harvesting from the human body. Skin surface temperature is lower than the roughly 37 °C internal figure, measuring around 35.8 °C, so a 25 °C ambient yields a gradient of only about 10 °C across the module — a realistic test condition that produces power output well below the microwatt level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1383" title="EEVblog 1383 - Human Battery Energy Harvesting BUSTED">1383</a></sup>

## Practice

The choice of temperature rise to design for, and therefore the required margin against ambient, is a judgement call. For [[pcb-trace|PCB trace]] current-rating calculations the governing parameters are copper thickness, trace width, the acceptable rise above ambient, and ambient itself; Dave Jones works to a personal rule of thumb of a 10 °C rise, with no formal justification beyond conservatism.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> The danger is in treating a generous rise as free: allowing 50 °C or 100 °C of rise may be tolerable on a bench but not on top of a 70 °C automotive ambient, and copper that runs that hot will discolour over years of service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> A representative calculation for a 100 mil wide external-layer trace in standard 1 oz copper, unplated, with ambient at the tool's default, gives about 5 mΩ of resistance and a nominal 3.28 A rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup>

Component selection can be driven by ambient extremes as well. Air-dielectric capacitors in shielded enclosures, used in a [[capacitance]]-measurement instrument in place of conventional dielectrics, are consistent with an environment whose temperature extremes exceed what an ordinary capacitor can tolerate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup>
