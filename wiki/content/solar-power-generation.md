Solar power generation is the conversion of incident sunlight into electrical energy by [[solar-power-system|photovoltaic]] panels, in practice almost always as a grid-connected installation whose output rises and falls with the sun's position, the season, and whatever happens to be shading the array.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-484" title="EEVblog #484 - Home Solar Power System Installation">484</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5x8UVZnwyL8" title="New Enphase Solar Power System - 1st Day">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dKOmNkVLUAA" title="Home Solar Upgrade Power Consumption">•</a></sup> What matters engineering-wise is rarely the nameplate rating of the array but the energy it actually delivers over a day and a year, and how much of that energy is consumed on site rather than exported.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1502" title="EEVblog 1502 - Is Home Battery Storage Financially Viable?">1502</a></sup> Because generation is cheap to claim and awkward to measure, the yield figure — kilowatt hours per day, per square metre, per year — is the number against which every proposal has to be checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1269" title="EEVblog #1269 - Solar Roadways SR4 DATA Hilarity!">1269</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1544" title="EEVblog 1544 - Platio Solar Pavement BUSTED!">1544</a></sup>

## Yield of a residential array

A nominal 3 kW rooftop array in Sydney reached 2.5 kW on a clear day in the middle of winter, near the annual low point, and returned a total of almost 6.5 kWh over that day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-484" title="EEVblog #484 - Home Solar Power System Installation">484</a></sup> Under overcast sky with no direct sun on the panels the same array still produced 660 watts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-484" title="EEVblog #484 - Home Solar Power System Installation">484</a></sup> Over almost eight years that 3 kW system accumulated close to 30 MWh of running total on its [[inverter]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1385" title="EEVblog 1385 - 8kW Home Solar Power System EXPANSION!">1385</a></sup> A later 8 kW nominal system was expected to peak at roughly 8 kW in midsummer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup> Combined output from two independent arrays on the same house peaked at 5.3 kW on a clear day, and at 3.82 kW on a cloudy, overcast one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eHCzTC9lku8" title="The DOWNSIDE of my Home Solar Storage Battery">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup>

The daily profile is shaped by array orientation. With six panels on the eastern roof and eight on the western, first production began about 7:45 a.m. and rose in a nearly linear slope as the eastern panels caught the morning sun.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5x8UVZnwyL8" title="New Enphase Solar Power System - 1st Day">•</a></sup> At the other end of the day, output drops off at around 5:30 p.m. and falls away sharply rather than tapering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dKOmNkVLUAA" title="Home Solar Upgrade Power Consumption">•</a></sup> A perfect sunny day gives a smooth profile; a broken-cloud day gives a choppy one, and a cloudy day can more than halve the total energy produced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dKOmNkVLUAA" title="Home Solar Upgrade Power Consumption">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 - Lightyear Zero Solar Powered Electric Car">1480</a></sup> Winter production is materially worse than summer, which also means much less excess energy is available for discretionary loads in the colder months.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-oWi6pgg1W1A" title="Pool Heat Pump Sizing">•</a></sup>

## Shading and underperformance

Shading is the dominant site-specific loss mechanism and it is seasonal: in winter one panel of an array falls into shadow, cutting the output of that string.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dKOmNkVLUAA" title="Home Solar Upgrade Power Consumption">•</a></sup> Shadowing need not come from terrain or trees. A mast-mounted [[antenna]] casting a shadow across the array was identified as the cause of a persistent shortfall, with the system delivering around 10.5 kWh a day before the antenna was taken down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> Being sited in a gully compounds the problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup>

Monitoring services flag this class of fault automatically. An underperformance alert lists the plausible causes as incorrect system details, incorrect device wiring, an actual system fault, an inverter fault, overvoltage, and shading or seasonal change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uuLwmQ7oLo8" title="Solar Power System Monitor ALERT!">•</a></sup>

## Monitoring and metering

Production monitoring is separate from, and more informative than, the inverter's own display. A whole-house monitor using [[current]] clamps can log total consumption alongside individual circuits such as the hot water system, the pool, and the electric vehicle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vDtxmikcYuc" title="Who Screwed With The Solar Analytics Code?">•</a></sup> Its principal value in a mixed installation is that it ties together two entirely independent solar systems — a string inverter system and a [[microinverter]] system — which have no knowledge of each other, and reports their total production as one figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eHCzTC9lku8" title="The DOWNSIDE of my Home Solar Storage Battery">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup> The daily chart plots production, consumption, and the overlap between them, with a seven-day breakdown of energy self-consumed, energy exported, and energy imported.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup> One such week showed 11.8 kWh of solar energy consumed on site, 13.1 kWh exported unused, and 46.3 kWh imported from the grid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup>

Adding a home [[battery]] breaks the production measurement of a clamp-based monitor, since the battery is not an AC-coupled unit and its charge and discharge are not distinguished from generation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vDtxmikcYuc" title="Who Screwed With The Solar Analytics Code?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eHCzTC9lku8" title="The DOWNSIDE of my Home Solar Storage Battery">•</a></sup> Auto-scaling axes on such dashboards can also make a poor day look like a good one until the scale is read.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dKOmNkVLUAA" title="Home Solar Upgrade Power Consumption">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup>

Net metering uses two meters: one totals energy bought from the grid, the other totals the excess exported during the day, and a separate meter records what the panels generated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup> Only energy not used in the house appears on the export meter, because household load is served from generation first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup>

## Economics of self-consumption

The export tariff is a fraction of the retail tariff, which inverts the intuition that generating more is what pays. Grid electricity was bought at 24 cents per kilowatt hour while exported energy earned 8 cents, one third as much.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup> A later tariff paid about 7.6 cents per kilowatt hour for exports.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1502" title="EEVblog 1502 - Is Home Battery Storage Financially Viable?">1502</a></sup> The consequence is that a kilowatt hour consumed at the moment it is generated is worth roughly three times one sold: generating 3 kW while using 2 kW in the house means nothing is exported and nothing is earned, but nothing is bought at the retail rate either.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-724" title="EEVblog #724 - Home Solar Power System Analysis &amp; Update">724</a></sup> For a financial assessment of a solar or storage decision, the energy bill is the only figure that ultimately matters, however many monitoring systems are installed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1502" title="EEVblog 1502 - Is Home Battery Storage Financially Viable?">1502</a></sup>

This is what makes load shifting worthwhile. An electric vehicle charger can be set to draw only surplus solar: at night it detects that no excess is available and does not charge, and once production begins in the morning with other household loads off, it starts charging and modulates the rate up to the full 32 A, 7 kW.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup> With an 8 kW system peaking near 8 kW in summer and under a kilowatt of house load, essentially the full 7 kW is available to the car.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup> Conversely, a pool heat pump is deliberately not sized for winter operation, because winter production leaves little excess to run it; the shoulder months are the design target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-oWi6pgg1W1A" title="Pool Heat Pump Sizing">•</a></sup>

## Hardware

SMA Sunny Boy string inverters are installed near-exclusively by some installers on reliability grounds, and one remained in service after close to eight years.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1385" title="EEVblog 1385 - 8kW Home Solar Power System EXPANSION!">1385</a></sup> There is no reason to scrap a working inverter during an expansion — the original unit can be retained to serve the original 3 kW array while new microinverters handle the added panels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1385" title="EEVblog 1385 - 8kW Home Solar Power System EXPANSION!">1385</a></sup> Enphase IQ 7 Plus microinverters carry a ten-year warranty; a unit that stopped reporting to the gateway had delivered 1.63 MWh over about four years and was still inside its warranty term.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-isnXYy9vCag" title="Enphase Microinverter FAIL?">•</a></sup>

Cell chemistry follows the application. Amorphous silicon cells are more efficient at very low light and very low power levels, which is why calculators use them, but they are a poor choice for roof-scale energy production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-958" title="EEVblog #958 - Mailbag">958</a></sup>

## Solar surfaces: roadways, pavements and railways

Proposals to generate power from horizontal walking and driving surfaces recur, and the arithmetic defeats them consistently. A practical ideal maximum for a good location is about 380 Wh per square metre per day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup> For one kilometre of an eight-metre-wide road — 8,000 square metres — that is 3,000 kWh generated in a day, at best.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup> [[led|LED]] lane marking for that same kilometre, taken as six strips at 10 cm spacing, works out to about 30 kW drawn continuously, or 720 kWh per day: roughly a quarter of the ideal maximum generation consumed by the line marking alone, before any other load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup> The line markers cannot be dimmed or duty-cycled, so that draw is constant regardless of what is being generated, and on a bad day consumption can reach half or more of production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup> Adding snow-melting heaters to the same energy budget requires at least an order of magnitude more generation than is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-632" title="EEVblog #632 - Solar Roadways Are RUBBISH!">632</a></sup>

Measured installations bear this out. The [[solar-roadways|Solar Roadways SR4]] array was a 1.5 kW system of 30 panels rated 50 W each, and its public data feed reported a lifetime production of four kilowatt hours — less than a 3 kW domestic rooftop produces in a single winter day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1269" title="EEVblog #1269 - Solar Roadways SR4 DATA Hilarity!">1269</a></sup> A representative day showed about 0.46 kWh for the entire installation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1269" title="EEVblog #1269 - Solar Roadways SR4 DATA Hilarity!">1269</a></sup> A predecessor installation managed 0.21 kWh a day before it stopped, which is not enough to light its own LEDs, let alone the heaters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TL94sJ3Ykgs" title="Merry Solar Roadways Christmas!">•</a></sup> That installation generated no power at all after 3 September 2018.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TL94sJ3Ykgs" title="Merry Solar Roadways Christmas!">•</a></sup> An electric vehicle charging point installed alongside it was drawing from the grid, not from the panels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1269" title="EEVblog #1269 - Solar Roadways SR4 DATA Hilarity!">1269</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup> A separate deployment of 36 panels was funded near the Baltimore Visitors Center as a test of the concept.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup> Marketing for the successor generation claims panels engineered to support 250,000 lb vehicles, on the argument that normal solar panels cannot be walked on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1738" title="EEVblog 1738 - Solar Freakin&#x27; Roadways NEW FUNDING!">1738</a></sup>

Claimed figures for road installations are correspondingly optimistic: one was to generate 1 GWh per year, enough for 800 homes,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup> and another to produce 30 kW and 30,000 kWh per year.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup> The controlling objection is not the panel technology but the geometry and the environment — the same land alongside the road could carry conventionally angled panels at lower installation cost, with the long service life expected of a commercial solar installation, and without vehicles driving over them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup> No improvement in panel [[efficiency]] changes that comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup>

A pavement variant used 2,544 monocrystalline pavers of 21.8% efficiency, rated to withstand two tonnes of pressure without microcracking, and was claimed to generate 55,000 kWh — 55 MWh — per year, elsewhere framed as enough energy to power 47 houses for one day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1544" title="EEVblog 1544 - Platio Solar Pavement BUSTED!">1544</a></sup> A railway variant put 48 panels between the rails for 16,000 kWh per year, described as the average consumption of four to six households, against a project cost in the hundreds of thousands; extrapolated across 5,300 km of railway, the claim was 300,000 homes or 2% of Switzerland's electricity, in a country that needs to produce seven times more solar energy than it currently does to meet its 2035 goals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1683" title="EEVblog 1683 - Sun-Ways Solar Freakin&#x27; RAILways - Part 2">1683</a></sup> Roofs remain the better place for the panels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1683" title="EEVblog 1683 - Sun-Ways Solar Freakin&#x27; RAILways - Part 2">1683</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1233" title="EEVblog #1233 - The Demise Of Solar Roadways">1233</a></sup>

## Generation at night

Schemes to extract energy from photovoltaic cells after dark, on the argument that this would lift the daytime limitation of PV generation and reduce or eliminate the need for battery storage, recover on the order of 0.02% to 0.04% of daytime output — enough to run a small torch bulb, and not a substitute for storage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1467" title="EEVblog 1467 - Stanford Solar Power at Nightime!  BUSTED">1467</a></sup>
