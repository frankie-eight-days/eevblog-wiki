The cutoff voltage of a [[battery]]-powered product is the terminal [[voltage]] at which its circuitry shuts down, switches off automatically, or raises a low-battery warning.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> It is the single design parameter that most directly determines how much of a battery's stored energy a product can actually use, and it heads the list of factors that affect delivered [[battery-capacity|battery capacity]], ahead of temperature and discharge rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Because a cell's discharge curve slopes continuously rather than holding flat to exhaustion, every tenth of a volt of unnecessary cutoff strands capacity that is physically present in the cell but electrically unreachable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup>

## Effect on usable capacity

On an alkaline AA the penalty is severe. A product that cuts out or warns at 1.1 V per cell discards the entire area under the discharge curve below that point, and one set at 1.2 V wastes roughly half the battery's capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> A well-designed product therefore sets its cutoff as low as it practically can; for an [[alkaline-battery|alkaline cell]] that floor is about 0.8 V per cell, and a superbly designed product operates all the way down to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> The same reasoning scales to multi-cell packs: a two-cell 3 V design should work down to 1.6 V, a nominal 6 V system down to 3.2 V, and a standard 9 V battery down to 4.8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Instruments that cut out at 6.5 or 7 V on a 9 V battery — and some are higher still — throw away a large fraction of the pack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup>

Lithium-ion behaves differently because its discharge curve collapses at the bottom. Taking a cell down to a 2.8 V stop rather than a more conservative 3 V yields only about 15 mAh of extra capacity, because terminal voltage drops like a brick wall at 2.8 V and practically no energy remains below it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup> Setting the cutoff too high on lithium-ion is nonetheless costly in the other direction: on one aged 680 mAh-class cell, a 3.3 V cutoff left only 220 mAh available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup> The general rule holds for any chemistry — the lower the voltage a product can be designed to run down to, the more capacity is extracted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup>

## Calculating wasted capacity

Wasted capacity can be read off directly by logging a constant-[[current]] discharge and plotting voltage against elapsed time alongside a cumulative remaining-energy curve on the same time axis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> A horizontal line drawn at the chosen cutoff voltage is dropped vertically where it meets the voltage curve, then extended horizontally to the remaining-energy axis, giving the energy left in the cell at the moment the product declares itself flat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> For a Duracell copper-top AA discharged at 100 mA with a 1 V per cell cutoff, the remaining energy at shutdown is under 5% — negligible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> The logging itself needs only voltage samples at a known constant discharge current; the current need not be measured if the load holds it constant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

This method bounds how much energy any voltage-boosting sleeve can recover from an exhausted cell. Across discharge currents from 5 mA to 1 A, a product with a 1 V or 1.1 V cutoff leaves only about 10–20% of the battery's energy on the table as an absolute best case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Recovering the frequently claimed 80% requires the host product to be extremely poorly designed, with a cutoff under load of around 1.4 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Any product designed for rechargeable cells already has a cutoff of at most 1.1 V, so the recoverable margin there is negligible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> The underlying premise is real — some modern electronics do have cutoff voltages too high to reach the last part of the discharge curve — but the magnitude is small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ixbLmBWUqcQ" title="Popular Mechanics Promotes Batteriser 2.0!">•</a></sup>

## Measuring a product's cutoff voltage

Only the voltage present on the product's battery terminals matters, because the product's own internal [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] is what decides when to shut down, and that reference sees nothing but the terminal voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> It follows that a bench [[power-supply|power supply]] is a valid instrument for determining a product's cutoff voltage: it forces the chosen voltage directly onto the terminals with effectively zero source [[impedance]], so the value at which the product quits is its true cutoff voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> The battery's internal [[resistance]] is a red herring in this measurement, which is also why manufacturers publish loaded discharge curves in the first place — terminal voltage is the only thing obtainable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Open-circuit [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] is not a substitute, since a power supply has no electrochemistry to model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>

The contrary claim, that using a power supply to show a battery-operated device's cutoff voltage "ignoring the battery's internal resistance is wrong and misleading at best", rests on failing to probe at the terminals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>

## Bench practice

Electronic loads with a battery-test application accept a stop voltage directly: the load draws a programmed constant current until the terminal voltage reaches that threshold, then cuts out automatically and reports accumulated amp-hours and watt-hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src--ir61ARd5T4" title="WTF is Wrong with my Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load?">•</a></sup> Typical settings include 1 V for a nominal single-cell NiMH or alkaline test, 1.8 V for two alkaline cells in series (0.9 V per cell), and 2.8 V for an aggressive lithium-ion run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9FCzAgJhRdc" title="NiMH 8000mAh Capacity Claim TESTED">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XDjyY48u0PU" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup> A separate voltage-on threshold, commonly set around 0.5 V, gates when the load engages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RFb3TwWzza0" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Test Setup">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9FCzAgJhRdc" title="NiMH 8000mAh Capacity Claim TESTED">•</a></sup> Where a test must run for a fixed duration regardless of voltage — such as a 24-hour leakage run — the voltage stop is simply disabled in favour of a time stop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RFb3TwWzza0" title="Alkaline Battery Leakage Test Setup">•</a></sup>

Remote or Kelvin sensing at the cell terminals is necessary for the stop voltage to mean anything, since drop in the leads and contacts otherwise triggers the cutoff early.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1732" title="EEVblog 1732 - 20yo 1000+ Cycle Panasonic Li-Ion Battery TESTED">1732</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9FCzAgJhRdc" title="NiMH 8000mAh Capacity Claim TESTED">•</a></sup> A discharge logged with a [[multimeter]] instead of an [[electronic-load|electronic load]] has no programmable stop at all, so the test duration and sample count must be calculated in advance from the expected capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

Cutoff voltage is also what makes an accumulated milliwatt-hour measurement meaningful: a battery's watt-hour capacity is only defined against a stated cutoff, which is what allows a logged [[energy-consumption|energy consumption]] figure to be converted into expected runtime.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> In systems with more than one cutoff in series, the lowest one governs — a portable power station's 9 V load-side cutout tripped before the station's own low-battery shutdown, with the pack still indicating 2%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lYKjScnkeq0" title="Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery: Capacity Test">•</a></sup> Low-power devices can likewise be characterised by discharging their storage element until the circuit stops running, in one case at about 3 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-S-UqkM8iDKM" title="#1179 - PowerFilm Flexible Indoor Solar Cell">•</a></sup>
