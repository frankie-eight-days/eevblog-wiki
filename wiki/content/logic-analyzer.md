A logic analyzer is a multi-channel digital acquisition instrument that samples many signals at once and displays each as a stream of ones and zeros. Its defining advantage over an [[oscilloscope]] is channel count: a scope gives two or four analog channels, while a logic analyzer gives eight, sixteen, thirty-two or more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Its defining limitation is that it discards everything about the [[waveform]] except which side of a threshold it fell on, so what appears on screen is an interpretation of the circuit rather than a picture of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup>

Most designs do not need one. Deep-memory oscilloscopes have absorbed much of the traditional workload — [[spi]], I²C and RS-232 debugging are routinely done on a scope with a serial decoder — and for most developers a logic analyzer is not a necessary purchase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Reaching for one generally signals that a design is already ninety-nine percent working and the remaining fault is a subtle timing problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> It is the right tool for protocol work; a broken, shorted or floating line is a job for a scope probe instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

## Timing analysis and state analysis

There are two modes of operation, and the distinction determines what an instrument can and cannot do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Timing analysis runs from an internal clock, taking fast samples and showing inputs changing against time much like an oscilloscope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> State analysis samples on an external clock supplied by the circuit under test, so acquisition is synchronised with the target and every captured sample corresponds to a real bus cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup>

State analysis therefore requires an external clock input. An instrument without one — such as the GW Instek GDS-2000A logic analyzer pods, which carry no external clock connection — is timing-analysis-only regardless of what else it offers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> The same restriction applies to essentially all mixed-signal oscilloscopes: they capture digital channels on the scope's own [[time-base|timebase]] and cannot be clocked from the target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup> Low-cost [[usb]] instruments are frequently in the same position; lacking an input latch, the Saleae Logic is effectively timing analysis only.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup> State analysis has become less common in ordinary work, but it remains a genuine capability line between a real logic analyzer and a digital option bolted onto a scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup>

Timing analysis carries its own artefact: because sampling is asynchronous to the signal, edges land wherever the sample clock happens to fall, producing apparent jitter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup> Undersampling is worse. A 2 MHz square wave captured at 12 megasamples per second reads back as alternating 2.4 MHz and 2 MHz because there is insufficient temporal resolution to place the edges; dropped to 8 megasamples per second, whole sections of the waveform vanish entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup>

## Thresholds and the limits of a digital view

What appears on screen is what the input latch believed the signal to be at the instant it sampled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> The real input may be noisy, overshooting or undershooting, and the instrument reduces all of it to a decision against a logic-low and a logic-high threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Probing itself influences that decision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> A logic analyzer returns a one or a zero against a fixed threshold and takes no account of [[signal-integrity]], so a circuit with a marginal analog problem can produce plausible-looking digital results and send an investigation chasing faults that are not where they appear to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup> Working with a logic analyzer means largely trusting it, which is among its major pitfalls; an oscilloscope, correctly probed, shows what is actually present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Some instruments do display waveshape as well, effectively acting as a multi-channel crude oscilloscope, but they sit at prices that place them outside ordinary bench budgets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup>

Threshold flexibility separates price tiers. Fixed thresholds are the norm on low-priced instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> A $69 sixteen-channel USB unit defaults its threshold to zero volts and offers adjustment up to 6 V, but sets it globally rather than per channel — expected at the bottom of the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1720" title="EEVblog 1720 - Mailbag: USSR Bonanza + Logic Training + $69 Logic Analyzer">1720</a></sup> Threshold range also bounds what can be measured at all: a Padauk programmer proved undecodable because the supply [[voltage]] changed between programming stages, moving above the analyzer's input range, and inserting a [[voltage-divider]] pushed the earlier samples below the threshold instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup> Threshold handling can also simply be wrong. A Rigol MSO5000 displayed multiple pulses on two channels at 50 MHz that were not present in the signal, consistently, at a 1.4 V threshold, while a Rigol 7000 fed the identical signal showed none.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup>

## Memory and compression

[[sample-memory]] is the other axis on which logic analyzers are separated. Compression sampling — storing a sample only when something changes — is extremely valuable because it maximises the use of available memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Its trade-off is that a change on any one channel forces a sample across all channels, so a single fast-clocking line can consume the entire buffer before a slow, widely spaced packet of interest arrives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Instruments using compression are commonly fitted with only a couple of kilobytes of sample memory on the assumption that compression will cover it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup>

Without compression, raw depth is what remains. The Digilent Analog Discovery provides sixteen digital channels at 100 megasamples per second with 4 k of channel memory and no compression, which is thin for logic work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup> Capture depth is a practical constraint rather than a specification detail: analysing a NiMH charger's multiplexed [[mosfet]] drive ran into the limit of what a single acquisition could hold, requiring deeper memory, sample compression, or segmented memory to see further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> Implementing compression is not free — it needs hardware and [[firmware]] capability in the instrument, usually inside an [[fpga]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-290" title="EEVblog #290 - Mailbag">290</a></sup>

Architecture also determines whether data can be lost. An instrument that buffers into its own memory and uploads afterwards is immune to host contention but bounded by buffer size; a streaming USB device has effectively unlimited memory provided the data rate stays within the transfer capability, but depends on a low-latency port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup>

## Instrument forms

Three forms are worth distinguishing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Traditional bench instruments are as large as or larger than a bench oscilloscope, cost from around $5,000 into the tens and hundreds of thousands, and consume bench space that is hard to justify without a specific need.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Instruments of this class are still built for cases that demand them, including units with 136 channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup> The second form is the combined scope and logic analyzer, typically two or four analog channels plus eight or sixteen digital.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> The third is the USB instrument that hands display and analysis to a PC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup>

For logic work specifically, the PC form has substantial advantages: a large screen, a mouse, a keyboard and better search than any front-panel interface can provide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup> Sixteen digital channels plus four analog crammed onto a scope screen is a genuine usability problem, and mixed-signal capability, however convenient, is fiddly by comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup> The reverse holds for analog work, where physical knobs beat a PC interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup>

Against that, the mixed-signal instrument gives time correlation between analog and digital channels in one acquisition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup> That correlation can be reconstructed with separate instruments by feeding the scope's trigger output into any spare input channel of the logic analyzer, which requires no dedicated external trigger input — less convenient, since the two displays live on different screens, but functional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup> Where a spare pin exists on a design under development, firmware can be made to assert it as a trigger marker for whatever instrument is attached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## The cost of the digital option

On oscilloscopes the logic analyzer is almost always a paid option, and it is priced aggressively. The eight-channel option on the Agilent 2000 X series ran about $700, and on the 3000 X series the digital option exceeded a thousand dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> Siglent charged $469 for a sixteen-channel probe and software licence on the SDS5000X.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> The GW Instek eight-channel option was around $570.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Rigol asked $400 for MSO5000 probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup> On the Tektronix 4 series the digital capability consumes one of the instrument's flexible input channels rather than adding to them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup>

Those figures sit against standalone instruments in the same range or below. A capable standalone USB logic analyzer could be had for under a thousand dollars against a scope option costing over one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> The consistent engineering judgement is to buy the base scope, take the software serial-decode option, and add a separate logic analyzer if and when the need appears — unless the mixed-signal correlation is specifically required or budget is not a constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup>

Capability at the low end varies considerably. A ChronoView LA8 in the sub-$200 category offered no sample compression, rudimentary triggering and no state triggering, though it did the job it claimed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-290" title="EEVblog #290 - Mailbag">290</a></sup> ZeroPlus hardware runs from around $3,000 to $6,000 for a 64-channel unit, but the same software — carrying the widest set of serial decoders of any instrument of its kind, down to SD card protocol — ships with models around $139; the software itself is unpolished, lacking edge snapping on cursors and sensible time readout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup>

## Probing and practice

Input front end and probing are decisive. A cheap front end and cheap probes produce the same result on a logic analyzer as on an oscilloscope: not a serious tool.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Inexpensive USB units are adequate for I²C and similar low-speed buses, but at 50 to a few hundred megahertz they do not hold up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> The act of connecting probes can alter the circuit, and on a multi-channel system probing one line can make a marginal fault appear or disappear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup>

Two habits follow. Hook-up is laborious — every channel must be attached, verified for contact, and identified — and labelling each channel in software before capture is worth the few minutes it costs on anything beyond one or two channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Where signals are physically hard to reach, [[soldering]] fine mod wire to convenient vias creates accessible tap points, avoiding the impossibility of holding several probes on a fine-pitch package at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Scope Bandwidth Hack Investigation">879</a></sup>

Typical bench use is protocol work: SPI, I²C, CAN and one-wire buses, rather than the wide parallel buses of the era when boards carried hundreds of interconnected logic chips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup> Concrete applications run from verifying that an SPI bus is carrying the data the firmware intends before the fault is sought elsewhere,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> to capturing an infrared remote control's code output for protocol [[reverse-engineering]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup> to confirming that a [[microcontroller]] is emitting the expected data before it is connected to the display hardware it drives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup> The standing recommendation is a simple USB logic analyzer kept in a drawer for the occasions that need it: valuable, infrequently used, and cheap enough that it need not earn its bench space.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup>

## Implementation and internals

Inside oscilloscopes, the digital acquisition path is visible as a distinct block: input connector, comparators, level shifting and latching, feeding the main capture FPGA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> Because the option is so commonly unsold, the depopulated footprint is a recurring sight — the Siglent 1000X, the Uni-T UPO3000 and the Rigol DHO800 all carry unfitted logic analyzer circuitry, and an Agilent 2000 X series board laid out for sixteen channels was fitted with only two of four devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-144" title="EEVblog #144 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">144</a></sup> Some designs move the analog front end off the mainboard entirely; the Rohde & Schwarz HMO1202 has no comparators or threshold circuitry on the main board because the level detection lives in the HO3508 logic probe pod.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup>

The instrument class is also a long-standing hobbyist project target. A 32-channel PC-based design published in *Electronics Australia* in October 1996, drawn up in 1995, ran 40 MHz in timing analysis mode and 20 MHz in state analysis, was TTL/CMOS compatible, used a PC parallel port interface, and provided latch trigger word masking fully maskable across all 32 channels plus optional glitch capture, with address and data disassembly in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1326" title="EEVblog #1326 - How Engineering Minds Think Alike">1326</a></sup> Its control and trigger functions were split across two devices because fully maskable triggering — high, low or don't-care per channel, set from software — needed dedicated logic, ultimately implemented in a CPLD/FPGA part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup> Elektor published a near-identical design in May 1996, independently arrived at on the other side of the world.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1326" title="EEVblog #1326 - How Engineering Minds Think Alike">1326</a></sup> Such designs are also the reason timing diagrams matter: a timing diagram is simply digital transitions plotted against time, the same view a logic analyzer produces from real hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup>
