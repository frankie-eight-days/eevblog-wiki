A low dropout regulator, universally abbreviated LDO, is a [[linear-regulator|linear voltage regulator]] that requires only a small [[voltage]] differential between its input and output in order to stay in regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Every linear regulator carries a [[dropout-voltage|dropout voltage]] specification — the minimum input-to-output differential below which the device stops regulating and the output simply follows the input down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The LDO is not architecturally exotic: it is the same closed-loop series-pass regulator as any other linear part, distinguished only by a pass element chosen to drop less voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup> That single property is what makes [[battery]]-powered and cascaded designs practical, and it is bought at the cost of loop stability, [[ripple]] rejection, and fussier [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

## How the dropout voltage arises

In a conventional linear regulator the series pass element is an NPN Darlington [[transistor]], so the minimum input-to-output differential must cover two base-emitter drops plus the saturation voltage of the driving transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> That is why a 7805 shows a typical dropout of around 1.5 to 2 volts, and why the figure cannot be designed away without changing the topology.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The [[lm317|LM317]] is likewise not an LDO: its [[datasheet|data sheet]] calls for roughly 3 volts of input-to-output differential typically, and around 1.5 to 2 volts is the working rule of thumb at full [[current]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Its dropout curve runs from about 1 volt to 3 volts depending on load current and temperature, with separate load lines for 20 mA, 200 mA, 500 mA, 1 A and the maximum 1.5 A output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Dropout is therefore not a single number but a surface over load and temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

An LDO replaces the Darlington with a single PNP or P-channel pass element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Everything else in the loop is unchanged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> With no Darlington stack to feed, the differential collapses: general-purpose LDOs reach several hundred millivolts, and parts exist with dropout in the region of 50 to 100 millivolts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> A typical figure for a small linear LDO powering a 3.3 volt rail is 0.1 to 0.2 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> The LM1117 family sits between the extremes — significantly better than the LM317, on the order of 1 to 1.2 volts, which is good enough to derive a 3.3 volt rail downstream of a 7805 but not good enough when tens of millivolts of headroom are all that remain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

## Why the headroom matters

The obvious penalty for a high dropout voltage is dissipation, since the pass element carries the full load current across the differential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The less obvious and often more binding constraint is that the margin simply is not available. In circuits with cascading regulators — one regulator powered from another — the differential budget can run out entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> A design running an LM317 near its dropout limit will fail to deliver rated [[output-current|output current]], whereas an LDO with 0.2 volts of dropout in the same position leaves a large margin and the current specification is met without difficulty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Where an LM317 has been used only because nothing better was on hand, replacing it with a fixed-voltage LDO removes both the margin problem and the adjustment [[resistor]] pair, leaving just the regulator and its input and output capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

Battery-powered equipment is where the headroom translates most directly into runtime. A 3.3 volt circuit can be run straight from a lithium-ion cell through an LDO, exploiting the flatter discharge curve of a graphite-anode cell that holds a usable voltage far longer than a coke-anode one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> In a [[multimeter]] whose highest-voltage internal part is a 3.6 volt chip, LDOs with dropout of perhaps 50 or 100 millivolts allow the low-battery threshold to be set only slightly above 3.6 volts — 0.9 volts per cell across four AA cells, which extracts the vast majority of the stored energy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup> The same reasoning applies in reverse: a nickel-metal-hydride pack dropping to 1.1 volts per cell across eight cells reaches 8.8 volts, and without an LDO a 6.[[5-volt-rail|5 volt rail]] derived from it is in trouble well before the cells are exhausted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup> A 5 volt regulator fed from four AA cells at 6 volts nominal will not hold regulation across much of the discharge curve even if it is a low dropout type.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup> Where an internal regulator is a 4 volt LDO that drops out at roughly 4.1 volts, a 9 volt battery can be run down to 5 volts and its full capacity used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup>

## Stability and the output capacitor

The classic problem with LDOs is stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> The PNP or P-channel pass element is inherently less stable than the Darlington NPN it replaces, and an LDO will oscillate if it is not loaded with the correct amount of [[capacitance]], of the correct type, with the correct ESR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> LDOs are accordingly less tolerant than a 7805-class part: they need a specific type and size of output capacitor to work properly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> With no [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] at all, a low dropout regulator driving 200 milliamps misbehaves visibly; 0.47 µF restores stability at that current, though good practice is to fit the recommended 2.2 µF ceramic rather than anything less.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

This requirement is prominent enough that an LDO data sheet will often devote an entire section to output capacitor selection, specifying not just a value but an ESR window — for example, stability with tantalum and aluminium electrolytic capacitors of ESR between 0.2 and 10 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The [[capacitor]] that satisfies the value, [[impedance]], and stability requirements simultaneously can cost more than the regulator's other passives combined, which undercuts the low-cost premise of a jellybean part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> There is a stable region and an unstable region, and a capacitor cannot simply be added to the output on the assumption that more is safer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The same caveat applies to any circuit built around an error amplifier: linear regulators, LDOs, and voltage references alike oscillate without the correct bypass capacitors on input and output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Low-ESR electrolytics are made specifically for this service alongside switch-mode supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup>

Because an LDO's stability requirements are strict and its advantage is irrelevant when input voltage is plentiful, using an LDO on a 9 to 12 volt input to produce 5 volts is poor practice — a conventional fixed regulator is the better choice there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup>

## Ripple rejection

Regulators are widely assumed to clean up a dirty supply, and they are in fact quite poor at attenuating large amounts of input ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> An LDO holds its output precisely at 3.3 volts or whatever it is set to, but with tens or hundreds of millivolts of ripple at the input, the attenuation from input to output depends on both the regulator type and the input-to-output differential actually present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> As the differential is reduced, the rejection can get worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> This creates a direct conflict with the reason for choosing an LDO in the first place: with only a low dropout voltage across the part, [[noise]] can pass largely through the regulator, so an LDO on the output is not a magic bullet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

The practical consequence appears in charge-pump designs. A 7660-style [[inverter]] has a ripple floor that cannot be driven to zero simply by increasing capacitance, so post-regulation with an LDO is a common remedy — but its effectiveness is limited by how much voltage is dropped across that post-regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Post-regulation with an LDO, a Zener, or RC or LC filtering all suffer the same dependence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Where the requirement is only a small clean low-current rail, an LDO downstream of an inverter or doubler works well: from a 5 volt supply, even with 1N4148 diodes and modest capacitors, a fully regulated 3.3 volt output is achievable, and a low-power LDO at 3.3 volts will supply a couple of hundred microamps from a 3.64 volt inverter output loaded at 10 k.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup>

Where noise rather than ripple is the concern, dedicated low-noise LDOs exist for analogue front ends: the LP3878 is an adjustable low-noise LDO used to power [[oscilloscope]] analogue front-end circuitry, and the Micrel MIC5209 serves the same role.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup> Vendors also offer high-PSRR linear regulators matched to their own chipsets, and these are often adopted straight from the data sheet's recommended application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup>

## Where LDOs are used

The dominant architecture in modern instruments is a switching pre-regulator followed by local LDO post-regulation at the point of load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup> This is why a well-designed board can carry no regulation at all on the output of its main converter — every rail is regulated locally by LDOs instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> Because the pre-regulator sets the input close to each rail's target, the LDOs dissipate perhaps a watt each regardless of [[output-voltage|output voltage]] while doing the job of cleaning up the switcher output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup> Multiple converter taps are used specifically to keep the LDO dissipation as low as possible, since the [[pcb|PCB]] alone is serving as the [[heat-sink|heat sink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> In sensitive RF instruments the same logic is pushed further: the switch-mode supply stays off the analogue board entirely and everything on it is linear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup>

Local low dropout regulation of individual rails is pervasive: SOT-223 1117-series LDOs scattered across an oscilloscope main board with small heat sinks attached,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> local 1117-class regulators feeding an RF front end,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup> a dedicated LDO for a 2.5 volt memory termination rail,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-491" title="EEVblog #491 - Nintendo 64 Game Console Teardown">491</a></sup> and 3 volt LDOs in a precision multimeter's isolated analogue section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Recurring named parts include the LM1117 and its variants,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup> the Linear Technology LT3009 in a smartwatch running directly from the battery,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup> the LP3982 supplying 3.3 volts from 5 volts to a [[microcontroller]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> the L4931,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup> the LM340,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup> and the Microchip TC1014, a low-power fixed-voltage LDO in a SOT23-5 package with an industry-standard pinout that allows it to drop in as a replacement for competing parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 1 of 2 - Meterman 37XR Multimeter review">6</a></sup>

LDOs are also among the cheapest silicon available; on Chinese catalogue distributors they can be had for around one cent in small SOT-23 packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

## Integration into other silicon

Because an LDO is small and cheap in die area, it is frequently absorbed into the chip that needs it. A GPS receiver with a built-in LDO removes a discrete regulator from the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]], and vendors advertise this explicitly as a systems-integration and BOM-cost argument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> Microcontrollers do the same internally: an STM32L derives its 1.2 volt core rail from an on-chip low dropout regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> At the other end of the scale, a power management IC can bundle five configurable synchronous step-down converters, nine LDOs, two switches, a battery fuel gauge, a real-time clock and an audio codec in one package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup>

Integration is not free. A charge-pump inverter offered with and without an integrated LDO costs 44 cents against 82 cents in 3,000-piece quantity, the difference being the additional silicon area the regulator occupies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Against that, integrating the LDO can consolidate a 7660 and a separate positive regulator and the negative-rail filtering LDO into a single device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

The low-dropout principle extends beyond voltage regulators: low dropout [[led|LED]] drivers for parallel-connected LEDs achieve a [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] below 150 millivolts, which matters directly for battery-driven lighting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup>

## When it is not needed

An LDO is not automatically the right choice. Where the input voltage is comfortably above the output, the extra cost, the output capacitor constraints, and the degraded ripple rejection buy nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The 7805 and the LM317 remain the archetypal jellybean regulators precisely because they impose no capacitor selection problem, and both are explicitly not LDOs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The decision turns on whether the differential margin exists: when it does, a conventional linear regulator is the simpler part; when it does not — a cascaded rail, a discharging battery, a 3.3 volt load run from a lithium cell — the LDO is the only linear option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>
