Mains voltage is the alternating supply [[voltage]] delivered to a domestic or commercial outlet, and it is the single environmental parameter that most equipment on a bench is forced to live with unmodified. It is not a constant: it drifts by several volts over the course of a day, varies by region and by street, and is specified as a nominal figure with a wide tolerance band rather than as a fixed number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> Because almost everything mains-powered derives its internal rails from it, the actual voltage at the socket propagates into [[transformer]] secondaries, regulator dissipation, [[fuse]] ratings and component stress.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gqzZHbEfWDU" title="KoradFollowup Charles">•</a></sup>

## Nominal values and real values

Australia is nominally 230 V with a tolerance of plus or minus 10 per cent, having been changed from a 240 V nominal a decade or two earlier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-31gkF0q9VfE" title="Zappi EV Charger &amp; Cable Thermal Imaging">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vNKcRs3zDBI" title="We&#x27;re Gonna Need a Bigger Solar Battery...">•</a></sup> The nominal figure and the delivered figure are not the same thing. Dave Jones's lab and home in Sydney both sit near the top of the allowable band: readings of 240 to 245 V are typical, 246 to 248 V is common, and it reaches almost 250 V at times.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uC-q-FjXEsA" title="1980s EEVblog Pocket Multimeter Overload Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1305" title="EEVblog #1305 - HEPA Solder Fume Extractor">1305</a></sup> The legal ceiling in Australia is around 253 or 254 V, and the local supply runs right at that top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vNKcRs3zDBI" title="We&#x27;re Gonna Need a Bigger Solar Battery...">•</a></sup> A wrist-worn meter probed directly into an outlet read 254 V AC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1706" title="EEVblog 1706 - K8 Wrist Worn Multimeter REVIEW">1706</a></sup> Elsewhere the corpus records 110 or 120 V nominal supplies, and equipment sold into those markets is built accordingly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-2 - Talking Electronics - America - Part 2 of 5">280</a></sup>

Other territories present the same gap between label and reality. A US-supplied instrument arriving in Australia is typically still configured for 115 V, and some dealers will reconfigure it to 240 V before shipping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> A 110 V supply feeding a 2,000 W copier sagged to 80 or 90 V, enough for the machine to hiccup and stall; the cause was the wiring around the wall rather than the service entry, since plugging in at the incoming point cured it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-2 - Talking Electronics - America - Part 2 of 5">280</a></sup>

## Variation through the day

Mains voltage rises through the day by a couple of volts, tapers off, and then curiously rises again around 7:00 p.m.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> The drift is large enough to show up downstream: an amplifier rail measured 48 V one day and over 50 V the next, with no change to the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

Distributed [[solar-power-generation|solar generation]] is a plausible driver of the daytime rise, since exported power pushes up the local grid voltage. A grid-connected [[inverter]] reached around 250 V, and at times 252 V, on its own front-panel display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> Where the supply already sits at the top of the band, this is close enough to the trip threshold to be suspected of curtailing output. Logging a [[121gw|121GW]] [[multimeter]] at 10-second intervals and overlaying that against 5-minute solar production data showed that mains voltage went *down* at the exact moment output plummeted by 15 to 20 per cent, which ruled mains voltage out as the cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> Even when a correlation had appeared, correlation would not have established causation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> The actual culprit was the diffuse shadow of a [[coaxial-cable|coax]] [[antenna]] mast crossing the array at 9:40 a.m.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup>

A hybrid inverter installation logged an AC output excursion down to 181 V that correlated with grid [[frequency]], suggesting a protective setting rather than a supply fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vNKcRs3zDBI" title="We&#x27;re Gonna Need a Bigger Solar Battery...">•</a></sup> A line-interactive UPS handles the same problem in hardware, indicating over-voltage and switching in a [[transformer-tap|transformer tap]] to pull the output back down, or the reverse tap when the mains is sagging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup>

## Equipment configured for a specific supply

Much bench equipment is single-voltage only, because its transformer is wound for one supply and has no taps. [[soldering|Soldering]] stations from several makers are built this way, so buying a unit from a foreign market means buying the wrong one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup> A 120 V soldering station plugged into 240 V billowed smoke out the bottom within seconds, with the enamel burned off the thin primary winding; the absence of a fuse meant it could have caught fire unattended.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup> A DIY reflow oven controller was 240 V only and not offered in the US at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>

Where selection is provided it takes several forms: a rear-panel voltage selector switch,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> the old-style plug-in voltage selection block on vintage instruments,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1716" title="EEVblog 1716 - University Dumpster Diving: Kikusui Oscilloscope">1716</a></sup> internal jumpers or straps that put two primary coils in series for 230/240 V or in parallel for 115 V,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> or genuine dual-range operation with no configuration worry at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup> The primary [[resistance]] measured with the coils in series is more than double the parallel value, since the parallel measurement returns half of a single coil.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> Fuse rating is part of the configuration and differs per mains voltage — a rear-panel label specifying half an amp for 240 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> The practical rule is to download and read the manual before buying, since it will state whether the unit can be configured for other regions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup>

Running equipment at a higher voltage than it was designed for stresses the parts even when it does not destroy them outright. Linear supplies designed for 220 V and run on 240 V put that extra 20 V across the regulator transistors, dissipating substantially more power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gqzZHbEfWDU" title="KoradFollowup Charles">•</a></sup> A bench supply whose transformer tap was never changed continued to deliver an unnecessarily high input to its [[lm317|LM317]] because the lab supply ran at 247 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> A fume extractor was not rated for the 240 or 245 V present at the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1305" title="EEVblog #1305 - HEPA Solder Fume Extractor">1305</a></sup> A linear supply with a 220/240 V inlet and no selection switch simply will not work on 110 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup>

## Measurement

Measuring mains directly calls for the right instrument. A dedicated high-voltage probe is the proper way to safely measure mains voltage on an [[oscilloscope]]; 704 V peak-to-peak corresponds to 245 V AC RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1283" title="EEVblog #1283- What is Mains Ripple Injection?">1283</a></sup> Socket testers report the line-to-neutral voltage and, more usefully, the neutral-to-earth voltage, which indicates not merely that neutral and earth are bonded but how well — effectively the resistance of the neutral-earth path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> The chipsets used in such testers are multimeter ICs that also integrate a non-contact voltage circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kU7zSSuy9WQ" title="How to ID a Mystery Microcontroller">•</a></sup>

Many multimeters include a non-contact [[electric-field|electric field]] function that indicates the presence of mains voltage when brought close to an energised 240 V or 110 V cable, though detector sensitivity varies and some units fail to register on unloaded mains even on their low range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup> Meters also measure mains frequency in hertz, and a 50 Hz mains period appears as 20 milliseconds across a screen at 5 ms per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1059" title="EEVblog #1059 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Waveform Measurement">1059</a></sup> Some oscilloscopes derive line sync capacitively: a two-pin header with wires laid alongside the active and neutral inside heat-shrink, with no [[electrical-contact|electrical contact]], couples enough signal for the [[processor]] to measure mains frequency and adjust automatically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-564" title="EEVblog #564 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Teardown Repair">564</a></sup> Mains voltage can also be logged continuously over a [[serial-port|serial interface]] for later analysis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1684" title="EEVblog 1684 - Awesome Logging Software:  Test Controller">1684</a></sup> For deliberately testing the upper and lower limits of a mains [[power-supply|power supply]], a programmable AC source allows the voltage to be dialled in, with frequency selectable at 50, 60, 100 or 400 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1698" title="EEVblog 1698 - Awesome Ebay AC Power Source: GW Instek APS-7050">1698</a></sup>

## Hazard and protection

Mains is a low-[[impedance]] source with a great deal of energy behind it, which is why X-class capacitors placed directly across it are designed not to fail short and not to catch fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1183" title="EEVblog #1183 - RIFA Madness (Schaffner Repair)">1183</a></sup> The same energy makes instrument [[input-protection|input protection]] non-negotiable: a pocket multimeter blew up in the hand while measuring 240 V mains,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> another failed outright on mains applied to its ohms range,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1084" title="EEVblog #1084 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout Part 2">1084</a></sup> and a scope-meter with no input protection at all should not be used on the mains under any circumstances.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W2o8otvVML4" title="ZERO INPUT PROTECTION! Micsig MHO14 Oscilloscope/Multimeter">•</a></sup> Well-designed instruments protect against a user mistakenly plugging 240 V mains into the sense terminals,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup> and a robust handheld tolerates 240 V applied to a millivolt range without damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

Historic live-chassis televisions connected all internal metalwork directly to the mains, placing it at 240 V potential with only the plastic case separating the user from it; even the shield of the coax at the antenna [[connector]] sat at mains potential, isolated only by a [[capacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup> Present-day hazards are more mundane: an instrument whose pop-out foot leaves a gap through which a screwdriver can reach the mains input circuitry,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> an appliance with mains taken straight to the element and no earth on its metalwork,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-893" title="EEVblog #893 - Mailbag">893</a></sup> and abandoned equipment with mains-rated conductors and heatsinks left exposed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-YaTldV7-uXo" title="When Safety Goes Out The Window">•</a></sup> When probing a powered device, the mains side is off-limits and only secondary-side heatsinks should be touched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CK5nbC_dBWk" title="Siglent Signal Generator FAIL">•</a></sup> Circuits that derive a low-voltage supply straight from the mains through series resistors and a [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] are non-isolated, and testing them requires an isolation transformer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup> On a board carrying mains, everything on the primary side — bridge rectifier, filter caps with their bleed resistors, common-mode chokes, Y-class capacitors and varistors — is at mains potential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - ## Mains as a design input

Loads are frequently connected straight across the mains and switched by a [[relay]], as in a heater whose controller does nothing more than close a relay on the element,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> or modulated by a triac switching whole or half cycles of the 50 Hz [[waveform]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1059" title="EEVblog #1059 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Waveform Measurement">1059</a></sup> Devices intended to operate from the mains directly need high-voltage parts: a jellybean bipolar with a 400 V collector-emitter rating covers most mains-type applications at 220 mA continuous collector [[current]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup>

For low-current loads, a small regulator fed from 240 V mains through a rectifier and capacitor draws orders of magnitude less current than a Zener-based alternative.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Constant-current drive removes the supply voltage from the equation altogether: an [[led|LED]] lamp with a constant-current driver produces the same brightness whether it is fed 110 V, 230 V or the 245 V present in the lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Not all mains-powered lighting is so forgiving — an LED tube driver can drop 50 to 100 V of the 240 V available, and a tube whose [[pcb|PCB]] is inserted the wrong way round applies the mains to the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup> LED tubes rated for 230 V operated in a lab sitting above 245 V are being run at the top of their limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-531" title="EEVblog #531 - LAB LED Fluoro Lighting Tube Replacement">531</a></sup>

True zero [[standby-power|standby power]] is difficult on a 230, 240 or 110 V mains set precisely because switching the mains input itself requires a solid-state relay with adequate current handling and, in the case of SCR-based devices, significant input drive current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup> Measuring standby consumption after a 12 V plugpack, rather than at the mains input, hides the plugpack's own quiescent draw.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup>

Higher mains voltage is not uniformly a disadvantage. An EV charger drawing a fixed power at 240 to 245 V rather than 230 V draws less current, giving lower copper losses in the cable — a 6 mm² radial measured roughly 10 °C above ambient at 32 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-31gkF0q9VfE" title="Zappi EV Charger &amp; Cable Thermal Imaging">•</a></sup> A portable EVSE on a 15 A outlet delivers 15 A at whatever the supply voltage happens to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup>
