Burden voltage is the [[voltage]] dropped across a [[multimeter]]'s terminals when it is used to measure [[current]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> It exists because [[current-measurement|current measurement]] works by passing the current through an internal [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] and measuring the voltage developed across it; that voltage is unavoidably subtracted from the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup> Every multimeter on the market has this problem, and the drop is far larger than most users expect — hundreds of millivolts to a volt at full scale on ordinary meters, and in bad cases several volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Inserting an ammeter therefore does not merely observe a circuit, it loads it: the meter becomes a burden on the circuit, which is where the name comes from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup>

## Mechanism

The meter's terminal [[resistance]] is the sum of the current shunt resistor and the internal [[fuse]] resistance, and both are in series with the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> The consequence follows directly from Ohm's law. A 1 V supply across a 100 ohm [[resistor]] should give 10 mA, but with a meter inserted the loop resistance is 100 ohms plus the meter's shunt resistance, so the measured current is less than 10 mA — in practice 9.8 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> The error is an insertion error: the act of measuring changes what is being measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup>

Because each current range uses a different shunt value, the burden voltage changes with the range selected as well as with the current flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> Switching the same 9.8 mA measurement from the milliamp range to the microamp range, where the internal resistance is much higher, collapses the reading to 4.96 mA; returning to the amps range restores the true 10 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> The drop also scales with current within a range — taking a meter from 1 mA to 10 mA raises the burden voltage by an order of magnitude, in one instrument reaching a full 1 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup>

## Typical values and how the specification is written

A representative multimeter front end uses three shunt values shared across the ranges — 100 ohms, 1 ohm, and 10 milliohms — which are the values found in the vast majority of meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Each range is designed for 50 mV full scale: 500 microamps through 100 ohms gives 50 mV, which is what a [[datasheet]] means when it quotes a maximum burden voltage of 50 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Because ranges share resistors, some combinations are far worse than others; the 5000 microamp and 500 milliamp ranges are the troublesome ones, dropping 500 mV where the well-matched ranges drop 50 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Half a volt on a 3.3 V rail means the circuit sees 2.8 V at the chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup>

Burden voltage is more correctly specified in volts per amp rather than as a single full-scale figure, since that form lets the drop at any particular current be calculated: at 0.1 mV per microamp, 100 microamps produces a 10 mV drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Published figures in that form span roughly an order of magnitude across the market. A good handheld should be around 1 mV per milliamp, or equivalently 0.1 mV per microamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A Fluke 87 V is specified at 1.8 mV per milliamp, which at 200 mA is a 360 mV drop — enough to throw a 5 V circuit out of specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-i069j6S80OU" title="uCurrent">•</a></sup> A Gossen Metrawatt instrument reaches 1.6 mV per mA, or 0.12 mV per microamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup> An Agilent U1253A sits at 2 mV per milliamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup> At the bad end, the Meterman 37XR drops 10 mV per milliamp, an order of magnitude worse than a good meter, and the range had to be limited arbitrarily to 400 mA rather than the full 999 mA because of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 1 of 2 - Meterman 37XR Multimeter review">6</a></sup> Amprobe meters have carried some of the worst burden voltages in the industry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Price is a poor predictor: a $2,000 multimeter can have the same problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup>

Measured performance often beats the specification. An [[agilent-u1272a|Agilent U1272A]] claims under 40 mV on the 300 microamp range and delivers 30 mV, claims 0.4 V on the 3 mA range and delivers 0.3 V, and worst-cases at 0.66 V at 300 mA against a 1 V claim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Some analog meters state the figure directly — 316 mV on DC amps in one instance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Comparing two meters in the same family, the [[brymen-bm786|Brymen BM786]] specifies 0.15 mV per microamp against 0.2 mV per microamp for the BM869, and is lower on both major milliamp ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PfzgOuBHJFM" title="Brymen BM786 vs BM869 DC Current">•</a></sup>

The fuse is part of the burden and is easily forgotten. Reducing a shunt resistor by an order of magnitude does not help if a 600 mA fuse contributing 1 ohm remains in series, which keeps the total in the region of 500 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> In the [[121gw|121GW]] the milliamp-range shunt is about 1 ohm and the [[hrc-fuse|HRC fuse]] about 2 ohms, so the fuse dominates the burden voltage on the 50 mA range; worst case including the fuse is about 150 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> Fuse resistance is also a reason not to substitute fuse types freely, since the characteristics of a product can change on burden voltage grounds alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1377" title="EEVblog 1377 - The Amazing UNPREDICTABILITY of Fuses!">1377</a></sup> A 400 mA automotive-style fuse can itself drop a substantial voltage: 400 mA through one measured 0.3 V, though a two-wire measurement includes cable drop and the fuse alone is nearer 0.2 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup>

## Consequences on the bench

The failure mode is that the device under test stops working, or works differently, while being measured. On a development board with the power jumper replaced by a meter on the milliamp jack, an [[led|LED]] barely lights and the board does not power up; moving to the amps jack, with its much lower shunt, brings the board straight to life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup> An LM3914 [[battery]] gauge shows the same effect at 56 microamps: switching from a low-burden front end to the meter's internal shunt turns an LED off, and switching back turns it on again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> A cheap multimeter under test would not start at all through the low-burden setting of a 121GW and had to be measured on the 500 mA range instead, where it drew 22 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>

The most damaging case is voltage-dropout testing, where the point of the exercise is to know the supply voltage precisely at the moment a product stops working or raises a low-battery warning.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> With a meter in series, the supply may read 4.5 V while the product is actually seeing 3 V or 3.5 V, and the measured dropout point is simply wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> The same trap appears in DC-DC converter testing: with 260 mA flowing and the input apparently at 4.5 V, right on the converter's datasheet minimum, switching the meter from the milliamp jack to the amps jack returns the same current but sends the input voltage back to 5 V — the entire drop was inside the meter, not the leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> Burden voltage likewise sits in series with wiring and [[breadboard]] resistance when a load voltage is measured through the meter's path rather than at the load itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> It can also unbalance the circuit being probed: measuring the supposedly zero current in the arms of a resistor cube built from 4.7 k resistors is unreliable even with a low-burden meter, because a couple of hundred ohms of inserted resistance disturbs the bridge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>

Not every situation is affected. On the amps ranges the burden voltage is typically never really an issue, because high-current measurements are usually made on system-level hardware running from 12 V or similar rather than on low-voltage logic rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> On a DC-DC converter output, the ammeter's burden does not matter provided the load has been dialled in to give the intended [[output-current|output current]], although a fixed resistive load has to be tweaked to compensate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup>

## Working around it

Two workarounds require no extra hardware. Switching up a range trades resolution for a much lower shunt: a 490 mA measurement moved from the 500 mA range to the 5 A range drops only about 5 mV instead of 490 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Alternatively, the supply feeding the project can be tweaked upward to compensate for the meter's drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> A related discipline is to measure the device's voltage directly at its input pins rather than upstream of the meter, which removes both lead drop and burden voltage from the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-78" title="EEVblog #78 - Fenix LD01 LED Keychain Flashlight And Victorinox Minichamp Review">78</a></sup> Keeping two multimeters on the bench supports both practices, and also allows the burden voltage to be measured directly by putting the second meter across the current jacks of the first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup>

The general solution is to lower the shunt resistance and amplify the resulting smaller signal. This is the principle of the µCurrent: a box placed in series with the meter that substitutes a much lower shunt resistor and a times-100 amplifier, so the effective shunt is 100 times lower — though still not zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup> The design is not free of difficulty; a times-100 stage divides an op-amp's gain-[[bandwidth]] product by 100, and offset and [[noise]] become significant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> The same amplifier approach was built into the 121GW, giving it low burden voltage on the meter itself along with the ability to measure burden voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> Low-burden meters remain uncommon, and low is not the same as low enough — the manual should be consulted and the actual figure known for the measurement at hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup>

Redesigning a meter's front end runs into the fuse and the switching element. Bypassing a high-resistance fuse means routing lower ranges through the 11 A HRC fuse, which requires a [[relay]] or a beefy [[mosfet|MOSFET]] rated for tens of amps to switch a 10 milliohm shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The MOSFET's RDS(on) then adds to the burden: 5 milliohms at 5 A contributes 25 mV on top of 50 mV from the shunt, for roughly 125 mV total across the terminals on the 5 A range before the fuse heats up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> Cascading shunts in series by decades — 10 milliohms, 100 milliohms, 1 ohm, 10 ohms, 100 ohms — makes the full-scale burden voltage identical on every range, since current and resistance change by the same order of magnitude each step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The MOSFET and fuse contributions shrink by a decade per range as well, becoming negligible above the lowest range; the resulting specification was summarised as "50 millivolts plus meh, best spec ever."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> A meter with 50 mV burden voltage on every range would be an excellent instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> The 121GW takes a simpler version of the same route by putting its 500 mA range on the amps jack, so that range uses the 11 A fuse and its burden voltage stays very low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

## Low-burden instruments

Dedicated current instruments achieve burden voltages several orders of magnitude below any handheld by using an active input rather than a passive shunt. A feedback-operational picoammeter such as the Keithley 480 specifies input burden voltage as 200 microvolts or less for an on-range reading when zero is properly adjusted, and measures around 110 microvolts under probing pressure and about 80 microvolts on the 100 nA range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup> An electrometer input is an active servo that compensates for the burden voltage, which is why such instruments reach practically zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> That performance depends on [[calibration]]: a femtoammeter measured about 270 mV of burden voltage at 10 mA on its 20 mA range before self-calibration and 0.3 mV afterwards, the difference being drift since the factory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> Its input amplifier's low [[offset-voltage|offset voltage]] — on the order of 50 microvolts, with a 100 microvolt maximum on the integrated guard buffer — is what sets the burden voltage specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> Burden voltage falls on the lower ranges of such instruments, down to tens of microvolts below 2 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

At the other extreme, a battery-profiling instrument that supplies the power to the device under test rather than sitting passively in series has essentially no burden voltage at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Practical judgement

Burden voltage is a trap for young players: the drop is invisible on the meter's own display, and the reading looks entirely plausible while the circuit behind it is running out of specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> Anyone doing significant current measurement should treat burden voltage as a selection criterion when buying a meter, alongside construction quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> The practical rules are to know the meter's figure in volts per amp, to check whether the resulting drop matters at the voltage being worked at, and to remember that it is not really a problem on the amps range but very much is on the milliamp and microamp ranges where low-voltage rails are measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup>
