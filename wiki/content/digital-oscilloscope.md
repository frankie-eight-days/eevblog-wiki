A digital oscilloscope samples an input [[waveform]], stores the samples in memory, and then draws them on a display, in contrast to an [[analog-oscilloscope|analog oscilloscope]] that traces the signal continuously onto a phosphor [[crt|CRT]] as it arrives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> Both instruments do the same underlying job — displaying [[voltage]] against time — and the controls map onto one another closely enough that the concepts carry straight across.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> The crucial difference is storage: because the waveform exists as data rather than as light on a screen, it can be frozen, zoomed, measured, and analysed after the fact, and single events that occur only once can be captured at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup>

## What storage buys

Pressing stop freezes the acquisition, and the captured record remains available for zooming in and out and for detailed analysis even after the probe has been removed from the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> A 1 Hz signal that an analog scope can only paint as a slowly moving dot stays on a digital screen permanently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Single-shot mode arms the instrument to wait for an event that happens once, and provided the trigger is set up correctly the event is captured and held — something an analog scope cannot do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Digital storage also gives pre-trigger and post-trigger data, since the instrument is sampling continuously and the trigger merely marks a position within the record.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>

Storage is the dominant consideration when choosing an instrument on a budget: a very low-end digital scope capable of single-shot capture is more useful than a top-of-the-line analog scope with dual time bases and high [[bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup> Roll mode is a further digital-only display behaviour, in which the trace does not retrace at all but scrolls continuously like a chart recorder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup>

## Automatic measurement

Modern digital scopes compute measurements from the stored record rather than requiring them to be read off a graticule: peak-to-peak voltage, average value, [[frequency]], and rise and fall time among many others.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> They can calculate [[true-rms|true RMS]] mathematically, agreeing with a [[multimeter]]'s reading to within a small error — 1.74 V against 1.79 V in one direct comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Cursors can be placed on the waveform and the instrument reports the exact time between them, removing the need to count divisions by eye.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Phase difference between channels is likewise an automatic measurement on most modern digital scopes, found under vertical measurements despite having nothing to do with vertical voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup> Because these functions are essentially software, features that a digital scope lacks are often absent by omission rather than by any hardware limitation — XY mode operating on channel differences such as 1 − 2 and 3 − 4 is one such function that ought to be possible but is hard to find implemented.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup>

## The apparent-noise question

Digital scopes have a persistent reputation for being noisier than analog scopes, and it is not correct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup> A digital scope samples the signal and then displays it, so any [[noise]] present is captured in the acquisition and drawn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> An analog scope averages by brightness: noise that is uncorrelated with the sweep and the trigger appears dim or not at all, because those photons strike any given part of the phosphor too rarely to be visible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> The hidden content can be recovered from an analog display by photographing the screen at a long shutter speed, which reveals noise superimposed on the waveform that the eye could not see.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup>

The practical consequence is that the analog scope is the misleading instrument. High-frequency content genuinely present on the signal is hidden by phosphor persistence, and the digital scope is better at showing what is actually there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Common-mode noise in particular is easy to pick up on a digital scope and easy to miss entirely on an analog one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup> [[switching-noise|Switching noise]] measured on both instruments has the same amplitude; the digital scope simply displays it more readily by virtue of its sampling and its greater effective persistence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup>

No fewer than six mechanisms, quite apart from the genuine [[noise-floor|noise floor]] of the input channel and its ADC, affect how much noise appears on a digital display: [[memory-depth|memory depth]], [[analog-bandwidth|analog bandwidth]], boxcar averaging, the intensity-graded display, the [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]], and the [[sample-rate|sample rate]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Turning the intensity control down on a digital scope makes noise vanish in the same way it does on an analog scope, and turning it back up makes it reappear — the data was there throughout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup> Counter-intuitively, a slow-updating low-end scope shows more of this noise than a fast-updating one with variable persistence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-442" title="EEVblog #442 - Analog Vs Digital Oscilloscope Noise">442</a></sup> Intensity-graded displays exist precisely to simulate the analog scope's brightness weighting, and with the grading set to 100% a digital scope behaves like an old-school display with none.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Understanding these mechanisms is a prerequisite for using the instrument properly; the noise myth persists mostly among users who do not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

Digital scopes do have one genuine noise disadvantage: they can radiate interference of their own — screen refresh switching, for instance, couples into a [[coaxial-cable|coax]] held in the hand — which corrupts low-level measurements in a way an analog scope will not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup>

## Vertical resolution and acquisition modes

Most digital scopes use an eight-bit converter, and this is the clearest area in which the analog scope retains an advantage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> When a signal with large [[dynamic-range|dynamic range]] is expanded — for example at 500 µV per division on a math trace — the individual quantisation steps of the converter become visible on screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

High-resolution mode, also called boxcar averaging, trades sample rate for effective resolution, yielding nine, ten, twelve or even as much as fourteen bits depending on the sampling settings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> It is distinct from mathematical averaging mode, and both are found under the acquire menu on essentially any modern digital scope alongside normal and peak-detect modes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> High-resolution mode is the more forgiving of the two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> Its absence is a real deficiency in an instrument — a scope offering only regular average mode and no high-resolution mode is limited in what it can clean up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> Where the mode is implemented in software rather than hardware, the waveform update rate suffers noticeably while it is engaged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

## Triggering

[[trigger-holdoff|Trigger holdoff]] works on a digital scope exactly as it does on an analog one, minus the physical retrace period, though there may still be processing time before the instrument can re-arm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> All modern digital scopes provide a holdoff setting in the trigger setup menu; a typical minimum default value is 500 ns, and raising it to roughly 45 µs is what allows a burst waveform with that much dead time between packets to trigger stably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> AC trigger coupling likewise behaves the same on digital instruments as on analog ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-685" title="EEVblog #685 - What Is Oscilloscope AC Trigger Coupling?">685</a></sup> With DC trigger coupling the level has to be reset by hand for every new signal probed; AC trigger coupling allows a single zero-level trigger point to work across a wide range of input signals with differing DC offsets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-685" title="EEVblog #685 - What Is Oscilloscope AC Trigger Coupling?">685</a></sup>

## Bandwidth and front-end response

The classic rise-time relation of 0.35 divided by bandwidth applies to Gaussian-response instruments, which is to say traditional analog CRT scopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Digital scopes do not necessarily have a Gaussian response on their analog input channels; they typically use a maximally flat response, and the applicable constant is somewhat higher — roughly 0.4 divided by bandwidth, with the exact figure depending on the roll-off of the analog front-end filtering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup>

## Construction

The digital front end downstream of the analog input is largely commodity. The HMCAD1511 ADC from Analog Devices appears in a large proportion of scopes on the market, and the manufacturer's typical applications list names digital oscilloscopes directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup> Around the converter, the design is close to off-the-shelf application-note material; what a manufacturer must actually do itself is the analog front end and the [[fpga|FPGA]] work — the phosphor-emulation and sampling technology.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup> Parts such as the 12-bit AD9235, offered in speed grades from 20 up to 65 megasamples per second, are similarly marketed for ultrasound equipment and low-cost digital oscilloscopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup>

## Practical selection

Waveform update rate, expressed in waveforms per second, is a separate axis from responsiveness of the user interface; an instrument can feel fast to operate while being slow in updates per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup> Update rate is what determines whether an infrequent glitch is caught, and this is the case in which the digital instrument's superiority over analog is unambiguous — an analog display cannot accumulate enough photons from a rare event to make it visible, and turning up the intensity to extend persistence only goes so far.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup>

Memory depth on early digital scopes was measured in a couple of kilobytes, which is the main reason vintage digital instruments such as the HP 54600 series are of limited use today.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-809" title="EEVblog #809 - Mailbag">809</a></sup> LeCroy pioneered much of the digital scope art, with large sample memories, fast acquisition rates and high analog bandwidths, alongside Tektronix and Agilent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup> Modern low-end digital scopes offer very large memory by comparison — 14 megapoints as standard on one representative instrument — though a large feature list does not guarantee competence in every function; [[fft|FFT]] performance in particular can be inadequate on scopes that are otherwise strong value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup>

Instrument-to-instrument consistency in the user interface is high enough that familiarity with one digital scope transfers to most others within about ten minutes of use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Update rate and general performance nevertheless vary enormously between models at similar price points, and a sluggish instrument is a poor purchase regardless of its specification sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup> For a given bandwidth requirement such as 1 GHz, second-hand or refurbished instruments offer considerably better value than new ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup>

An analog scope still earns bench space alongside a digital one for its higher update rate, lower noise, and ability to show signals that a low-end digital scope cannot capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-51" title="EEVblog #51 - A tour of the EEVblog Electronics Lab">51</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> Some instruments provide both: the Fluke combiscope switches between analog and digital modes, showing sample points, memory usage and record position when in digital mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup>

The claim that analog scopes are hard to use and digital ones easy does not survive scrutiny — used in manual mode the two are operationally equivalent, and a case can be made that a well-laid-out analog scope is the easier instrument, the auto-set button on modern scopes notwithstanding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> Techniques often assumed to require a digital instrument, such as Bode plotting with a sweep generator, work on an ordinary analog scope as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup>
