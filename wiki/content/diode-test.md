The diode test is a [[multimeter]] function that drives a small [[current]] through a semiconductor junction and displays the resulting forward [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]], typically 0.5 to 0.6 volts for an ordinary silicon [[diode]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Almost every meter on the market carries it, marked with a diode symbol on the [[range-switch|range switch]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Its value in [[troubleshooting]] is that a junction can usually be judged good or bad in seconds, often without unsoldering it, which makes it one of the first checks in any dead-equipment diagnosis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup>

## What the reading means

The function reads the forward drop of a diode in circuit, and the same reading taken with the probes reversed distinguishes a working junction from a failed one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> A dead short inside a semiconductor is a more likely fault than a short inside a [[capacitor]], and diodes fail both open and short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup> A junction that reads identically in both directions is either shorted or is being read through some other path in the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> Beyond plain diodes, the function reads [[transistor]] base-emitter junctions and zeners: a supposed 8.2 V zener reading a normal diode drop one way and its rated [[voltage]] the other confirms the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup>

Because the drop of a [[led|light emitting diode]] is also just a forward voltage, the same range lights LEDs and reads their drop, which is what makes it useful for checking indicator and display parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> A meter with enough headroom and current can light an LED dot matrix display through the probes; one such display measured a 1.8 volt drop, read as 1.85 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-960" title="EEVblog #960 - Mystery Merry Mailbag Teardown">960</a></sup>

## Compliance voltage

The single specification that separates one meter's diode range from another's is the open circuit, or compliance, voltage — the maximum the meter can put across the junction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A meter limited to 2 volts cannot test a modern white LED at all, and this limit is common on otherwise excellent instruments: the Fluke 27's diode range stops at 2 volts, and the Fluke 28 Series II likewise reads only to 2 volts, so a Cree XPG LED that measures 2.477 volts on a Fluke 87 barely comes on and gives no reading on the 28-2.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> The Fluke 17B Max shows the same behaviour, the LED barely lighting and the reading over range, while a companion meter read 2.38 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

Compliance figures across the field span an order of magnitude. A Hioki clamp meter designed for electrical rather than electronics work manages only 1.5 volts from four AA cells, not enough to light even a red LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> A GVDA pocket meter offers 2 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> The Ziboo 17B Pro reaches 2.1 volts from three AA cells, below the 2.3 volts a two-cell Fluke achieves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> The Fluke 101 gives 2.43 volts from two AAA cells, enough to light a white LED dimly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup> The retro Fluke 37 gives 2.5 volts despite a 9 V [[battery]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup> The Uni-T UT71E reaches 2.8 volts, marginal for a white LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup> A $25 meter reached 3.28 volts on fresh AA cells, though that figure falls as the batteries age.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> The Zotek ZT-702S manages 3.2 volts, comfortably turning on a white LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup> The Gossen Metrawatt Xtra reaches roughly 8 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup> The Agilent 34461A raised its predecessor's 1 volt maximum to 5 volts specifically to allow testing of modern LEDs, at the cost that the higher [[open-circuit-voltage|open circuit voltage]] may disturb junctions in existing automated test systems that did not expect it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

[[compliance-voltage|Compliance voltage]] is a direct consequence of the battery topology. A 9 V battery supplies a high diode and ohms test voltage without a DC-DC converter; running from two AA cells forces a converter, with its own [[efficiency]] penalty and shortened [[battery-life|battery life]], as the only route to a higher diode test voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup>

## High-voltage diode ranges

A 15 V diode range, as fitted to the [[121gw|121GW]], extends what the function can do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The extra headroom is not only about reaching higher forward drops — it also delivers more current capability, which is what allows LED displays to be driven visibly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-960" title="EEVblog #960 - Mystery Merry Mailbag Teardown">960</a></sup> It permits reverse breakdown measurement: a transistor's emitter-base breakdown, specified at 6 volts but highly variable in practice, measured 7.7 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup> It reads zeners directly rather than by inference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup> And it reaches parts that lower-compliance meters simply report as open, such as a pair of unusual diodes in a Fluke combiscope that read open on a normal meter but 10.7 volts on a 15 V range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup>

## Test current

Test current is not standardised and differs between meters even where compliance voltages match.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1084" title="EEVblog #1084 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout Part 2">1084</a></sup> A $25 meter delivered roughly 1.6 milliamps, sufficient to light an LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> The DT71 LCR tweezers supply 2 milliamps, enough to light an LED and particularly useful for lighting LEDs already populated on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> A meter drawing 1.7 milliamps on DC volts drew 5 milliamps on the diode range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> A user-selectable test current for the diode range, aimed at LED testing, is a feature worth buying a meter for, and none on the market offers it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup>

## In-circuit measurement

Diodes are among the easiest parts to check in place, and in most cases can be measured without removal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup> The trap is parallel circuitry. A rectifier diode with a 68 ohm [[bleeder-resistor|bleed resistor]] in series with a coil across it presents well under 100 ohms in parallel with the junction regardless of probe polarity, so the meter reads that path rather than the diode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> A reading of about 50 ohms in both directions invites the conclusion that the part is shorted, when in fact the surrounding network must be accounted for before the measurement means anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> Where the in-circuit reading is ambiguous, pulling the part settles it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup>

## Use in fault-finding

On equipment drawing excess current, the diode test is an early step: something is shorted or heavily loaded, and the rectifiers are prime suspects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> Blown rectifier diodes are a classic culprit in switch-mode supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> A standard sequence after a visual inspection for dry joints is to measure every diode on the board before moving to other component classes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup> A short found this way inside a semiconductor is the more probable failure than one inside a capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup>

## Implementation differences

Meters vary in how the function is presented as well as in its specification. Some give no audible or visual indication that a good junction has been found, and no indication of probe polarity, so the operator must track which lead is which.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> The [[agilent-u1272a|Agilent U1272A]] adds an automatic diode mode that displays a good or bad verdict alongside the voltage, using thresholds of 0.3 volts and 0.8 volts, and reports a reversed junction as a negative reading rather than refusing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> The Smart Tweezers [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] shows a diode symbol on its display and flags a short across the probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup> On the Agilent U1253A the continuity beeper also sounds on the diode range, and responds about twice as fast there as on the ohms range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup>

Implementation can also simply be broken: one meter in a $50 shootout could not measure a standard silicon diode at all on its diode range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> On another, the diode reading in circuit failed where a bare junction measured 0.4 volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> A meter with a poorly designed range switch flickers between the ohms and diode positions, a symptom that points at dirty or worn switch contacts rather than at the measurement circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-IoRks5bJw8Y" title="Brymen BM786 Switch Fault Investigation">•</a></sup>

## Safety

Meter manuals prohibit connecting the test leads across a [[voltage-source|voltage source]] while the function switch is in the [[capacitance]], [[resistance]] or diode position, on the grounds that doing so can damage the meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> The countervailing engineering position is that a meter which cannot survive 240 V mains applied to those ranges should not be on the market, particularly a single-switch instrument whose own marketing shows people probing mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> Robust meters do survive it: applying 240 volts to a Fluke 117 makes the diode and capacitance range misbehave visibly while the meter itself comes through undamaged, and other meters recover fully after the same treatment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 2of2">99</a></sup>

## Beyond the handheld multimeter

The function is not confined to bench and field multimeters. It appears on LCR tweezers, where the small probe spacing suits [[surface-mount-technology|surface mount]] parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> It appears on scopemeters and combined scope-meter instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> It appears on bench system multimeters alongside [[thermocouple]] and continuity functions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup> It is fitted to clamp meters intended for industrial electrical work, where its usefulness is questionable given that little on a large site presents a junction worth testing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> Even a wrist-worn meter carries the symbol on its case, in that instance for a function the meter cannot actually perform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1706" title="EEVblog 1706 - K8 Wrist Worn Multimeter REVIEW">1706</a></sup>
