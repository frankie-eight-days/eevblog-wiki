Wave soldering is a bulk [[soldering|soldering process]] in which a populated [[pcb|printed circuit board]] is conveyed over a standing wave of molten solder, so that every joint on the underside of the board is formed in a single pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> It is the mass-production method for through-hole components, and for decades it was also the method for surface-mount parts, which were glued to the underside of the board and dragged through the bath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-907" title="EEVblog #907 - RETRO Teardown: Sony Video Walkman">907</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1033" title="EEVblog #1033 - Sony WatchCam Pocket Flat CRT Monitor Teardown">1033</a></sup> Its economics are unbeatable — one pass solders an entire board face — but it is a far less controlled process than reflow, and almost every characteristic feature of a wave-soldered layout exists to stop the wave from bridging pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

## The process

The board travels on a conveyor through a preheat stage and then over an open bath of molten solder, where a pumped wave rises up to meet the underside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> The bath is genuinely hazardous in operation, since the solder can splash.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> Better machines use multiple waves rather than one: the extra waves prevent shadowing, where a tall through-hole lead or a can-style component blocks solder from reaching what sits behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup> Machine temperature profiles are stored and selected to suit the board's [[thermal-mass]] and component mix, and the machine tracks the board's progress through preheat and over the bath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup> The bath is charged from solder bars, which in modern installations are lead-free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2vJ0c0ioAXY" title="Electronex 2014 - Walkaround">•</a></sup>

On a mixed-technology board the sequence is two-step: the surface-mount side is reflowed first, then the board is passed through the wave to solder the remaining through-hole parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup> Because the wave heats only the individual pins from below rather than the whole component body, through-hole parts see a different thermal exposure from reflowed ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup>

## Glued-down surface mount

Surface-mount parts on the wave side cannot simply be placed; they must be bonded to the board first, or they float off into the solder bath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> Manufacturers dispense dots or lines of adhesive under each part, and this glue is one of the most reliable tells of a wave-soldered board — red or orange blobs visible oozing from beneath resistors, MELF diodes and small ICs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-907" title="EEVblog #907 - RETRO Teardown: Sony Video Walkman">907</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1562" title="EEVblog 1562 - THE 1980&#x27;s Boombox TEARDOWN - Sharp GF-7600">1562</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-378" title="EEVblog #378 - Dumpster Diving Teardown Repair">378</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> The glue pattern is laid down from a glue mask profile, so dots often appear under positions that were later depopulated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1091" title="EEVblog #1091 - LED Studio Light Repair">1091</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1ngqB4mxZOI" title="Smeg Oven Goes Clunkedy Clunk + Capacitor Go Poop">•</a></sup> After gluing, the board is inverted and run over the wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1ngqB4mxZOI" title="Smeg Oven Goes Clunkedy Clunk + Capacitor Go Poop">•</a></sup>

## Solder thieves and pad design

The dominant layout concern is preventing bridges between closely spaced pins as the wave sweeps across them. The standard countermeasure is a solder thieving pad — an oversized pad placed beyond the last pin of a package, which captures the excess solder peeling off the wave and stops it dagging across to the adjacent pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1033" title="EEVblog #1033 - Sony WatchCam Pocket Flat CRT Monitor Teardown">1033</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> These pads go by several names and are covered by established footprint standards for bottom-side wave soldering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup> Since the thief only works on the trailing end, a designer who knows the direction of board travel needs them on one end only; putting them on both ends is insurance against the panel being run the other way round.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

Boards that predate [[solder-mask]] between pins relied entirely on this kind of geometry, and it worked — wave-soldered vintage boards with enormous solder mask expansion and no mask webs between pins show no bridging at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup> Where mask between pins is absent, another defence is to rotate the package: mounting DIPs at 45° to the direction of travel prevents shorts between pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup>

## Orientation and direction of travel

Component orientation relative to the wave is a design decision, not an aesthetic one. ICs should be oriented so the solder drags along the row of pins rather than across it, which is why every chip on a properly laid-out wave-soldered board points the same way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> Passives should sit with their long axis vertical relative to travel; a passive lying the other way forms a cavity behind it as the last of the solder passes, and will not solder as consistently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

Because all of this depends on which way the board goes through the machine, designers mark the direction on the [[silkscreen]] — commonly a large arrow, or the word DIP with an arrow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup> The marker survives on the finished board and makes the process direction readable long afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Even boards that appear to be all surface mount carry the arrow, because their connectors still need the wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup> The convention is followed loosely enough that direction arrows sometimes appear on boards that are double-sided reflow and never see a wave at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1150" title="EEVblog #1150 - Electrostatic Speaker Teardown">1150</a></sup>

Direction is also readable from the joints themselves: the pins at the trailing end of a package carry visibly more solder than those at the leading end, because the leading pins hit the solder first and it drags across.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

## Identifying wave-soldered work

Beyond the glue and the thieving pads, the signature is solder plated all the way up the legs of the parts — evidence a molten bath washed over the whole component, whereas reflow from paste leaves solder only on the pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> Wave joints simply look different from reflow joints, carrying noticeably more solder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup> Wave joints are also uniform across a board, which makes later rework obvious: a repaired chip stands out by its flux residue and mismatched joint appearance against the original wave soldering around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-815" title="EEVBlog #815 - Mailbag Teardown Bonanza">815</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> Parts that would not survive the bath — reed relays, temperature-sensitive supercapacitors, batteries, and anything physically awkward — are excluded from the wave and hand-soldered afterwards, as are ribbon cables and similar assemblies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup> After auto-insertion, through-hole leads are bent over on the underside before soldering, which makes them awkward to clear later with a solder sucker.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup>

Joint quality varies widely between manufacturers. Good wave soldering is clean and consistent even on large power transformers; poor operations produce crusty joints and messy, uneven results.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-449" title="EEVblog #449 - Absopulse VFC500 Variable Frequency Converter Teardown">449</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1278" title="EEVblog #1278 - Mailbag">1278</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1091" title="EEVblog #1091 - LED Studio Light Repair">1091</a></sup> Clearance problems coming out of the machine were historically a routine source of shorts between pins and ground planes on dense boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717ampROkvc" title="PRO TIP: Using a 6.5 Digit Multimeter for finding Shorts">•</a></sup>

## Deliberate tinning

Wave soldering is also exploited as a free way to increase trace [[current]] capacity. Leaving solder mask off a trace lets the wave deposit solder onto the bare copper, thickening the conductor at no extra cost, since the board has to go through the machine anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> Measured against thicker copper — 2 oz instead of 1 oz — which costs real money, the technique is effectively free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup> Controlled measurement puts the improvement at roughly a halving of trace [[resistance]] for a heavy deposit, with lead-free solder giving a comparable result of around 72% reduction at maximum thickness on a 4.2 mm track.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-319" title="EEVblog #319 - Lead Free PCB Tinning">319</a></sup> The gain is real but not dependable: the spread is wide, and a thin coating does very little.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-317" title="EEVblog #317 - PCB Tinning Myth Busting">317</a></sup> The same effect appears unintentionally on older tin-plated boards, where wave flowing over untinned, non-levelled plating leaves an uneven, globby surface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-557" title="EEVblog #557 - Retro Sinclair ZX Spectrum Computer Teardown">557</a></sup>

## Thermal limits and process choice

The wave imposes its own constraints on part selection. Through-hole connectors are frequently specified as wave solder capable with no reflow rating stated at all; running such a part through a reflow oven instead — paste-in-hole — melts the plastic body, while the surrounding surface-mount parts survive unharmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup> Connectors intended for paste-in-hole use high-temperature thermoplastics specifically so they can take the full reflow profile.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup> Layout rules interact with the process too: solder mask expansion between pads must leave four or five thou of mask, or solder will bridge during hand or wave soldering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Where a section of board must be kept clear of solder entirely, peelable solder mask can be specified to protect it through wave and reflow, then removed afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup>

For surface-mount work the process has been almost entirely displaced by reflow, which is far more controlled and produces much cleaner joints; passing a bath of molten solder across an entire populated board face, with every part individually glued, is a comparatively crude way to build.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> Wave soldering persists where through-hole content remains, which on double-sided-loaded consumer and industrial boards is still routine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-856" title="EEVblog #856 - Mailbag">856</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup>
