Output ripple is the periodic AC component that rides on the DC output of a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]], charge pump, or regulated supply. A DC-to-DC converter does not produce a flat line at its nominal [[voltage]]; connecting an [[oscilloscope]] to the output shows a repeating charge-and-discharge [[waveform]] at the switching rate rather than a steady level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> Because [[ripple]] is a designed-in quantity rather than an accident, it is chosen as a specification at the start of a converter design and then used to size the output components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

## Where it comes from

In a switching converter the [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] is charged during the switch-on interval and discharged into the load between pulses, and the resulting sawtooth is the ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> The same mechanism is visible directly on a [[microcontroller]]-driven charge-pump [[inverter]], where the [[capacitor]] charging and then discharging can be watched on the output waveform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> Ripple amplitude therefore depends on the [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] and on the capacitor values: raising the [[frequency]], raising the [[capacitance]], or both produces a smoother output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> Driving such an inverter at 10 kHz can remove visible ripple entirely, while dropping to 1 kHz into the same load makes it severe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup>

Because switching frequency is what sets the component values, converter chips that look interchangeable are not. Substituting a different brand of switcher generally still functions, but the [[inductor]] and capacitor values chosen for one part may give more ripple on the output with another, along with operation at a different point on the [[efficiency]] curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup>

## Sizing the output capacitor

Ripple enters the design as a target. In a 5 V to 15 V, 100 mA boost design the accepted figure was 100 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> The output filter capacitor follows from nine times the [[output-current|output current]] multiplied by the on-time, divided by the allowed ripple; with 100 mA, an on-time of 7.3 µs and 0.1 V of ripple, the result is 66 µF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> The measured result at no load was roughly 200 mV peak against a 100 mV target, and the ripple changed as load was dialled in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

Where the calculated capacitor is not enough, an additional LC section on the output is the standard remedy: in the same design a second 33 µH inductor and a further 100 µF capacitor were added as an optional output filter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> On a bench supply, bolting a larger capacitor across the output likewise cut the low-frequency ripple from a couple of hundred millivolts peak-to-peak toward the 50 mV specification, though the higher-frequency switching content survived the treatment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> High-frequency spikes are a separate problem from low-frequency ripple and respond to different measures; on an isolated [[usb|USB]] supply, an RFI suppression capacitor of 1 nF between primary and secondary knocked down part of the [[switching-noise|switching noise]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

## The ESR floor

Capacitance cannot be increased without limit. Beyond a certain point the [[equivalent-series-resistance|equivalent series resistance]] of the capacitor dominates, and since a higher capacitance in a given package size implies a higher ESR, adding capacitance stops reducing ripple and eventually becomes counterproductive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> There is consequently a minimum ripple that filtering alone can reach, and fitting a thousand microfarads does not drive ripple to zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Where a lower figure is genuinely required, the answer is post-regulation — a low-dropout regulator on the output, a Zener, or an RC or LC filter stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

The ESR relationship also runs in the other direction as a diagnostic. Aged output capacitors that have gone high-ESR produce excess ripple on the output even under light load, and that excess ripple can be enough to trigger hiccup mode in the supply's controller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup>

## Load, temperature and mode dependence

Ripple is not a single number. It scales with output current, so a quoted figure is meaningful only against a stated load, usually full load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> [[datasheet|Datasheet]] curves carry the same caveat: a graph showing output ripple versus input voltage may omit the output current it was taken at, and the plotted figures assume particular capacitor values — for charge pumps typically a nominal 10 µF output capacitor and 10 µF switching capacitor — so the ESR of the actual capacitor used changes the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Reading a peak-to-peak figure off such a graph and assuming the chip will deliver it is a trap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

Curve families plotted against [[ambient-temperature|ambient temperature]] show the ripple at a fixed load [[current]] climbing steeply as temperature moves, so a design verified at one temperature and one operating current can behave very differently at another — a particular hazard for circuits with two distinct current modes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

The sharpest discontinuities come from converters that change operating mode. A traditional 7660-style charge pump has a single mode of operation and its ripple rises roughly linearly with output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Parts with a light-load pulse-frequency-modulation mode do not: the LM2776 allows the charge pump to switch less below 40 mA of output current to cut quiescent draw, and at that mode boundary the output ripple can rise by a factor of four or five, from around 20 mV to 80 or 100 mV peak-to-peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> A step from 80 mV to 120 mV may be tolerable in a design that post-regulates the rail, but the transition is abrupt and appears exactly where light-load operation is expected to be benign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

Frequency boost options work the other way. Running a 7660-class inverter at a boosted switching frequency can give around 50 mV peak-to-peak at 10 mA output where the plain ICL7660 gives about 200 mV; a 2 MHz switching frequency in this family implies low output ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> At the low end of the range, capacitive inverters can reach figures an external LDO would struggle to match: under 1 millivolt in one case, and 4 mV peak-to-peak for a small negative-bias generator running from a 3 to 5 V supply and delivering perhaps 10 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

## Measurement and specification

Ripple is measured AC-coupled at the output, typically at 100 mV or 50 mV per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> Both peak-to-peak and RMS figures are used and they are not interchangeable: one bench supply showed 78 mV peak-to-peak alongside 6 mV RMS, still half its specified figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Manufacturers usually bound the measurement [[bandwidth]], a variable-frequency converter specifying less than 500 mV RMS over a 20 Hz to 20 MHz bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-449" title="EEVblog #449 - Absopulse VFC500 Variable Frequency Converter Teardown">449</a></sup> On a well-behaved switching supply the low-frequency ripple can be essentially absent, leaving only content at the switching frequency itself, 120 kHz in one 360 W bench supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Quoted figures span a wide range by class: less than 50 mV at full load on a 2000 W supply,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> 100 mV on a low-cost 360 W bench unit,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> less than 75 mV peak-to-peak of ripple and [[noise]] at a rated 400 mA on a small isolated DC-DC module,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> and just under 500 µV AC RMS on a 100 W USB-C supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> Oscilloscope power-analysis packages treat output ripple as one of a standard set of converter measurements alongside efficiency, inrush current, switching loss, transient response and PSRR,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup> and a full characterisation of a converter brick means sweeping ripple across the whole input-voltage and output-current envelope rather than measuring it at one operating point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

Ripple also rises sharply when a converter is pushed past its rating: a 2 W isolated module run at 3 W kept regulating and restarted cleanly, but its output ripple increased drastically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

## Ripple as a symptom

A low ripple figure is not on its own evidence of a healthy output. Adding [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] to a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] whose error amplifier was oscillating reduced the visible variation to roughly half a millivolt, from about 370 mV to 368 mV, while the op-amp continued to oscillate underneath — the capacitance hid the symptom rather than fixing the instability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup> Similarly, an offline [[led|LED]] driver with a 330 µF output capacitor showed clean output ripple while drawing 24 W at a [[power-factor|power factor]] of 0.58, a defect the ripple measurement gave no hint of.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup>
