Power dissipation is the electrical energy a component converts into heat rather than delivering onward as useful output. It is the quantity that decides package size, [[heat-sink]] area, [[transformer]] tapping, and whether a design survives its own worst case, because every watt dissipated raises a junction temperature somewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Nothing about the conversion is optional: put an AC [[waveform]] into a [[resistor]] and power comes out as heat regardless of which way the [[current]] is flowing at any instant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup>

## Calculating it

For a series pass element the dissipation is simply the [[voltage]] across the device multiplied by the current through it. In a [[mosfet]] constant-current load, the power in the heat sink is the input voltage minus the voltage across the sense resistor, times the load current, because no current flows into the gate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> With 12 V in, 1 V across a 1 Ω sense resistor and 1 A flowing, that is 11 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> A [[linear-regulator]] dropping 12 V to 5 V at 0.7 A dissipates 7 V times 0.7 A, near enough to 5 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

For resistive elements the I²R form is more convenient. A MOSFET with 24 mΩ typical on-[[resistance]] carrying 3 A dissipates about 0.2 W; one at 30 mΩ carrying 5 A dissipates 0.75 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> A 75 kΩ 2 W dropper resistor in series with an [[led]] can only ever pass about 5 mA before it reaches its own rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-435" title="EEVblog #435 - 3D Rocker Teardown">435</a></sup> A 1 A constant current through a 5 Ω resistor is 5 W, which is why a decade box that reaches 5 Ω with a single resistor instead of five in series has a much worse worst case in that position than in the first four.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1586" title="EEVblog 1586 -  Stranger Things Decade Resistor Box + Repair">1586</a></sup>

Average current is the wrong measurement for AC work. Power is dissipated in the resistor on both the positive and the negative half of the waveform, but the average current over a symmetric waveform is zero, and squaring zero gives zero power, which is plainly wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> RMS is required. Some older instruments sidestepped the mathematics entirely by physically measuring the heat produced in a resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> Apparent power and real power also diverge: real power is the actual heat dissipated in the components, and the difference between two candidate designs on that basis can run to three orders of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

Simulation and datasheets both help, with caveats. A DC operating point analysis in LTspice holds every node result in memory, so hovering over a resistor reports its current and its power dissipation directly — 333 µA and 111 µW for one part in an example network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-516" title="EEVblog #516 - LTSPICE Tutorial - DC Operating Point Analysis">516</a></sup> [[datasheet]] power figures, on the other hand, are quoted under specific conditions, and front-page numbers such as a 2 µW flash-freeze consumption apply only to the supply configuration and mode listed in the fine print.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

## Package and thermal limits

A package can only shed so much heat. A SOT-23 is rated in the region of a fifth of a watt at room temperature for some parts and an absolute maximum of about 1 W for others; a watt in a SOT-23 will get distinctly warm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Small-signal bipolars in that package run to a couple of hundred milliwatts, though a higher-grade SOT-23 part such as the FMMT619 is specified at 625 mW alongside a sub-200 mV saturation voltage at 1 A and 68 mΩ of on-resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup> Moving up to SO-8 buys more surface area, lower on-resistance and correspondingly greater dissipation capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> A TO-220 with no heat sink at all manages around 3.8 W, and far more once it is bolted down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

[[thermal-resistance]] converts watts into degrees. A plastic DIP has a junction-to-case figure of 45 °C/W, so a chip dissipating 1 W sits 45 °C above its own case top — a device measuring 45 °C on the outside has silicon at roughly 90 °C inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> This is why 0.3 W, the quiescent dissipation of a 10 mA part running from 30 V, is genuinely significant in a DIP-8 rather than negligible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup> Resistors carry the same constraint: a half-watt part has a published curve of temperature rise above ambient versus power dissipated, and there is no way to make it dissipate half a watt without its temperature rising.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Above about 70 °C the rating derates linearly to zero at the part's maximum temperature, 155 °C in one example, at which point no power at all may be dissipated in it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup>

Heat sinks are characterised the same way. A small clip-on sink of the sort fitted to a switching regulator module runs around 6 °C/W, so a claimed capability of 52 W up to 97.5 W from such a sink does not withstand inspection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> In a bench measurement, 10 W into a power MOSFET on its heat sink raised the sink 49.5 °C above ambient by calculation;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> the same 10 W with forced air held the junction at 78 °C, while sealing the assembly into an unventilated box drove it to 96.7 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> When a datasheet graph of mounting-surface temperature rise runs off the top of the page at the power in question, that is the design failing rather than the graph: 5 W into a sink of the size fitted to one bench supply was already off-scale.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup>

## Linear regulation

A linear regulator dissipates continuously by construction. Unlike a switching element, which is either off or fully saturated and drops little either way, a linear pass [[transistor]] sits in a continuous control loop and is always burning the difference between input and output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> LDOs add a second mechanism: ground current that scales with load current and can itself waste appreciable power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Running a 3.3 V project directly from a 12 V [[battery]] pack through a linear regulator throws a large fraction of the battery's capacity away as heat in the heat sink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-258" title="EEVblog #258 - PSU Housing Design - Part 11">258</a></sup>

The worst case for a bench linear supply is maximum current at minimum output voltage. A 30 V 3 A supply set to 1 V from a 30 V rail must drop essentially the whole 30 V at 3 A in the pass transistor and heat sink,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> and the standard remedy is multiple secondary taps on the transformer switched by relays as the output voltage is turned up, which is what lets an apparently undersized heat sink serve a 30 V 5 A supply or a 150 W-per-channel triple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Tap switching does not eliminate the problem, only bound it — one supply still faces a 15 V drop at the worst point between taps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Deliberately setting a supply to about 1 V and shorting the output is therefore the natural worst-case power dissipation test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> An alternative is a tracking pre-regulator, which holds a fixed and therefore known drop across the pass transistor: at most about 2 V at 1 A is 2 W, which a very low-profile heat sink can handle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

Getting the input rail wrong pushes the pass devices past their design point. Supplies built for a 220 V mains and run on 240 V put the extra 20 V across the regulator transistors, dissipating substantially more power, and if the transistors are not up to it they fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gqzZHbEfWDU" title="KoradFollowup Charles">•</a></sup> The same reasoning works in reverse as a diagnostic: a bench supply drawing 0.7 A and 27 W with nothing connected to its output has 27 W going somewhere it should not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup>

## Switching converters and efficiency

In a switcher, dissipation is the arithmetic complement of [[efficiency]], and at high power the residual is large. A 3,000 W supply at 92 % efficiency has 240 W to get rid of inside the case, spread across the heat sink, the inductors and everything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> A 450 W unit cannot be built at 90 % efficiency, because that leaves 45 W inside a small enclosure with very little heat sinking; such parts have to be far more efficient, and even 99 % leaves 4.5 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> A 360 W bench supply at 86.8 % dissipates about 9 W and stays cool to the touch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> A small [[boost-converter]] delivering 50 W from a 56 W input is dissipating 6 W, rising to 7.6 W at 60 W out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup>

Efficiency is not constant across the load range, and the dissipation curve is what matters practically. A surface-mount power brick dissipates around 200 mW at its 100 mA rated output, and the loss climbs steeply beyond that current — which is precisely why it is not rated any higher.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> A wide-range adjustable module shows the same tension: at low output current the efficiency is poor but the absolute loss is small, so it does not matter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> while at 5 V and 3 A from a 30 V input it reaches about 4.5 W of dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> The extreme case is a module delivering 1.5 V at 3 A — only 4.5 W of output — while dissipating 3.8 W itself, an inevitable consequence of asking one converter to cover a full output range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> Quiescent loss belongs in that total: a 0.39 W standing draw is not dissipated in the output transistor, [[diode]] or [[inductor]], but it still heats the module and so belongs in a module power loss figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

A shorted output is the pathological case. A converter delivering 5 V at 0.4 A into a short is dissipating the full 2 W internally instead of the 20 % of it that it would normally lose, and the package reaches close to 100 °C while surviving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

## Sense resistors, shunts and dropper resistors

[[current-sensing]] costs power. Traditional current shunts in a three-phase meter would dissipate up to a couple of watts each and heat the enclosure, which is the reason for using current transformers instead — they draw only what the measurement circuitry needs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> A 10 mΩ shunt at 20 A dissipates 4 W, which drives the sizing of the heat sink attached to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> A 1 Ω shunt drops 1 V at the 1 A maximum output of a regulator and dissipates that power, tolerable in a small supply but not free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> At 250 mA the same 1 Ω resistor reaches a quarter of a watt, the limit of a quarter-watt part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

Fault conditions dictate the physical size. Splitting a sense element into four paralleled resistors gives margin in case range-switching software turns the wrong transistor off and forces the full 2 A through them, which would otherwise be 4 W into a single small part and burn it out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> A motor current-sense resistor is given a larger physical package than an 0805 for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> Sometimes the correction is made late: a resistor left standing proud of the board for dissipation reasons indicates a footprint chosen without the thermal case in mind.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-355" title="EEVblog #355 - Dictaphone Dumpster Diving">355</a></sup>

## Protection and clamp circuits

Protection networks are transient dissipators. In a [[multimeter]] input, a high-wattage wire-wound high-voltage resistor in series with the MOV must absorb the energy alongside it, because the MOV does not magically make it disappear; the resistor has to have adequate power dissipation for the interval before the PTC rises into the megohms and stops the current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The self-limiting behaviour of PTCs is exploited directly for [[capacitor]] discharge: plain 1.5 kΩ resistors across a 240 V bank would burn up quickly, whereas PTCs increase in resistance as the dissipation inside them heats them, so they self-regulate and protect themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup> Transistor zener clamps are paralleled for extra power dissipation where a single pair is not enough, as in the 1000 V CAT III input of one meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1157" title="EEVblog #1157 - Transistor Zener Clamp Circuit">1157</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1000" title="EEVblog #1000 - Fundamental Mailbag Retro Teardown Shootouts are Bullshit">1000</a></sup>

A zener used as a regulator must be checked against its own rating each time the operating point moves. A 5.1 V zener passing 50 mA dissipates a quarter of a watt, comfortably inside a 1 W part; raise the current to 100 mA and it is half a watt, still inside but no longer with much room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Zeners are used as power devices, so they heat up, and their junction temperature shifts their dynamic resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> At low load currents they are very inefficient, taking 50 to 70 mA of [[quiescent-current]] where a 7805 takes almost none.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Replacing a mains-fed zener-and-dropper arrangement with a rectifier and regulator can reduce the wasted power by orders of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

## Layout and heat spreading

Copper geometry affects dissipation without affecting conductor resistance. Whether a trace is on an external or internal layer, and whether a plane is present, changes the power dissipation figures while leaving the conductor resistance the same.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> [[via]] stitching under a large package moves heat between the layers and ground planes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup> Dave Jones sets trace and via calculators to a 1 °C temperature rise rather than the customary 10 °C default as a conservative habit, on the grounds that almost no power should be dissipated in a via at all: a 1 mm hole at 1 °C rise carries 0.4 mW, and accepting 10 °C only buys 4 mW.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Surface-mount packages can be made to dissipate real power through thermal vias, surface-mount heat sinks and transfer blocks, but the calculation has to account for the via thermal resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Mechanical detail follows the same logic — ridges machined into a small circular heat sink exist to increase surface area for dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup>

## Where the heat ends up

Identifying the dissipating element is a standard diagnostic step, because the heat has to be somewhere. A power amplifier stage dissipating 10 W will warm its heat sink; 10 W cannot be dissipated in a capacitor without the capacitor exploding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup> A power resistor is a power resistor because it dissipates a lot of power, and things that dissipate a lot of power can go open circuit under overload or stress, which makes them cheap and worthwhile to check first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> A resistor that had been heating up and was clearly dissipating a fair bit of power points to a design choice made without enough thought about the current through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> When a resistor on the primary side fails because the secondary drew more than it could handle, the resistor is the symptom rather than the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> Where power is not shared equally between paralleled resistors — 5 % tolerance parts are not matched — the lowest-value ones take the brunt and fail first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-SoeVNaz7KGc" title="EEVsmoke #2 - Magic Smoke Captured!">•</a></sup> Dissipating something on the order of a kilowatt instantaneously in a small package blows the case apart, and heat sinking does nothing for that failure mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-SoeVNaz7KGc" title="EEVsmoke #2 - Magic Smoke Captured!">•</a></sup>

The absence of a hot spot is equally informative. An instrument drawing 11 A on a 3.3 V rail that should draw six, with a thermal camera finding no localised hot spot, points to the power being spread uniformly across four ASICs — and to an unrepairable fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

Capacitors dissipate too. Ripple current through the [[equivalent-series-resistance]] of an aluminium electrolytic heats the capacitor internally; an ideal capacitor would dissipate nothing, but real ones always do, and in high-power products the resulting internal heating shortens their life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

## Dissipation as a design constraint

Where the heat must go often shapes the architecture more than the electrical function does. In a source measure unit, a large N-channel power MOSFET dissipates the whole output power, or a quarter of it where four such sections are paralleled;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> devices used purely as switches nearby have visibly less heat sinking and no thermal vias under them, because slamming a device hard on and off does not dissipate much.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Another SMU brings out ±36 V taps in addition to its ±225 V rails, so that generating 1 V or 10 V on a low range does not force the output stage to dissipate an enormous amount of power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup>

At the extreme, a 121-chip processor module with each chip dissipating up to 10 W has a maximum module dissipation of 600 W, with cooling infrastructure designed around that figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup> A plasma television is specified at 570 W maximum total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup> Conversely, a large forward drop can dominate a small circuit: a diode with a 1.4 V typical forward voltage at 40 A means 56 W to dissipate, which is why it is mounted on a substantial block.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-592" title="EEVblog #592 - Mailbag">592</a></sup> An [[electronic-load]] handles 25 W passively and up to 100 W continuous with a fan added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-749" title="EEVblog #749 - Mailbag">749</a></sup>

Where dissipation is genuinely low, the design can be simple: a CMOS-based circuit drawing almost nothing makes the choice of linear regulator a non-issue,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> and a driver transistor that never gets warm has no power dissipation problem to solve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> The converse is a warning sign — a free-standing regulator with no heat sink and no [[pcb]] provision for one is only viable if it really is not dissipating much power, and clearly is not when it overheats quickly in service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup>
