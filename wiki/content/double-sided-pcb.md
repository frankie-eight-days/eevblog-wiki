A double-sided [[pcb|PCB]], also called a two-layer board, carries copper on both faces of the substrate with no internal layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> It is the workhorse construction for most low- and medium-complexity electronics: cheap enough to be near-free from prototype houses, dense enough to route a complete microcomputer, and limited enough that its shortcomings — no dedicated [[ground-plane|ground plane]], no buried power distribution — define a whole body of layout practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> The term is ambiguous in one important respect: it can mean two copper layers, or it can mean components loaded on both faces, and these are independent choices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup>

## Construction and the plated-through-hole distinction

The defining feature is copper on top and bottom, with the two sides joined electrically. Modern boards use plated through holes, so a hole doubles as a layer-to-layer connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1705" title="EEVblog 1705 - World&#x27;s First Desktop Personal Computer: Olivetti Programma 101">1705</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> Older and cheaper boards frequently skipped the plating: the board was etched on both sides but the vias were formed by hand, with a wire link soldered on both faces, or by a component lead soldered top and bottom to serve as the interlayer join.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> One keyboard board used exactly this compromise, taking the double-sided process purely to get a ground plane on the top face while declining to pay for through-hole plating, so every layer transition became a soldered link.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup> A related trick is a board that is single-sided in the true sense but built up with a second, separately applied copper layer for jumpers, bridged and masked as an extra process step rather than drilled and plated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-541" title="EEVblog #541 - Mailbag">541</a></sup>

Board thickness is a design variable rather than a constant. Standard stock is 1.6 mm, but thin two-layer boards down to 0.8 mm or 0.5 mm are used where the finished rigidity requirement permits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-243" title="EEVblog #243 - Vintage Brick Mobile Phone Teardown">243</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-130" title="EEVblog #130 - The µCalc Credit Card Scientific Calculator / Computer">130</a></sup> Etching resolution for home-made double-sided boards using pre-sensitized photoresist and a transparency overlay reached roughly 8 thou track and 8 thou spacing, and turnaround could be an hour or two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1197" title="EEVblog #1197 - DIY PCB Etching With Water">1197</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Commercial fabrication of a double-sided board can be turned around in 24 hours, against 48 hours for a four-layer and about six working days for an eight-layer board, most of that engineering and tooling rather than processing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup> Milling machines can produce double-sided boards without plated through holes or [[solder-mask|solder mask]], which makes assembly harder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1565" title="EEVblog 1565 - UNSW Electronics Lab &amp; Makerspace Tour">1565</a></sup>

## Orthogonal routing

The dominant layout convention for two-layer boards is to dedicate each layer to one routing direction: all traces predominantly horizontal on one side, predominantly vertical on the other, with vias handling the turns.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> This is what makes a large, densely populated digital board routable on two layers at all; attempting it without a directional discipline collapses quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup> The pattern is visible in essentially every classic through-hole digital board — the Apple 1, the Amiga 500, the TRS-80 Model I, the Gigatron TTL computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-911" title="EEVblog #911 - 2016 Sydney Maker Faire Part 2">911</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Autorouting algorithms are built around it, and CAD packages expose the per-layer preferred direction as a setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup>

Layer count changes the character of the problem entirely. With eight layers, routing options open up dramatically; on two layers, the constraint is severe enough that resistors are deliberately used as jumpers, letting a single component hop four, five, or six traces at once and materially improving routing density.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> A working goal on a two-layer layout is to route as much as possible on the top layer before resorting to the bottom, keeping the second layer free for the connections that genuinely need it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Some two-layer designs come so close to being single-sided that the difference amounts to a dozen jumper links.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

For fine-pitch [[bga|BGA]] parts the layer count determines whether a fan-out is even possible. A 36-pin BGA with only two rings of pads can be completely fanned out on one layer, which is what allows it onto a double-sided board rather than forcing a multilayer stackup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

## Autorouters on two layers

Automatic routers perform poorly on simple double-sided boards, and the simpler the board, the worse the result tends to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-93" title="EEVblog #93 - PCB Autorouters Suck">93</a></sup> A fairly average double-sided board of modest complexity, with a few awkward cross-routing situations, defeated a commercial autorouter in direct comparison with a manual layout of the same design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup> A dedicated AI routing service, whose beta supported two layers, produced output that failed the basic orthogonal-direction rule that governs double-sided routing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup> Autorouters are capable tools under heavily constrained setups — potentially a week of constraint definition before the button is pressed — but they are not a substitute for placement and manual routing judgment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1535" title="EEVblog 1535 - DeepPCB AI AutoRouting FAIL!">1535</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-975" title="EEVblog #975 - Human vs Autorouter">975</a></sup>

## Electrical limitations

The central weakness of a double-sided board is that neither layer can be given over entirely to a solid, low-[[impedance]], low-[[inductance]] ground plane once signals must be routed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> Both faces are signal layers with [[ground]] reduced to flood fill and occasional stitching, so return-[[current]] loop areas cannot be kept tight across a whole board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> With two dozen chips spread over a large board it is not possible for every trace to have a short loop area, and loop area is the governing quantity for radiated emissions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup>

Measured against an electrically identical four-layer version of the same layout, a two-layer Gigatron board showed roughly 15 dB more broadband [[noise]] on near-field probing, and would perform worse in a full compliance test across the spectrum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The blunt summary of two-layer EMC behaviour is that it radiates like buggery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Bypass capacitors cannot repair this: switching artefacts traceable to a shared ground return are a structural property of the board, not a decoupling deficiency, and would be largely suppressed by stitching the pins together on a four-layer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

This does not make two-layer boards unusable. Careful — and somewhat lucky — layout can approach four-layer performance on two layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The useful practices are keeping ground and power traces stacked directly over one another wherever possible, not splitting the ground, and flood-filling ground as the default rather than star-pointing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> On a two-layer board the VCC track to a [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] should be routed as a priority, because there is no plane to fall back on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup> Where a big ground plane is wanted under a chip, a double-sided board allows planes on both faces to be strapped together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-23" title="EEVblog #23 - GSM mobile phone audio design">23</a></sup> [[via-stitching|Via stitching]] between the two layers is also used for current handling: for high-current traces, doubling the copper across both layers helps, and although stitching does little for [[resistance]] it does assist thermal dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup>

There are designs where two layers simply will not do. Fast ECL logic drawing large switching currents relies on the power and ground planes acting as one distributed low-inductance [[capacitor]]; such a circuit would almost certainly not work on a two-layer board because the edges are too fast and the current gulps too large.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup> Compliance requirements have historically forced the same conclusion: an early portable computer built on a double-sided board with no substantial ground plane was re-spun as a multilayer design to meet FCC limits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup>

## The cost boundary against single-sided

Single-sided board remains cheaper in production, and the gap is large enough that high-volume consumer products go to remarkable lengths to stay on one layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LAbWsqS51Tk" title="When you REALLY don&#x27;t want to pay for a Double Sided PCB">•</a></sup> Zero-ohm jumpers are the usual instrument, and one speaker board used what amounted to a whole reel of them: "I have never seen so many zero-ohm jumpers before in my life."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LAbWsqS51Tk" title="When you REALLY don&#x27;t want to pay for a Double Sided PCB">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> The same calculation appears with hand-fitted jumper wires on a single-sided consumer product, and with a run of wire links added because a decent ground could not be got across the layout — extra assembly labour judged cheaper than a double-sided layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup> A keyboard design avoided the double-sided board by moving to a carbon process, which also removed the gold flash and discrete resistors from the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1108" title="EEVblog #1108 - Casio CT-X700 Keyboard Teardown">1108</a></sup> Going double-sided also costs more than copper alone: on a thermal design it brings [[via]] holes, thermal bonding to a [[heat-sink|heatsink]], and the associated process steps, all of which can be avoided if a single-sided pad is adequate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup>

Counter-intuitively, a single-layer board is sometimes quoted higher than two layers, because the manufacturer's stock material is double-sided and the unwanted copper must be etched away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> At prototype quantities the cost of two layers has collapsed to the point where a professional double-sided board with solder mask and [[silkscreen]] costs a few dollars, against roughly $500 for a single board when a full panel had to be purchased.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9iXFhKUa1BU" title="eevBLAB #2 - Are Electronics Hobbyists Useless?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mW-1FMGpsFg" title="UNSW Talk - Part 2 - Job Tip, What I hated about university EE, Contracting + more">•</a></sup> The standard prototype order is a two-layer board with top and bottom copper, both [[solder]] masks and both silkscreen overlays, with no paste stencil when assembly is by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup> Two-layer flex is fabricated the same routine way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>

Layer count also propagates back into component selection. Placing an [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]] chip directly against the display pins can allow a two-layer board where a four-layer board would otherwise be needed, and at consumer volumes that difference in cents matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup>

## Double-sided component loading

Separately from copper layers, "double-sided load" means components populated on both faces. It is a near-universal consequence of small form factors: a smartwatch, a compact audio interface, a densely packed test instrument, an [[oscilloscope]] module board with bypass caps that must sit directly under the BGA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-342" title="EEVblog #342 - Agilent 90000 Oscilloscope Teardown">342</a></sup> The usual pattern is active devices on top and passives on the bottom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup> The trade-offs are additional assembly cost and thickness — laying components on both sides of a board adds on the order of 30 to 40 percent to the assembled thickness, which was one of the compromises separating an early analogue mobile phone from a later single-sided-load design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-243" title="EEVblog #243 - Vintage Brick Mobile Phone Teardown">243</a></sup>

For that reason, single-sided loading is often specified deliberately. Professional equipment intended to be serviced may forbid chips on the bottom, allowing passives only, which constrains placement severely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup> A board that must sit flat in its case is easier to design with everything on top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-833" title="EEVblog #833 - Mailbag">833</a></sup> Where a heatsinked surface-mount part could be handled by flipping the board over, the cost of doing so is precisely that it forces a double-sided load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Cost comparisons between competing instruments turn on this too: a single-sided-load design has lower construction cost even where its components are individually more expensive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup>

## Assembly, rework and repair

On double-sided through-hole boards the solder should wick completely through the plated hole and appear on the opposite face; that through-wicking is the mark of a sound joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Even a very small solder feed of 0.46 mm wire is enough to achieve it on a normal DIP pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> A low-power iron can manage a DIP package on a double-sided board while failing entirely on a ground plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup>

[[desoldering|Desoldering]] effort scales with copper mass. Two layers of ground fill around a power package already represent substantial [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] on both faces, and hot-air rework must supply enough energy to heat all of it; a [[multilayer-pcb|multilayer board]] connected to internal planes is considerably worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Desoldering stations need noticeably longer dwell on multilayer boards than on a double-layer board, and may require raising the tip temperature from 350 °C to around 380 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup>

## Historical prevalence

Double-sided construction with no internal planes was the standard for late-1970s and early-1980s computing and instrumentation, and the layouts achieved on it are often remarkable given the constraint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1021" title="EEVblog #1021 - Retro Radio Shack TRS-80 Color Computer">1021</a></sup> Examples run from the TRS-80 Model 100 [[processor]] board and the TRS-80 Color Computer through the Cambridge Z88, the Sinclair flat-screen pocket television, the Apple IIc, the Olivetti Programma 101, and Intel's 8085 design kit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1021" title="EEVblog #1021 - Retro Radio Shack TRS-80 Color Computer">1021</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-554" title="EEVblog #554 - Sinclair FTV1 TV80 Flat Screen Pocket TV Teardown">554</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-788" title="EEVblog #788 - Apple IIC Teardown">788</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1705" title="EEVblog 1705 - World&#x27;s First Desktop Personal Computer: Olivetti Programma 101">1705</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup> Older instrument boards show tin roll-plated traces applied before solder mask, producing the characteristic crinkled raised tracks, or the classic green crinkly solder mask finish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 -
