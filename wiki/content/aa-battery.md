The AA cell is the default primary cell of portable electronics: a 1.5 V nominal cylindrical cell whose energy content, roughly 2.2 watt hours for an alkaline, sets the achievable runtime of an enormous range of [[battery]]-powered products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> Its significance in design is less about the chemistry than about the volume: for a given enclosure, choosing AA over AAA or a 9 V battery is usually the single decision that determines whether a product runs for tens of hours or hundreds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> Internally it is the same electrochemical stack as an AAA, C, D or 4A cell — a central negative conductor rod surrounded by the anode, a separator, then the [[cathode]] and the positive terminal — differing only in the diameter of the rod and the quantity of material.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-518" title="EEVblog #518 - 9V Alkaline Battery Construction">518</a></sup>

## Capacity and discharge behaviour

The milliamp-hour figure printed on a cell is not a fixed quantity but a function of discharge [[current]]. At 25 milliamps a AA might deliver a nominal 2,800 mAh; taken to 500 milliamps the same cell yields only about 1,200 mAh.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> Shelf life degrades the figure further: after seven years from date of manufacture at 21 °C a cell retains around 80 % of its initial capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup> An alkaline AA is conventionally taken as about 2,000 mAh for a first-pass design estimate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

The discharge curve has a characteristic shape — a sharpish drop at the start, a long gradual decline, then a tail-off at the end — which is what a battery-emulation [[power-supply|power supply]] must reproduce to stand in for a real cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y2rcx4vKxlc" title="Unboxing the Keysight E36731A DC Power Supply, Electronic Load, and Battery Emulator">•</a></sup> Manufacturer characteristic curves stop at 0.8 volts, the industry-standard cut-off, on the general agreement that essentially nothing useful remains below it; the amount of energy actually left under 0.8 volts is poorly documented and requires dedicated long-term discharge testing to establish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

Internal [[resistance]] sets a practical ceiling on current. Discharging a AA much above one amp is counterproductive because ESR losses dominate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hSkaZEgrZkY" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 3">•</a></sup> At high currents the endpoint also shifts: a pair discharged to 1.4 volts across the terminals — nominally 0.7 volts per cell, apparently a deep discharge — may still hold something like 5 to 10 % of capacity, because less total capacity was extractable at that rate in the first place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hSkaZEgrZkY" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 3">•</a></sup>

Lithium primary variants change the arithmetic substantially. A lithium thionyl chloride AA of 2,600 mAh feeding a 1.5 mA load gives roughly 1,700 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1371" title="EEVblog #1371 - A 10,000hr Battery Life Bench Multimeter?">1371</a></sup> Rechargeable NiMH AAs are commonly marked 2,100 to 2,300 mAh, with low-self-discharge types retaining on the order of 80 % of charge after twelve months.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-35" title="EEVblog #35 - Inside the Varta 15 minute NiMH Battery Charger">35</a></sup>

## AA versus the 9 V battery

The 9 V battery is not a distinct technology but six 4A cells in series inside a wrapper, a size sold individually but hard to obtain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> The consequence is poor volumetric energy density: a rechargeable 9 V rated 300 mAh at 7.2 V stores only about 2 watt hours, less than a couple of AA cells, and the series string is prone to cell reversal on recharge, which kills the pack quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup> The exposed terminals are a further liability, since a spare thrown loose into a toolbox will short across a screwdriver or the case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup>

Size arguments rarely favour the 9 V either. At roughly 16.5 mm it is thicker than a AA's 14 mm, so a meter powered from a 9 V ends up as thick or thicker than the AA equivalent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> Two 9 V cells in series occupy similar or slightly smaller area than four AAs but are thicker, giving no volumetric advantage while sacrificing stored energy to the 4A cell format inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> Enclosure selection reflects the same geometric equivalence from the other direction: off-the-shelf boxes advertised as accepting either a 9 V or AA cells will typically hold only two AAs, because the 9 V is physically a similar size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src---oKMQITODc" title="eevBLAB #29 - How To Search For An Enclosure">•</a></sup> Three-cell holders in either AA or AAA are uncommon; two- and four-cell holders are the norm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src---oKMQITODc" title="eevBLAB #29 - How To Search For An Enclosure">•</a></sup>

Leakage is not exclusive to the AA format — 9 V batteries leak and vent as well — though the AA is the format in which the failure is most often encountered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> Investigating it systematically means holding the discharge conditions constant across brands, since the load matters: a bare [[resistor]] discharges a cell down to the last electron, whereas a DC-DC converter in a real product cuts off at some threshold, and that difference may account for more of the observed leakage behaviour than brand does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1508" title="EEVblog 1508 - We FINALLY Got Alkaline Battery LEAKAGE!">1508</a></sup> One approach is to instrument every cell in a four-cell string with its own voltmeter so that any cell driven to negative [[voltage]] can be correlated with subsequent leakage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9lIC3ZzIht4" title="Junk box Digital Panel Meter Analysis CX101">•</a></sup>

## Multimeter power

AA cells are the preferred [[multimeter]] power source where long [[battery-life|battery life]] is valued, and four of them are enough to make 500-plus hours a realistic target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> Four cells give a 6 V nominal rail, which matters when the analogue front end has a hard minimum: the AD8436 true-RMS converter requires ±2.4 V, or 4.8 V single-ended, leaving relatively little headroom on a 6 V pack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup> Extracting the full pack capacity therefore means pushing the low-battery threshold as low as the regulators allow — a 3.6 V warning point corresponds to 0.9 volts per cell, which uses the vast majority of the energy in the four cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup>

The [[121gw|121GW]] is a worked example of the trade-off being taken deliberately. Fitting four AAs required about five millimetres of extra case thickness over the equivalent AAA design, accepted in exchange for runtime, and yielding an unusually square, thick meter in roughly the same footprint as a [[bm235|BM235]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The manufacturer's 5 mA constant-current curve predicted around 580 hours to a 1.05 V per-cell cut-out rather than the 700 hours the raw capacity might suggest; a two-week bench run on Duracell Ultra Power cells returned about 600 hours, matching the curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> Continuous logging to [[sd-card|SD card]] cut that to something closer to 350–400 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup>

Comparable figures across instruments show how much depends on the design rather than the cells. A Fluke 28 Series II on three AAs achieves 800 hours, and a Keysight U1282A on four AAs likewise 800 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup> A Fluke 233 draws about 5 mA across its base and display units, which against roughly 2,800 mAh AA cells implies about 560 hours, comfortably covering its 400-hour claim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-43" title="EEVblog #43 - Fluke 233 Multimeter Review">43</a></sup> By contrast a Fluke 289 manages only about 100 hours despite running from six AAs and using an ultra-low-power MSP430.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15" title="EEVblog #15 Part 1 of 2 - Fluke 189/289 multimeter review">15</a></sup> A three-AA Fluke 87VI is specified at a minimum of 250 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4UY3uI6tl1E" title="eevBLAB 121 - NEW Fluke 87VI Multimeter! MAYBE?">•</a></sup> Cheap meters on two AAs sit far lower — around 1.7 mA of supply current in one case, giving perhaps 60 to 70 hours in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup>

A high battery-low threshold can negate the advantage entirely: a meter drawing 1.8 mA at 3 V from AA cells still cuts out at about 2.4 V, throwing away most of the pack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> [[battery-voltage|Battery voltage]] also propagates directly into measurement capability. [[diode|Diode]]-test compliance is bounded by the pack: two fresh AAs give about 3.28 volts of open-circuit drive, falling as the cells discharge, which remains sufficient to light a white [[led|LED]] at around 1.6 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> Three AAs do not automatically beat two — one three-cell design manages only 2.1 V of diode compliance against 2.3 V from a two-cell Fluke.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Eight-cell instruments need explicit chemistry selection, since a NiMH pack dropping out at 1.1 volts per cell reaches 8.8 volts where an alkaline pack would still be usable, forcing either an LDO or a switchable low-battery trip point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup>

## Regulation and conversion

A four-AA pack nominally at 6 V falls a long way over its discharge, so even a low-dropout 5 V regulator will not hold regulation for much of the curve — which is why many AA-powered products need a boost or DC-DC stage rather than a linear one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> Boosting from two AAs to 5 V or 3.3 V is a well-trodden path, with typical small modules delivering up to a few hundred milliamps from fresh cells.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup> A pair of AAs and a [[boost-converter|boost converter]] is entirely sufficient to make a 5 volt supply capable of charging a phone; there is nothing exotic about it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-55" title="EEVblog #55 - RCA Airnergy WiFi Charging Free Energy Harvesting Marketing BS">55</a></sup>

Two cells in series with the junction taken as [[ground]] is one way to synthesise a split ±1.5 V supply from AAs, but it fails when the two halves draw unequal current, since the cells then discharge at different rates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

## Hazards

The energy in two AA cells is more than sufficient to do real damage on a short. In one toy failure the lever contact of a slide switch melted clean off and embedded itself in the switch cap, with 3 volts from two AAs passing through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1219" title="EEVblog #1219 - Don&#x27;t Trust Switches - Toy Repair">1219</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GoKbPDADG0c" title="The Incredible Switch Replacement Repair">•</a></sup> The general rule follows: a single AA is enough to ruin a day and possibly start a fire, and any product that clamps additional circuitry onto a cell's terminals — where a burr can short to the negative can — carries that risk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

Peak current capability, not just average, determines whether a booster or adapter can substitute for a bare cell. A device advertising extended battery life that cannot deliver the peak currents a AA-designed toy demands — potentially an amp — will simply cause the product to fail, and an unpublished maximum [[output-current|output current]] of a couple of hundred milliamps is the tell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

Claims of extracting anomalous energy from a AA are readily settled by arithmetic rather than measurement. Powering 23 parallel LEDs for 52 hours from a single AA requires no new physics: LEDs give reasonable brightness at 1 mA, 23 of them amount to about 37 milliwatts, and 37 mW over 52 hours is 1.9 watt hours — comfortably inside the roughly 2.2 watt hours the cell holds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> Similarly, a proposed self-charging AA built around a 100 microwatt nuclear source is bounded by the volume of that source: a couple of hundred microwatts at most in the cylindrical form factor, which then has to charge a [[supercapacitor]] to reach standard AA output capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup>

## Products designed around AA cells

Standardising on AA is itself a product feature, because cells can be shared between devices and replaced anywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1251" title="EEVblog #1251 - LifeSaber Kickstarter - A Master of None FAIL">1251</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-18" title="EEVblog #18 - The Garmin Yellow Etrex GPS">18</a></sup> The Garmin yellow eTrex takes two standard AAs in a removable rubber-sealed compartment and provides [[firmware]] selection between alkaline and rechargeable chemistry, giving 18 to 20 hours — enough for continuous tracking over a multi-day hike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-18" title="EEVblog #18 - The Garmin Yellow Etrex GPS">18</a></sup>

Early portable computers demonstrate what the format allows when consumption is low. The Tandy TRS-80 Model 100 runs 20 hours from four AAs in the back with a month of standby, instant on and off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup> Its successor the Tandy 102 is specified the same way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> The Cambridge Z88 ran its built-in applications for about 20 hours on four AAs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> A Psion 5 returned around 24 hours of solid use from a couple of AAs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup> The Amstrad NC100 uses four AAs in series for a 6 V nominal rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup>

Elsewhere the format appears across a wide range: a Sennheiser EW100 G3 wireless transmitter running about 8 hours on standard AAs,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup> a Zoom H1 recorder on a single AA,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup> the original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 on a couple of AAs,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup> a Sony flat-[[crt|CRT]] WatchCam,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1033" title="EEVblog #1033 - Sony WatchCam Pocket Flat CRT Monitor Teardown">1033</a></sup> Sony's early digital cameras,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-625" title="EEVblog #625 - Retro Teardown: Sony&#x27;s First Digital Camera">625</a></sup> a Casio CT-X700 keyboard on six AAs with a 9.5 V 1 A jack as the alternative,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1108" title="EEVblog #1108 - Casio CT-X700 Keyboard Teardown">1108</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1727" title="EEVblog 1727 - REPAIR: Casio CT-X700 Keyboard">1727</a></sup> a Medela Swing breast pump on four rechargeable AAs in a compartment with finger cut-outs for easy removal,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> an electro-[[adhesive]] whiteboard on four AAs,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1149" title="EEVblog #1149 - How An Electrostatic Whiteboard Works">1149</a></sup> a CPM700 surveillance monitor on eight,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup> and blood-pressure and GPS units on four.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-677" title="EEVblog #677 - Mailbag">677</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup> Analogue multimeters use a single AA solely for the ohms ranges, the meter movement deriving its deflection power from the circuit under test for voltage and current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1079" title="EEVblog #1079 - Mailbag">1079</a></sup> Bench-level construction detail varies: [[soldering]] the AA terminals directly to the [[pcb|PCB]], and fitting an O-ring around the battery compartment, are marks of a well-executed design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup>
