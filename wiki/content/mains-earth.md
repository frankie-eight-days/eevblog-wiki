Mains earth is the protective earth conductor of the AC supply, brought into an appliance on the third pin of its IEC or mains connector and bonded to the chassis and to any exposed metalwork. In test and measurement equipment it is not merely a safety conductor: it is the reference node that the instrument's measurement common is usually tied to, which makes it the single most consequential fact about how a bench instrument may be connected to a circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> Almost every hazard, measurement artefact and blown input on an earthed bench traces back to the fact that mains earth is common to every earthed instrument, every earthed mat and every earthed device under test simultaneously.

On a [[schematic]] and on chassis hardware the chassis mains earth symbol is distinct from the internal signal ground symbols, and the two are not interchangeable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup>

## Earth referencing in oscilloscopes

On almost all benchtop oscilloscopes the outer metal ring of the input BNC is mains earth grounded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> The shell is connected through to the earth pin of the IEC inlet by a very low [[impedance]] path — measured directly between the earth pin and the BNC shell, the reading is 0.9 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> "It's effectively a dead short."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Because the probe's ground clip lead is bonded to that shell, the alligator or crocodile clip on the end of the probe sits at mains earth potential too.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup>

The consequence is asymmetric. Clipping the ground lead onto a point in a genuinely floating circuit does no damage; it simply mains earth references that node, which in most cases has no effect beyond possible hum in audio work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Clipping it onto any node of a circuit that is itself already mains earth referenced — anything other than that circuit's own earth — creates a fault loop: [[current]] flows from the node, through the probe lead, through the BNC shield, through the [[oscilloscope]], out through mains earth, back through the power board and the IEC lead into the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> The ground lead and the instrument input are what absorb it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup>

The path has to be able to carry that fault current. Early Tektronix TDS220 boards were recalled and modified with a strap taking the earth terminal directly down to the BNCs, because the original path did not have low enough impedance to carry the current safely through to mains earth; a fault could destroy a channel and leave it floating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-690" title="EEVblog #690 - TDS220 Oscilloscope Autopsy">690</a></sup> Later Tektronix designs make the point emphatically — the 2 Series has a substantial dedicated [[grounding]] point and issues a boot-time warning insisting the earth terminal be connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup>

## Working safely around an earthed reference

The first rule when probing unfamiliar equipment is not to assume that a black wire or a labelled ground in the target is mains earth referenced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> Where it matters, the assumption is tested rather than trusted: buzz the suspect node against a known mains earth point, confirm it reads essentially zero ohms, and confirm there is no AC [[voltage]] on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> The same buzzing technique identifies polarity on unmarked connectors — a barrel connector whose outer shell goes to mains earth must be the negative.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup>

Where the target genuinely cannot be earth referenced, the alternatives are a high voltage differential probe or a floating system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> A differential probe removes the constraint on the input side but not on the output: the common of its output is connected to the oscilloscope and therefore through to mains earth, so its common mode voltage is measured with respect to mains earth and its common mode rating is the limit that matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> Floating means either [[battery]] power or a supply that is not itself mains earth referenced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-319" title="EEVblog #319 - Lead Free PCB Tinning">319</a></sup> A circuit powered from a floating bench supply can be probed anywhere with impunity — until something re-earths it, such as plugging in a [[usb]] cable to a mains-powered computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup>

Conversely, where everything else in a setup is floating, deliberately commoning one node to mains earth is the safe move rather than the dangerous one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> The same logic makes secondary-side repair work routine: once the chassis is confirmed to be mains earth and the output is mains earth referenced, the scope probe can go on the chassis and the secondary side probed freely, while the primary side remains off limits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup> An element or panel confirmed to be at mains earth can be touched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup>

## Soldering irons and live work

A mains-earthed [[soldering-iron]] tip is shorted through to mains earth, which is why [[soldering]] on a powered circuit that is not floating shorts the circuit through the tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-319" title="EEVblog #319 - Lead Free PCB Tinning">319</a></sup> Tip earthing is a design decision that varies: the Quick TS11 runs the earth through to the front so the tip is earthed, with a 1 megohm [[resistor]] from mains earth as well, while the JBC CD-2BB takes earth from the mains inlet only as far as the main board, leaving the tip isolated from earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup> Internal earth wiring is also easy to misread — on the Bakon BK212 a connection that looks like a mains earth is in fact the sleep-sensing line back to the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OvGdE5hC1Ro" title="Bakon BK212 Soldering Station - URGH">•</a></sup>

The hazard extends to component-level rework inside earthed instruments. Replacing the [[calibration]] backup battery in an HP 3478A requires care because the battery's negative terminal is mains earth referenced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup>

## ESD bench earthing

Anti-static mats and wrist straps should be referenced to mains earth, though a mat retains some usefulness unearthed because charge still dissipates across its surface and into the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> A dissipative mat is built as a very high [[resistance]] top surface over a conductive backing, and it is the backing that is taken back to earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-679" title="EEVblog #679 - How To Build A Small Electronics Work Bench">679</a></sup>

The correct topology is a star: every strap and every mat runs its own lead back to one central mains earth point, and mats are never daisy chained in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-679" title="EEVblog #679 - How To Build A Small Electronics Work Bench">679</a></sup> Each path carries a 1 megohm protection resistor, either built into a proper bonding point or into the strap lead itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-679" title="EEVblog #679 - How To Build A Small Electronics Work Bench">679</a></sup> That resistor is not optional — a direct zero ohm short between the wearer's body and mains earth is dangerous, and the resistor is what stands between the operator and anything that goes live.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-679" title="EEVblog #679 - How To Build A Small Electronics Work Bench">679</a></sup> A typical strap therefore sits at a couple of megohms to mains earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1567" title="EEVblog 1567 - Wireless ESD Monitor? - The IONA StatIQ Band TESTED">1567</a></sup> A grounding bonding point wired back to mains earth, with the wrist strap plugged into it, is the minimum practical provision for a home lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>

## Earth as a measurement reference and a noise path

Because mains earth is shared, it couples as well as protects. A dodgy or absent earth connection at a probe raises the high [[frequency]] noise on a trace visibly, and a direct mains earth connection attenuates it substantially.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f58qoyCETAE" title="Scope WTF Follow-Up - BEWARE Dodgy Connectors">•</a></sup> The Keysight U1272A EMC anomaly involved conducted noise referenced to a mains earth referenced output, with the coupling persisting whether or not the operator was tied to the ESD mat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup> Earth referencing is also enough to drive a display: with a common pin returned to mains earth, [[lcd]] segments can be turned on and held on by the electric field picked up by a body alone, then decay slowly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup>

In precision measurement the earthed shield is doing a specific job. The outer shield of a triaxial connector on a source measure unit goes to mains earth to keep out 50 Hz mains hum, with a separate inner guard shield inside it for the measurement itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> EMC pre-compliance hardware makes the choice explicit, with a switch to connect either directly through mains earth or through an inductive filter, plus a separate case earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup> In an anechoic test facility, the connector shell is at mains earth purely for safety, so that an accidentally energised fixture does not become a hazard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup>

## Earthed versus floating instrument architecture

Whether an instrument's front-panel common is earthed is a deliberate design choice with direct implications for how it can be interconnected. Bench multimeters are expected to float: the HP 3457A front panel ground has no connection whatsoever through to mains earth, and in the Keithley DMM7510 the input shielding and [[coaxial-cable|coax]] braid are tied not to mains earth but to the negative input, so the measurement ground is not earth referenced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> The NI VirtualBench is mains earth referenced throughout, with the earth pin soldered to the [[ground-plane]], except for the [[multimeter]], which floats.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup>

Mixed architectures are common and easy to get wrong. On a Siglent SDG5000, two of the rear connectors are mains earth referenced and three are isolated, distinguishable by the presence or absence of the white insulating collar.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> The current monitor output of a BK Precision 8601 [[electronic-load]] is isolated from mains earth, but is tied to the negative input terminal rather than being truly floating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> The pass/fail output of an Owon SDS oscilloscope is not isolated from mains earth or from the inputs at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Where the reference is left to the user, a mains earth binding post or terminal is provided so the outputs can be earth referenced by choice: an Agilent 6643A supply takes an optional earth binding post, and a variable frequency converter with isolated outputs brings out a ground that connects through to mains earth if an earth referenced output is wanted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-667" title="EEVblog #667 - Agilent 6643A Power Supply Binding Post Hack">667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-449" title="EEVblog #449 - Absopulse VFC500 Variable Frequency Converter Teardown">449</a></sup> Interconnect ratings are quoted against it — a mystery instrument input rated 100 V AC/DC maximum also carries a separate 300 V to mains earth limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup>

Isolation can also be deliberately bypassed. A Ness D16X alarm panel is fed from a three-wire plug pack that carries earth around the [[transformer]] rather than through it, so the panel's sensor ground — and everything referenced to it — sits at mains earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup>

## Earth bonding as a build-quality indicator

Inside an appliance the earth path is judged by its mechanical and electrical integrity. Good practice is a dedicated mains earth wire running to the earth terminal, terminated in a properly crimped spade lug with a shake-proof or split washer, giving a very low impedance path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup> Creepage between mains and earth is checked as well — around 5 to 6 mm in a Rohde & Schwarz NGP800.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> Cabinet and chassis parts are bonded for the same reason, including the die-cast front of a studio monitor and the earthed racking of a roof solar array.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1156" title="EEVblog #1156 - Focal Professional CMS40 Studio Monitor Teardown">1156</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1385" title="EEVblog 1385 - 8kW Home Solar Power System EXPANSION!">1385</a></sup> Where the bonding is not what it appears, it shows up on the meter: in one HEPA fume extractor the mains plug earth is not connected through to the chassis stud, only through a wire to the motor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1305" title="EEVblog #1305 - HEPA Solder Fume Extractor">1305</a></sup>

Earth and neutral are deliberately not joined inside a product; under a multiple earth neutral system they are bonded only back at the distribution point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1171" title="EEVblog #1171 - Yihau WEP SMD Rework Station Meltdown!">1171</a></sup> Transient protection is referenced to earth as well — MOVs from the input lines down to mains earth, and, in a Keysight EDU34450A, a 100 ohm resistor to mains earth in series with a MOV and a spark gap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup> Supply outputs commonly carry suppression capacitors to mains earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-667" title="EEVblog #667 - Agilent 6643A Power Supply Binding Post Hack">667</a></sup>

Claims made about earthing are also a useful test of a product's honesty. A television [[antenna]] marketed on the basis that its special circuitry is grounded appears to do nothing more than couple to mains earth through a [[capacitor]], which is not a mechanism by which it could receive anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LzAUa_FtuC0" title="Livewave SUPER ANTENNA! SCAM">•</a></sup>
