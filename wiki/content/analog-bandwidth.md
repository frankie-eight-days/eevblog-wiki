Analog bandwidth is the [[frequency]] at which an instrument's front end falls 3 dB down in its [[frequency-response|frequency response]], and it is the single most important headline specification of an [[oscilloscope]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup> It describes what the analog signal path — [[connector]], attenuator, amplifier — can pass before the ADC ever sees the [[waveform]], which is why it is quoted separately from [[sample-rate|sample rate]] and [[memory-depth|memory depth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> It is not, however, a specification that can be read on its own: a scope's usable performance depends on how [[bandwidth]], sample rate, memory depth, update rate and display processing interact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

## Definition and the 3 dB point

The number quoted on the front panel is the −3 dB corner of the front-end response, equivalent to the amplitude falling to 0.707 of its low-frequency value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> The corner is not a brick wall. A 200 MHz front end does not stop passing signal at 200 MHz; it begins rolling off there, and content beyond the corner is attenuated rather than removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> The practical consequence is that a 200 MHz [[square-wave|square wave]] applied to a 200 MHz input does not reach the ADC as a square wave — the sharp rise and fall times depend on harmonics well above the corner, and those are filtered out by the front end before digitisation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup>

The shape of the roll-off matters as much as its corner frequency. Traditional analog [[crt|CRT]] scopes have a Gaussian response, for which [[rise-time|rise time]] relates to bandwidth by the classic rise time equals 0.35 divided by bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Because the relationship between displayed rise time and true bandwidth is direct, a fast-edge pulse generator can be used to derive a scope's real bandwidth from the rise time it displays, assuming the input pulse is effectively perfect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Front ends with a different response shape produce a different resultant waveform for the same input, and the response type also changes how reconstruction filters such as sin(x)/x interpolation behave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup>

## Measuring it

The direct method is to sweep the analog input with an RF [[signal-generator|signal generator]] at a fixed input amplitude and find the frequency at which the displayed amplitude drops to 0.707, rather than inferring it from a pulse and the 0.35 formula.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Measured results can exceed the [[datasheet]]: a Siglent SDS1000X specified at 200 MHz was found to be 3 dB down at 300 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> Bandwidth that is honest in the analog domain can still be undermined by an inadequate sample rate, and by sample rate that halves when a second channel is turned on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup>

Claimed figures at the low end of the market are often unsupported. A pocket instrument advertising 40 MHz analog bandwidth showed jitter severe enough to be unusable at 20 MHz and could not reproduce a 1 MHz square wave with any high-frequency detail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup>

## Relationship to sample rate

Nyquist sets the floor: sample rate must be at least twice the analog bandwidth for interpolation to be mathematically valid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup> The working rule of thumb is ten times, not two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> A scope that does not carry ten times its bandwidth in sample rate is limited in single-shot use, and dividing the sample rate by ten gives the usable single-shot bandwidth — a 500 MS/s instrument nominally rated at 300 MHz delivers roughly 50 MHz for single-shot work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> Genuine ten-times ratios have been available since the Tektronix TDS 200 series, which paired 1 GS/s with 100 MHz of analog bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup> Modern bench instruments meet or exceed the ratio; a Rigol with 1 GS/s and 50 MHz analog bandwidth yields about twenty points per cycle of a waveform at the corner frequency, in real time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup>

The ratio breaks down at the top of the range, where sample rates cannot keep pace. A probe and front end offering 2 GHz of analog bandwidth backed by 3.2 GS/s cannot deliver that bandwidth in real time; two units can be synchronised to raise the effective sample rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> Equivalent-time sampling is sometimes presented as a substitute for real-time sample rate, but an instrument relying on it is not a real-time scope and is of little use above roughly 20 to 30 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup>

Sample rate and analog bandwidth serve different purposes, and both are needed. High analog bandwidth is what makes measurements such as eye diagrams possible, while sample rate governs single-shot capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>

## Typical values

Two or three decades ago a good entry-level scope offered 20 MHz of analog bandwidth; the figure for a general-purpose instrument has since risen to a recommended minimum of about 50 MHz, driven by high-speed digital and [[microcontroller]] work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup> Bench instruments now span a wide range: 300 MHz at 2 GS/s in a four-channel Siglent SDS2000X,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> 350 MHz at 4 GS/s with 12-bit resolution in a Rigol positioned under $1000,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> 500 MHz and 1 GHz options in the Tektronix MDO4000 at 5 GS/s,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> 1 GHz at 5 GS/s in the Rigol DS6104,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-114" title="EEVblog #114 - Electronex 2010 Electronics Exhibition">114</a></sup> and 1 GHz at up to 4 GS/s in the older LeCroy 9384C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup> Platforms have been announced with up to 16 GHz of analog bandwidth at 40 GS/s with channel interleaving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5YjS4DHKlQU" title="Tektronix 5 Series Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>

LeCroy historically [[led]] on large sample memories, fast acquisition rates and high analog bandwidths, and its instruments remain less sought-after second-hand than comparable Agilent and Tektronix models.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup>

Portable and PC-connected instruments sit lower. A ruggedised IP54 handheld with isolated BNCs offers a full 200 MHz;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup> a Fluke 91 Scopemeter, 50 MHz;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> a Moku:Go, dual 30 MHz inputs at 125 MS/s alongside dual 20 MHz analog outputs;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> an Analog Discovery 3, around 9 MHz with no real front end at all;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup> and a handheld Zotek instrument, 5 MHz at 48 MS/s with no equivalent-time sampling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup> An OWON XDS3202A pairs 200 MHz with a 14-bit converter, at the cost of only 1 GS/s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup>

Analog bandwidth is not exclusive to oscilloscopes. A Sony data recorder scaled its analog bandwidth with channel count, giving 20 kHz in two-channel mode and only 5 kHz with all sixteen channels sampling at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup> Catalogue ICs exist at 25 GHz analog bandwidth for a broadband AND/OR logic gate, and a spectrometer [[asic|ASIC]] processing at 5.5 GHz bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup> A Red Pitaya-class instrument with roughly 50 to 60 MHz of analog bandwidth is sufficient to plot [[capacitor]] [[impedance]] against frequency into the tens of MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

## Bandwidth as a purchasing and licensing variable

Within a model family the analog bandwidth is frequently the same hardware at every price point, with the difference sold as a software option — a 70 MHz Siglent SDS2000X and its 300 MHz sibling ran from $1285 to $2800 on hardware believed to be identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> The same pattern holds in the entry-level Rigol DS2000 class, where the purchase is essentially software options rather than analog performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> Where a lower-bandwidth model has been unlocked, the analog front end is generally judged capable of the higher figure, since the bandwidth is present in the hardware for a reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-978" title="EEVblog #978 - Keysight 1000X Oscilloscope Hacked!">978</a></sup>

Bandwidth is not the only axis worth paying for. Sustained [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]] that does not collapse when serial decode and mask testing are enabled can be worth more, for some requirements, than additional analog bandwidth or [[sample-memory|sample memory]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> Comparisons between scopes must be made on matched terms; a [[noise]]-floor comparison is only meaningful if memory depth, analog bandwidth, intensity-graded display setting, update rate and sample rate are all equal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

On analog scopes, a third channel is often the external trigger input pressed into service as a trace, typically with reduced analog bandwidth compared with the main channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup>

## Probes and the delivered bandwidth

The specification at the BNC is only achievable if the probe and its connection can reach it. Among passive probes, 700 MHz represents about the practical ceiling, with 1 GHz passive probes existing at the extreme.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> Mechanical design can defeat the specification entirely: an instrument with a nominally good 60 MHz analog bandwidth was rendered unusable with any ×10 probe because the BNC spacing prevented the probe bodies from seating, leaving it serviceable only with plain BNC cables.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1127" title="EEVblog #1127 - Mailbag Monday">1127</a></sup>
