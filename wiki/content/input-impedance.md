Input impedance is the effective [[impedance]] a measuring instrument or amplifier input presents to the circuit it is connected to. Because that impedance appears in parallel with whatever is being measured, it changes the circuit under test, and the resulting error is one of the most common traps in practical measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> The consequences run from a few least-significant digits of error on a low-impedance source to readings that are wrong by an order of magnitude in high-impedance circuits, so the number matters as much as any accuracy specification on the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## Circuit loading and the order-of-magnitude rule

A [[digital-multimeter|digital multimeter]] does not have infinite input impedance; it is roughly 10 megaohms for most instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> Placing 10 megaohms across a 1 megaohm [[resistor]] in a divider causes a measurement error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> The rule of thumb is that one order of magnitude between the circuit impedance and the meter impedance gives roughly 10% error, two orders of magnitude gives about 1%, and once the instrument is up in the gigaohms the loading is not really going to affect the result at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> In a divider of 1 megaohm and 166.6k, an 11 megaohm meter across the 1 megaohm leg produces a calculated 1.428 V where the unloaded division would give more, which is exactly what a meter reads out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

The same arithmetic explains why the effect is invisible most of the time. With 100 ohms in series feeding a 10 megaohm meter the error is negligible; the source impedance has to fall to around 100k before that 100 ohms costs about 0.1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> Conversely, a meter with only 120k input impedance placed across a node dominated by a 10 megaohm path collapses the reading — 10 megaohms in parallel with 120k is essentially 120k, and a 5 V reading drops to 0.33 V, then to 0.08 V on a lower range, then to zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

Loading also interacts with leakage. A reverse-biased PN [[diode]] with roughly 473 nanoamps of leakage develops nearly 5 V across a 10 megaohm meter input, which is why a diode that is supposed to block appears to pass [[voltage]]; a Schottky with 10 nanoamps of leakage produces far less.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## Multimeter practice

Ten megaohms, or thereabouts, is the standard on almost every voltage range, AC or DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Real instruments cluster around 10 to 11 megaohms depending on the front-end design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Measured examples include 11.1 megaohms on a [[brymen-bm786|Brymen BM786]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> 11 megaohms on the 2 V range of an Amprobe AM220 falling to 10 on higher ranges,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> 20 megaohms on a Uni-T UT892 2000 V meter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1734" title="EEVblog 1734 - Uni-T UT892 2000V High Voltage Multimeter TESTED">1734</a></sup> and 9.9 megaohms measured in circuit on a Keysight U1273AX with the possibility of about 11 megaohms total once a second resistor is included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup> A buying guide criterion is at least 10 megaohms; cheap meters down at one or two megaohms should be avoided.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Because the front end is high impedance, the series [[input-protection|input protection]] resistors ahead of the chipset do not need to be precision parts — 1% is adequate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

Millivolt ranges are frequently the exception, and are often higher: 27 megaohms on the millivolt range of an Amprobe AM220 and 17 megaohms on an Extech EX330, both dropping to about 10 megaohms elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> A top-of-the-line meter will have very high input impedance on the low millivolt ranges — greater than 1 gigaohm below 1 volt on an Agilent U1253A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup> Not every meter has it, and some handhelds advertised as having it do not deliver: one $50 meter measures 10 megaohms on millivolt mode where the comparable Fluke is genuinely high impedance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>

MOV-based overload protection sits across the input and leaks, so the nominal impedance is not perfectly constant with voltage. On a Brymen BM2257 the nominal 10 megaohms measures 10.02 megaohms at 1100 V and falls only to about 9.63 megaohms at the top of the range, which is not a meaningful departure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup> A degraded connection inside the input path is a different matter: on a Tektronix DMM916 a loose fastener created a high-impedance path in a 10 megaohm divider and produced readings that were slightly low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup>

## High-impedance and low-impedance modes

Precision meters offer a selectable high-impedance mode, usually presented as a choice between 10 meg and auto.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> In auto the input [[resistance]] is effectively removed and the impedance is set by the FET input amplifier — gigaohms, specified in one [[datasheet|data sheet]] as greater than about 2 gigaohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The mode is not always available across the full range set: one 6.5-digit Agilent meter allows effectively infinite input impedance up to the 10 V range but not on the hundreds-of-volts ranges,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> and a Prema 6047 has it on the 200 mV, 2 V and 20 V ranges but loses it at 200 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup> Some bench meters do not offer it at all despite otherwise good specifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup> Bench meters are widely assumed to be high impedance on more ranges than they actually are; a Keysight bench meter sits at 10 megaohms on ordinary voltage ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup>

The trade-off runs the other way. A very high input impedance picks up everything: with the leads flapping, readings drift with lead position, hand proximity and location in the lab, and twisting the leads significantly reduces the pickup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> On a high-impedance input the display charges up and can walk all the way to overload with nothing connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> [[multimeter|Multimeter]] low ranges can be effectively open — many gigaohms — which is why contamination matters, since [[flux]] residue, dust, grime and fingerprints all form leakage paths across a sensitive input node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup> A junk-box panel meter with 100 megaohms input impedance shows the same behaviour, drifting with capacitive build-up and responding to a wiggling hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9lIC3ZzIht4" title="Junk box Digital Panel Meter Analysis CX101">•</a></sup> Some of that [[noise]] is not an impedance problem at all but an integration-time problem: the same 10 megaohm input on a bench meter is quiet at one power line cycle and wildly noisy at 0.1 NPLC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup>

Low-impedance (low-Z) modes solve the opposite problem, deliberately loading the input to kill ghost voltages. Implementations vary: a Hioki sits around 900-odd kilohms at 1.5 V test voltage,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> a Brymen BM2257 measures 2.3k where the earlier [[bm235|BM235]] reads 10 megaohms because its low-impedance mode does not engage until about 6 or 7 volts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> and the [[121gw|121GW]] uses a thermistor directly across the input that starts near 2k cold and rises as mains [[current]] heats it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The impedance in these modes is not constant — it decreases as the applied voltage increases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

## Analog meters and ohms per volt

Analog meters specify input impedance in ohms per volt, because the load is set by the movement sensitivity and the range multiplier rather than by a fixed divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> A 20,000 ohms-per-volt movement corresponds to 50 microamps full scale and is typical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> On a 12 V range a 10,000 ohms-per-volt meter presents 120k.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Because the figure scales with range, the highest range can be better than a digital meter: 1,000 volts times 20k is 20 megaohms, twice a typical digital meter, though every lower range is far worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> A 100k ohms-per-volt movement gives 100 megaohms at the 1000 V range,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-633" title="EEVblog #633 - Mailbag">633</a></sup> and higher sensitivity movements were a significant selling point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup>

Vacuum-tube voltmeters were valued precisely because the tubes made them active and therefore high impedance, comparable in spirit to the fixed 10 megaohms of a modern digital meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup> The FET VOM that replaced them fixed the input impedance and nothing else: a Micronta 22220 gives a fixed 10 megaohms on DC volts only, with the same non-linear backwards ohms scale, the same zeroing problems and the same accuracy limits as any analog meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope and probe inputs

The standard [[oscilloscope]] input is 1 megaohm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> A times-one probe is essentially just a length of [[coaxial-cable|coax]] straight through to that 1 megaohm input, so the probe presents 1 megaohm at DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> A times-ten probe adds a 9 megaohm [[series-resistor|series resistor]] to give a nominal 10 megaohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> The times-ten label is a misnomer — the probe divides the signal by ten, and the marking is a reminder to set the matching factor in the channel menu.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> The [[bandwidth]] penalty of times-one is severe: 40 nanoseconds [[rise-time|rise time]] against 900 picoseconds for times-ten on the same probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup>

Selectable 50 ohm input impedance is standard on RF-capable and higher-end instruments and appears on some inexpensive scopes as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> Where it is absent, an inline [[50-ohm-termination|50 ohm terminator]] serves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> Termination also reduces displayed noise: on one instrument, switching from 1 megaohm to 50 ohm roughly halves the visible noise at 1 mV per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> It does not cure noise that originates elsewhere in the front end — a channel set to 50 ohms with nothing connected can still show substantial noise, and terminating the input makes no difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup> Spectrum analyser RF inputs are 50 ohm,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup> with a 75 ohm option on some models,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-891" title="EEVblog #891 - Siglent SSA3021X vs Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser">891</a></sup> absent on others.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> Real hardware does not always meet the nominal figure: one [[usb|USB]] oscilloscope measures 55 ohms on its 50 ohm setting, about 10% out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup>

Input impedance is only the DC half of the story. [[input-capacitance|Input capacitance]] dominates at [[frequency]]: 1 picofarad of probe [[capacitance]] is 159 ohms at high frequency, so a 1 megaohm probe becomes a 159 ohm load and can seriously load the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> A times-ten passive probe is around 16 picofarads,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> the highest-bandwidth passive probes reach 3.9 picofarads at the same 10 megaohm DC impedance,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> and active FET probes hold 1 megaohm with under 3 picofarads,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> one example combining 1 gigahertz bandwidth with 1 megaohm and 1.2 picofarads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup> A purely resistive high-frequency probe reverses the trade: adding a 450 ohm series resistor gives the same 10:1 division but with about 500 ohms input impedance instead of 10 megaohms, which loads the line heavily at DC while contributing almost no capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>

Scope loading is a real constraint on measurement, not a theoretical one. A peak detector with a 1 nanofarad hold [[capacitor]] droops visibly at 1 kilohertz purely because of the 10 megaohm scope probe, even though the [[op-amp|op amp]]'s [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] is only 1 picoamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> A slow-charging capacitor node in an overload detector cannot be observed at all with a 10 megaohm times-ten probe, because the probe discharges the node faster than the circuit charges it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Enabling a second channel on a 1 megaohm input can itself change the fidelity of the signal on the first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Logic analyser probes are lower impedance still — a nominal 100k with 8 picofarads is typical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup>

## Design considerations

On the design side, high input impedance is what makes several standard techniques work. An amplifier or ADC front end whose input impedance is essentially infinite draws no input current, so a [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] can be used as a Kelvin sense tap with no [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] across the tap path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> A unity-gain buffer is high impedance in and low impedance out, which is what makes it useful for isolating one part of a circuit from another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup> Inverting amplifier configurations, by contrast, draw input current through the input resistor and should not be driven from a high-impedance source, since the source resistance enters the gain equation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> Composite amplifiers exist partly to let a designer combine high input impedance with bandwidth, offset, drift and output drive characteristics that no single device provides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1609" title="EEVblog 1609 - Composite Amplifier Tutorial + Practical Demo">1609</a></sup>

Impedance can be too high as well as too low. A successive-approximation ADC cannot be driven from an arbitrarily high source impedance, because the external impedance sets how long the internal sampling capacitor takes to charge; a 12-bit converter must settle very close to the final value or the conversion carries additional error, and at 10k source impedance a 0.1% LSB deviation target becomes unachievable, which is why buffer amplifiers are placed ahead of the converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Chopper-stabilised amplifiers suffer charge injection from their internal switches, and the fix is to lower the input and feedback resistances as far as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-24" title="EEVblog #24 - Chopper Operational Amplifiers">24</a></sup> Floating a 4000-series CMOS input is dangerous for the same reason it is convenient — the inputs are effectively infinite impedance, so 10 megaohm pull-ups work fine and dissipate essentially nothing, but a floating input on a long line acts as an [[antenna]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup>

At the extremes, condenser microphone preamplifiers demand staggeringly high input impedances together with very low leakage currents and very low parasitic capacitances, which is a classic JFET application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup> Electrometer-class instrumentation goes further still, using 250 gigaohm resistors and hermetically sealed low-leakage relays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup> Picoammeters invert the usual concern entirely: manuals specify minimum input impedances, but what that really constrains is a minimum input voltage, so that the instrument's [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]] does not dominate the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup> High-voltage probe design can also relax the requirement — measuring a high-energy source with low source impedance does not call for ultra-high input impedance, so a divider built from five 10 megaohm resistors in series, each rated at 3.5 kV, is adequate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-85" title="EEVblog #85 - High Voltage Oscilloscope Probe Design">85</a></sup>
