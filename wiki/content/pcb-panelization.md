PCB panelization is the practice of stepping and repeating a single board design across a larger sheet of laminate so that many copies are fabricated, assembled and handled as one unit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> The panel is not merely a shipping convenience: it is itself a [[pcb|printed circuit board]], carrying the mechanical frame, the tooling holes and the optical fiducials that assembly machinery needs to find the work, and it can carry copper traces and even active circuitry of its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Getting a single small board manufactured and populated loose is uneconomical, because the [[pick-and-place-machine|pick and place machine]] processes that one board and ejects it, with handling problems at every stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup>

## Why boards are panelized

The dominant reason is assembly throughput. Rather than running one tiny board at a time through the pick and place machine, a panel lets every part on every copy be placed in a single pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Anyone building in reasonable quantity through a pick and place machine panelizes as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> On a production line, a six-board nest can clear an inspection or placement station in about 82 seconds, amortising the cycle across all six.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>

The benefit is not confined to machine assembly. A hand assembler often prefers a large panel, because handling is easier and the same component can be placed across ten boards in succession.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Handing a bare-board assembler a tiny loose board invites a large surcharge, and the work will likely end up hand-soldered anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1158" title="EEVblog #1158 - How To Create PCB Mod Boards">1158</a></sup>

Bare-board cost also favours panels. The fabricator charges essentially by panel area, so board outlines that pack badly waste money: an odd-shaped board simply does not fit as many times onto a panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3_CQKSC1JNE" title="Davinci Resolve Speed Editor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> Designs that square off their outline, using cutouts in the metalwork rather than a contoured PCB edge, fit more copies per panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup> A large four-layer board consumes a great deal of panel space, which is why small prototype-shield-sized boards can be sold five for ten dollars while a big board cannot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ct9K-EutiIQ" title="JLCPCB Prototype Inspection">•</a></sup> Very long assemblies are split into halves for the same reason, because a metre-long board is impractical for most pick and place machines and PCB panels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup> Historically the economics were starker still: a custom double-sided board once cost hundreds of dollars for a single piece precisely because the buyer had to purchase the entire panel, so the design was made to fit as many copies as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mW-1FMGpsFg" title="UNSW Talk - Part 2 - Job Tip, What I hated about university EE, Contracting + more">•</a></sup> Early volume production worked the same way, with roughly eight boards to a panel drilled several deep at once on quad drills.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-280" title="EEVblog #280-1 - Talking Electronics - History - Part 1 of 5">280</a></sup>

## Anatomy of a panel

In its minimal form a panel is a physical mounting frame: tooling holes to hold the assembly in place inside the pick and place machine, and visual alignment fiducials for the placement head to register against.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Breakaway rails run outside the board array to give the machinery something to grip, which matters particularly when several different designs share the sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup>

A worked example places the individual boards in an array, then adds tooling strips top and bottom carrying 3.2 mm tooling holes, with fiducials formed as pads with the [[solder-mask|solder mask]] expanded around them to give high contrast.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Fiducials are placed on both sides even when components populate only one, and the panel outline is sized from simple arithmetic on the individual board dimensions — a 79 mm by 50 mm board scaled up by the desired row and column count — then adjusted by dragging the panel corners.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Fabricators add their own margin of waste material around the outside of the customer panel for handling and alignment, including alignment holes of their own, since panels are stacked and drilled several at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup>

Spacing between adjacent boards is a deliberate choice rather than a leftover. Setting the pitch 2.4 mm wider than the board itself leaves a 2.4 mm gap, which matches a standard routing bit width and lets the fabricator clear the whole slot in a single pass of the router.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Fabricators can route any width requested, but a single-pass path is cheaper and cleaner.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> [[silkscreen|Silkscreen]] outlines are pulled back so that roughly a 1 mm gap remains, rather than pushing artwork to the edge of what the process will hold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

## Separation methods

Three techniques hold a board inside its panel, and they are routinely mixed on the same design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup>

V-grooving, or V-scoring, cuts a shallow groove from both faces so the boards snap out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> It is cheap and simple, but it is destructive to copper: the scoring wheel saws through anything crossing the groove, so no signal can be routed across a V-scored boundary on either face.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> A V-scored edge is also rough to the touch, which is one of the tells that a finished board was snapped out of a panel rather than routed individually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup>

Routing cuts the board outline with a drill that plunges and then mills any shape required, leaving small tabs — mouse bites — to retain the board until it is broken out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup> Routing is what makes non-rectangular outlines possible, including circular boards, which are panelized with breakout tabs like any other shape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-407" title="EEVblog #407 - Mailbag">407</a></sup> Because the router works to a finite bit radius, inside corners come back rounded, which is visible on the finished piece.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup> Mouse bites and break-off tabs are the standard evidence that a board was panelized; they appear on cost-optimised instrument boards where several copies clearly shared a sheet,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-985" title="EEVblog #985 - Siglent SDS1202X-E Oscilloscope Teardown">985</a></sup> on older commercial assemblies,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup> and on flex panels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>

Which method is chosen has consequences for the finished appearance. On a board that forms a product's front panel, the separation features are deliberately placed at the corners so that trimming with side cutters leaves clean edges; left to a fabricator's default they may be put on the sides, which looks noticeably worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> For a board hidden inside an enclosure this does not matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>

Break-off tabs are sometimes used for reasons that have nothing to do with fabrication. A section of a larger board may be tabbed off and separated into its own module simply because it is tested on a separate test rig, even though the mouse bites could have been left connected with no electrical penalty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> Conversely, cutting an outline back at the panelization stage buys nothing if the routing has to happen anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup>

## Panelizing yourself versus letting the fabricator do it

For production, the panel should be a controlled design the customer owns and hands to the assembler, so that every parameter is known.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> A fabricator left to panelize at will may omit the tooling holes and fiducials the assembly process requires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Supplying only a single-board file leaves the fabricator guessing, and the default placement of separation features can land in the worst possible position for the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> The recommended practice is therefore to do the panelization in-house: it is quick, and it avoids protracted back-and-forth with the bare-board manufacturer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup>

Fabricator panelization remains acceptable where the panel exists purely for handling — twenty copies of one design wanted as a single unit, with V-scoring, and no assembly-machine requirements to satisfy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> It is also worth noting that panelizing an existing loose design incurs a fresh tooling charge that a straight reorder would not, though the charge is modest; since retooling is being paid for anyway, it is a natural moment to fold in other artwork changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Some fabricators charge nothing extra per unit for a panelized board over a loose one, which changes the comparison between suppliers substantially: a supplier several times more expensive on a loose board may come out only modestly more expensive once panelization is priced in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-241" title="EEVblog #241 - Circuit Labs PCBs from New Zealand">241</a></sup> Panelization is sometimes supplied unrequested, along with spare panels from the same run.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-241" title="EEVblog #241 - Circuit Labs PCBs from New Zealand">241</a></sup>

## Shared prototype panels

Low-cost prototype services achieve their prices by pooling many customers' designs onto one manufacturing panel, then breaking them out and shipping each customer their own boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup> Because the sheet is shared, every design on it must conform to the same fixed process parameters, and the service will adjust incoming Gerbers where they do not comply — expanding or reducing solder mask, for instance, to meet a 0.2 mm minimum mask bridge between copper pad edges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup> A customer paying for a whole panel can dictate such terms; a customer paying a few dollars for five boards on a shared panel cannot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1327" title="EEVblog #1327 - 3 Ways to FAIL at PCB Manufacture">1327</a></sup>

Shared panels also constrain what post-processing is available. Selective V-scoring of one customer's boards cannot be done, because the whole panel goes through the scoring machine as a unit; requesting it forces the design onto its own separate process at substantially higher cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup> Where a fabricator does offer a customer-specific panel, the extra setup carries a price premium, and ordering several different designs as separate single pieces can work out cheaper than requesting one customer panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Some services also attach a yield disclaimer to shared panels, charging up to 30 percent more if the customer will not accept the possibility of a few bad boards in the batch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup>

The pooled-panel process leaves a visible artefact: the fabricator prints an identifier code on each design so that the correct boards can be cut out and shipped to the correct customer once the shared panel is separated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> The mark may land in copper, in silkscreen, or elsewhere, and it is unwelcome on a board whose appearance matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Some fabricators offer the option to remove it, and better ones omit it entirely; if a board is a visible front panel, the batch code can be excluded on request.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup>

Ordering a design that was published as a multi-up panel does not oblige the customer to build all of the copies; a panel of four programmers can be ordered when only one is wanted, though snapping the copies apart afterwards requires V-scoring to have been specified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup> Panelization can equally be declined outright — small flexible circuits, for example, may be ordered as single pieces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>

## Panel size limits

The usable panel size is bounded at both ends of the supply chain: the bare-board fabricator states a maximum panel it can process, and the assembler states a maximum the pick and place machine can accept.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup> A panel can be made as large as desired subject to those two limits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1158" title="EEVblog #1158 - How To Create PCB Mod Boards">1158</a></sup> Board outlines that do not tile efficiently may be constrained by panel width at the fabricator or the assembler.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-840" title="EEVblog #840 - Yamaha M3000 Mixing Console Teardown">840</a></sup> A board too large to fit a regular panel is effectively a custom job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup>

## Circuitry on the panel

Because the panel is part of the PCB, traces, connectors and complete circuits can be placed on the frame outside the product outline, and signals routed on and off the individual boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> This turns otherwise wasted frame area into [[production-testing|production test]] infrastructure: traces run from each board out to the panel rails let a whole array be tested while still panelized, without breaking boards out, inserting batteries, and plugging leads into each unit in turn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

The motivating case is instructive. A ten-up panel, arranged 5 by 2, reduced fabrication and assembly cost, but each unit still had to be snapped out, have a [[battery]] fitted, be tested and then have the battery removed again before shipping — a particular nuisance for a product containing a lithium cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Routing the supply and signal connections through the panel frame removes that per-unit handling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Even a modest implementation of this pays for itself at test time, and where such traces are needed the individual board must be routed to bring signals out to its corners before the panel is assembled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Nothing prevents adding active circuitry to the frame, in which case the panel warrants a [[schematic]] of its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> The one constraint is separation method: V-grooving forecloses this option, since the scoring wheel destroys any trace crossing the groove.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

Designing testability into the product or the production panel from the outset is what makes this cheap; retrofitting test to a finished board is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> Below the tens or hundreds of thousands of units that justify a dedicated automated test system, a panel-level test fixture is the practical middle [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## CAD support

Panelization is a distinct CAD operation rather than a matter of copying and pasting board outlines. In Altium Designer the panel is created as a separate PCB inside the same project, and an embedded board array is placed into it with a nominated source PCB file, a column count and a row count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> The array does not render the underlying tracks, silkscreen and other artwork on screen, but it records the placement information so that the [[gerber|Gerber]] generation produces the full panel — a large panel takes correspondingly longer to output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Panelization intent that the fabricator must act on, such as where V-grooving and routing are wanted, is added as lines on a mechanical layer, sometimes called a fab notes layer, alongside written notes the manufacturer reads and follows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> A single design can specify different treatments per edge, for example V-grooving on the top and bottom edges with the sides routed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup>

Doing it manually by copying boards has traps: designators may be automatically incremented on paste, requiring the behaviour to be disabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Packages without a panelization feature leave no alternative to the manual approach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup>

Panelization support is often positioned as a paid or professional-tier feature, on the reasoning that hobby users do not need high-volume manufacturing capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup> Automated panelization functions can be genuinely convenient — specifying a 3 by 3 array with top and bottom rail edges and having the tool calculate the resulting panel size, or checking that a 5 by 5 arrangement fits within the fabricator's maximum panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup> The common shortcoming is that the automation stops short of the details that make a panel manufacturable: an automatically generated panel may arrive with no tooling holes and no fiducials, which will cause trouble at the manufacturer, so the designer must know to add them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup>

## Panelization in practice

Beyond assembly, the panel is the unit of work throughout bare-board fabrication. Photoresist-coated panels are aligned to film on both sides, sometimes with two different panel sizes exposed together for [[efficiency]], and drilling is performed on stacks of several panels taped together and drilled at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup> Final routing cuts the specified panel outline out of the fabricator's larger working sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-939" title="EEVblog #939 - How Is A PCB Manufactured?">939</a></sup>

Finished boards are frequently shipped still attached to their panels, and assembled panels are a normal intermediate stock form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-266" title="EEVblog #266 - Mailbag">266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HyXMhto615c" title="Whatever Happened To The µCurrent?">•</a></sup> Some designers supply their assembler with a purpose-built aluminium frame carrier matched to the panel, which can also substitute for a [[solder-paste|solder paste]] stencil supplied by the assembler.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup> Where a board must be built loose, an assembler can produce a custom carrier routed to the board's shape and mount the pieces in it, at an additional tooling cost that may be justified when a fully routed edge finish is required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup>

Prototype quoting reflects the same structure: a service asks either for the dimensions of the single board or the dimensions of a customer-supplied panel, and if the fabricator is doing the panelization it computes the required area from the single-board size and quotes accordingly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> A representative prototype quote for a 185 by 69 mm design returned a five-board minimum at around thirty-two dollars with a two to three day turn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-990" title="EEVblog #990 - Getting The PCB Manufactured (Nixie PART 5)">990</a></sup>
