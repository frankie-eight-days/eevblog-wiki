Video editing, in the EEVblog production workflow, is the post-shooting stage in which raw camera clips are assembled on a timeline, trimmed, overlaid with a small number of supporting images, and rendered for upload.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454D6aisiAE" title="Why Don&#x27;t I Hire A Video Editor? (AGAIN)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> The defining characteristic of the process is deliberate minimalism: the edit is treated as a cost to be minimised rather than a creative stage, and almost all production decisions are pushed forward into the shooting itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1451" title="EEVblog 1451 - How I Shoot EEVblog Videos">1451</a></sup> The governing rule is that the less time spent editing, the better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1451" title="EEVblog 1451 - How I Shoot EEVblog Videos">1451</a></sup>

## The single-pass timeline workflow

The core loop is unvarying. Clips are shot in the order they will appear, dropped into the timeline in that order, and trimmed at the start and end of each clip; a small number of image or text overlays are added, and the project is rendered and uploaded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454D6aisiAE" title="Why Don&#x27;t I Hire A Video Editor? (AGAIN)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3_CQKSC1JNE" title="Davinci Resolve Speed Editor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1451" title="EEVblog 1451 - How I Shoot EEVblog Videos">1451</a></sup> Because the footage is already in sequence, no assembly or reordering step is required, which is the single largest saving in the workflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454D6aisiAE" title="Why Don&#x27;t I Hire A Video Editor? (AGAIN)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cPuMgh-BElU" title="Davinci Resolve 20: Automatic Silence Removal Tested">•</a></sup> There is no colour grading and no graphical effects work; the operations amount to joining clips, occasional audio-level tweaks, and a few overlays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WLcVcOgmzpE" title="Blackmagic Davinci Resolve CPU vs GPU Rendering">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-698" title="EEVblog #698 - GPU Video Rendering">698</a></sup>

Shooting in sequence is enforced strictly enough to accept redundant physical work during filming. If a close-up lens must be fitted and removed ten times across a shoot to capture successive components on a board, it is fitted and removed ten times, because batching all macro shots for later leaves an unordered pile of clips that costs more time in the timeline than it saved on the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1451" title="EEVblog 1451 - How I Shoot EEVblog Videos">1451</a></sup> Shooting in sequence is described as a big thing, with getting everything right in camera being critical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup>

The timeline itself stays shallow: typically two or three video tracks and rarely more than one audio track, with a second audio channel used only when other footage is being mixed in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Projects with a dozen tracks — heavy text fly-ins and overlays — are the exception rather than the practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup>

Whiteboard tutorials admit one relaxation. With a single board that will not be wiped, a segment can be reshot after editing has begun if something was omitted, and material can be moved earlier in the timeline without a visible discontinuity, provided clothing and framing continuity are preserved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2FbwdnMARAw" title="Behind The Scenes - The Making of an EEVblog Youtube Video">•</a></sup> The same mechanism allows a thought that arrives late in a shoot to be filmed at the end and dropped back into the timeline five or ten minutes earlier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2FbwdnMARAw" title="Behind The Scenes - The Making of an EEVblog Youtube Video">•</a></sup>

## Cutting decisions and the two-pass refinement

The first pass is pure trimming of clip heads and tails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup> A second pass, adopted after roughly a decade of single-pass editing, goes back through the material to remove filler: ums and ahs, pauses, repeated statements, redundant information, and waffle judged not to be helping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1v2_h-_YHEE" title="eevBLAB #71: Audience Retention + My Editing Process">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup> The yield is modest — around one to two minutes off a 25-to-30-minute programme — and it does not quite double editing time, but the effect accumulates across a body of work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1v2_h-_YHEE" title="eevBLAB #71: Audience Retention + My Editing Process">•</a></sup> Earlier material was not treated this way; repetition was simply left in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1198" title="EEVblog #1198 - 10 Years On Youtube!">1198</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup>

Cuts are frequently made from the audio [[waveform]] alone, without playback. A repeated passage or a fluffed take is recognisable by its shape on the timeline, and the cut is executed with a single hotkey bound to the slash key, taking seconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4Up3dshwg8Q" title="Behind The Scenes: How I Make Whiteboard Tutorial Youtube Videos">•</a></sup> The same shortcut is why a 29-minute single-take whiteboard shoot reduces to 19 minutes with essentially no content removed — the ten minutes are goofs, double takes, misstatements and changes of direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4Up3dshwg8Q" title="Behind The Scenes: How I Make Whiteboard Tutorial Youtube Videos">•</a></sup>

Information is preserved in preference to brevity. Raw footage is included by default, and material is only tightened where it is waffle rather than substance: 22 minutes of raw footage might reduce to 18, and 45 minutes to about 35.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Where an explanation genuinely requires the running time, it is kept even at forty minutes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-b-v3tz6beN4" title="The Signal Path - Online vs Real World Education &amp; Stories">•</a></sup> The one length-driven intervention is reshooting a passage more concisely — condensing a two-minute explanation into one — to bring a finished piece under the 30-minute or 20-minute psychological thresholds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2FbwdnMARAw" title="Behind The Scenes - The Making of an EEVblog Youtube Video">•</a></sup>

## Editing time

Reported effort is roughly proportional to runtime, with a ratio near or slightly above 1:1. A 30-minute piece takes 45 minutes to an hour; an hour-long piece takes two hours at the outside and often much less; an hour of raw footage takes about an hour and a half.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454D6aisiAE" title="Why Don&#x27;t I Hire A Video Editor? (AGAIN)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> In the earliest years the ratio was far worse: ten minutes of filming could take an hour or two to edit, plus format conversion, upload and posting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-74" title="EEVblog #74 - Video Blogging Tools">74</a></sup> Overhead is dominated not by cutting but by gathering supporting material — locating data sheets and screen captures to overlay — rather than by the trims themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup>

Clip count, not runtime, is the real driver of editing labour. [[multimeter]] shootouts shot as short repeated tests produced 150 and over 200 individual clips, each needing its own start-and-end trim, and took hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup> Compilation work is worse still: assembling a retrospective requires loading many full-length source programmes rather than isolated clips, which historically brought the editing machine to a crawl and took a week for a hundred-episode compilation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

## Overlays and supporting material

Overlays are the one production flourish considered worth the time: [[datasheet|data sheet]] captures, screen grabs, and annotated stills — for example an image with a red circle drawn around the [[mosfet]] under discussion plus a line of text.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1v2_h-_YHEE" title="eevBLAB #71: Audience Retention + My Editing Process">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Each is quick individually but the total adds up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1v2_h-_YHEE" title="eevBLAB #71: Audience Retention + My Editing Process">•</a></sup> On a whiteboard tutorial, sourcing and preparing overlay images can take longer than the cutting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4Up3dshwg8Q" title="Behind The Scenes: How I Make Whiteboard Tutorial Youtube Videos">•</a></sup> Text overlays, and in extremis a voice-over, also serve to patch in information realised to be missing during the edit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2FbwdnMARAw" title="Behind The Scenes - The Making of an EEVblog Youtube Video">•</a></sup>

Synchronising independent sources is a separate class of work. Two dash-cam feeds from a vehicle — one forward-looking, one on the driver — were inlaid and aligned by hand against a known event to produce a single combined view.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1066" title="EEVblog #1066 - Uber Autonomous Car Accident - LIDAR Failed?">1066</a></sup> Live-show post-production requires the chat log to be synced against the audio, for which a timestamp is noted during recording so the alignment point can be found again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Separate video feeds from a remote guest must be individually cut and rescaled to size the guest larger than the host.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XAbeXAFpyh0" title="Here&#x27;s How I SCREWED UP a Live Interview">•</a></sup> A green screen was considered and rejected on the grounds that it is difficult to get right, messy, and adds editing work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ti8mbPIAvbI" title="New EEVblog Lab Tour (Incomplete Edition)">•</a></sup>

## Unedited output

Not all material is edited. Single-take formats are recorded, stopped, and uploaded straight from the card with no editing, rendering, transcoding, or format conversion, specifically as a way to produce content quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9_qhlFc-MVU" title="eevBLAB #1 - The EEVblog Is Hiring">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Unpolished material is published without any spit and polish in the editing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Continuous off-the-cuff monologue recordings were an early experiment in exactly this, presented as unedited in contrast to the normal heavily edited segment-by-segment construction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-62" title="EEVblog #62 - The Drivetime Experiment">62</a></sup> The distinction is maintained deliberately: polished material receives the full two-step editing process, while quick material bypasses it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-T5cRvs44LcE" title="EEVblog Survey Results - 2021">•</a></sup>

## Verification and irreversibility

Individual clips are not watched through and analysed during editing; doing so would take two or three times longer than trimming.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-76" title="EEVblog #76 - Publish And Be Damned!">76</a></sup> The finished render is not reviewed at all. The justification is that trimming every clip amounts to having watched the whole thing already, leaving high confidence that nothing is badly wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454D6aisiAE" title="Why Don&#x27;t I Hire A Video Editor? (AGAIN)">•</a></sup>

This tolerance is reinforced by the cost of correction after publication. Re-filming and re-uploading a piece to fix a minor error destroys the existing links, resets the view count, and disturbs the ordering of the archive, so published material is generally not revisited.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-76" title="EEVblog #76 - Publish And Be Damned!">76</a></sup> Errors are handled by correction in the accompanying text rather than by re-editing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-76" title="EEVblog #76 - Publish And Be Damned!">76</a></sup>

## Delegation

Handing editing to someone else is judged unworkable on time grounds rather than quality grounds: reviewing another editor's cut of an hour of material takes about as long as editing that hour personally, which eliminates the saving entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XzmuKIYZkNo" title="Zencaster Video Beta Test with Chris Gammell">•</a></sup> A second objection is stylistic — externally edited material would take on the character of a highly produced, polished house style rather than the existing one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Professionally produced and edited programmes of that kind carry very large budgets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> The practical answer is that shooting in sequence makes the edit short enough that delegation is not worth the overhead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454D6aisiAE" title="Why Don&#x27;t I Hire A Video Editor? (AGAIN)">•</a></sup> Editing work has, however, been brought in-house through hiring.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1763" title="EEVblog 1763 - Meet Dylan">1763</a></sup>

Editing is also acknowledged as a tool of misrepresentation: with enough research and selective cutting, material can be assembled so that its presenter appears to know everything off the top of their head.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

## Software

The editing application has changed several times. Sony Movie Studio was used for years and was chosen for workflow fit and benchmarked performance rather than for feature depth, on the reasoning that the projects are untaxing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-698" title="EEVblog #698 - GPU Video Rendering">698</a></sup> The workflow later moved from Vegas to DaVinci Resolve, which became the standard tool.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4Up3dshwg8Q" title="Behind The Scenes: How I Make Whiteboard Tutorial Youtube Videos">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3_CQKSC1JNE" title="Davinci Resolve Speed Editor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup>

A dedicated hardware speed editor was evaluated against this workflow. Because the operations reduce to placing already-ordered clips on the timeline, setting in and out points, ripple deleting, and start/stop, only about ten percent of the panel's keys would see use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3_CQKSC1JNE" title="Davinci Resolve Speed Editor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> Automatic silence removal — automatic detection and ripple deletion of gaps — was similarly assessed as unsuitable for main content, applicable at most to long material destined for quick publication, and dangerous if applied across an entire timeline rather than per clip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cPuMgh-BElU" title="Davinci Resolve 20: Automatic Silence Removal Tested">•</a></sup>

By contrast, mid-1990s non-linear editing hardware costing tens of thousands of dollars was limited to motion JPEG and 640 by 480 titling, which was state of the art for the period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-969" title="EEVblog #969 - Vintage $80k NLE Video Editor Teardown">969</a></sup>

## Hardware and media handling

Footage is shot as AVCHD at 1920 by 1080, around 28 Mbit/s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Files are copied off the SD card before work begins; editing directly from the card is too slow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Mixed-source projects require reconciling cameras running at different frame rates — 60 and 50 frames per second — with output at 50, which cannot simply be dropped onto one timeline without a few minutes of extra handling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup>

Storage is a four-bay NAS populated with 6 TB drives in a single-redundancy array, giving 18 TB usable, with editing performed directly across a gigabit Ethernet link to the unit rather than from local copies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-taKKSSi0Td8" title="Synology NAS FAIL Adventure">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XsR_5-FJ05M" title="Synology NAS Western Digital RED Hard Drive FAIL">•</a></sup> Working files and raw footage both live there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XsR_5-FJ05M" title="Synology NAS Western Digital RED Hard Drive FAIL">•</a></sup>

The editing workstation runs three monitors, with a 1080p display used for capture and general browsing and a larger-real-estate display used for the timeline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qjLXJl0kDjg" title="How to fix Windows blurry font scaling problems">•</a></sup> GPU-accelerated rendering roughly halves render time on this kind of simple, overlay-light content.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WLcVcOgmzpE" title="Blackmagic Davinci Resolve CPU vs GPU Rendering">•</a></sup> Memory requirements are correspondingly modest: 8 GB is genuinely sufficient for this class of editing and 16 GB provides comfortable margin, with 32 GB fitted only for parity with the desktop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p287qKMXqxQ" title="Lenovo Legion 5i 17&quot; Ryzen 7 RTX2060 Laptop">•</a></sup> [[battery-life]] is the real constraint on mobile editing — 4K editing on a discrete-GPU laptop drains it in barely two hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zNG_oNhKCBg" title="Lenovo Legion 5 Laptop M.2 SSD Memory">•</a></sup> An SD card slot on a machine is valued specifically for ingest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1695" title="EEVblog 1695 - Mailbag: Robin RCD Tester, PCB Book, USB Knob, Geekom IT12 Mini PC">1695</a></sup> Editing has at various times been done at a lab, at a satellite office, and at home, with the SD card physically carried between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Transferring several gigabytes of part-edited project to a [[usb]] stick to continue elsewhere can itself take twenty minutes and must be planned for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

## Monitoring

Because editing sessions run to many hours and some projects contain hundreds of clips, audio monitoring is a working concern rather than an audiophile one; headphones were the long-standing default before powered studio monitors were sought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-169" title="EEVblog #169 - Samson StudioDock 4i USB Monitor Speaker Review">169</a></sup> Small monitor speakers have been assessed specifically for voicing on video editing work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1162" title="EEVblog #1162 - Little British Monitors">1162</a></sup>
