Slew rate is the maximum rate at which a circuit's output can change, expressed in volts per microsecond for amplifiers and logic, or amps per microsecond for [[current]] sources and loads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> It is distinct from [[bandwidth]]: bandwidth describes small-signal [[frequency-response|frequency response]], while slew rate is a large-signal limit on how fast an output can traverse its range, and a part can have adequate bandwidth yet still be unable to move a signal from rail to rail in the required time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup> Slew rate governs whether a [[comparator]]-style output arrives on time, whether a peak detector catches its peak, whether a logic input crosses its threshold cleanly, and how much high-[[frequency]] energy a nominally slow signal actually contains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-316" title="EEVblog #316 - More PFANG, More 13GHz Scope, &amp; More Pulser">316</a></sup>

## Slew rate in operational amplifiers

Jellybean op-amps are slow. The LM358 specifies 0.5 V per microsecond, so a 0 to 5 V swing — the kind of transition wanted from a 5 V comparator — takes about 10 microseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> This is the standard trap in pressing an op-amp into service as a comparator feeding a gate or flip-flop; the slew limit is compounded by overload recovery time, since an op-amp is not designed to have its inputs and outputs slammed non-linearly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup> The TL071 family sits somewhat above the LM358, with a reasonably high slew rate alongside 1 mV [[offset-voltage|offset voltage]] and 2 µV/°C offset drift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1436" title="EEVBlog 1436 - The TOP 5 Jellybean OPAMP&#x27;s">1436</a></sup>

The spread across the market is enormous. A [[datasheet]] comparison between two shunt-amplifier candidates put one at 20 V per microsecond against the other's 1.6 V per microsecond, more than an order of magnitude apart, tracking the bandwidth difference between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup> At the extreme, the BUF634 buffer specifies 2,000 V per microsecond along with 250 mA of drive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1609" title="EEVblog 1609 - Composite Amplifier Tutorial + Practical Demo">1609</a></sup> Wideband parts buy that speed with design compromises elsewhere: a fast op-amp's input architecture is optimised for bandwidth and slew rate rather than input current, which is why its [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] can be a couple of microamps where a precision instrumentation-grade part is in the tens of femtoamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup>

Slew rate is also a specification that can move under a design's feet. A revision of the NE5532 improved gain bandwidth product while reducing the typical slew rate from 9 V per microsecond to 5, with no change to the part number — the same silicon revision also dropped the maximum output swing versus bandwidth figure, the output [[impedance]] spec, and the crosstalk attenuation spec from the datasheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup>

## Charging capacitance: the peak detector case

A peak detector makes the consequence of finite slew rate visible. When a new peak arrives, the op-amp must charge the hold [[capacitor]], and it cannot do so instantly; the output ramps rather than stepping, so the captured value lags the true peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> Increasing the hold capacitor to reduce droop makes this worse — a very large [[capacitance]] cannot be charged quickly by a slow amplifier, so droop and acquisition speed are in direct conflict and the capacitor value is a compromise, not a free choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>

Pushed hard enough, the circuit drops peaks entirely: the amplifier begins to slew back up towards a peak, the input has already moved on before the capacitor is charged, and pulses go missing from the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> At low input frequencies the same circuit tracks fine, chopping up and following the input [[waveform]], because the signal is far slower than the amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>

## Digital logic and metastability

Any digital input without a Schmitt trigger — [[microcontroller]] inputs, [[fpga|FPGA]] inputs, ordinary logic gates — is susceptible to input slew rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> An input that changes too slowly can drive a flip-flop or counter into a metastable state, in which the output is indeterminate and the device may register multiple clock pulses, skip pulses, or produce none, appearing as erratic counting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> A representative failure: an open-collector [[optocoupler]] output pulled up to VCC, where the passive rising edge is not sharp but a slew that can be in the order of microseconds, set by the pull-up value against the total load capacitance — the driven chip's [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]], the [[breadboard]] or [[pcb-trace|PCB trace]] capacitance, and any other components on the bus.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> The same node driven from a proper CMOS or TTL output presents no such problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

Load capacitance accumulates faster than expected. A typical logic input is about 3 pF, so driving several gates from one output triples or quadruples the load and directly degrades the output's transition rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> The effect scales up across a board: daisy-chained boards driven from a single Raspberry Pi output can present more line capacitance than the driver can handle, slowing the edges, eroding [[voltage]]-level margin, and causing boards progressively further along the chain to fail to clock data in reliably — the fix being a buffer chip on each board to redrive the outbound signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

Pull-up selection on an open-collector bus is therefore a speed decision, not just a power one. Lower pull-up values give faster edges and more achievable I²C bus speed, at the cost of higher current, which is a trade-off worth spending a BOM line on rather than accepting whatever value a design tool suggests.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Some microcontrollers expose the same trade-off directly as a selectable output drive or slew capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1140" title="EEVblog #1140 - 3 CENT Micro LED Blinky with ICE!">1140</a></sup>

## Slew rate, rise time and bandwidth

A signal's frequency is a poor guide to the bandwidth needed to handle it; what matters is the rising edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-316" title="EEVblog #316 - More PFANG, More 13GHz Scope, &amp; More Pulser">316</a></sup> A 1 kHz pulse train with a fast enough edge is a high-speed signal in every sense that counts for transmission-line matching, output impedance, and attenuator behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-316" title="EEVblog #316 - More PFANG, More 13GHz Scope, &amp; More Pulser">316</a></sup> A [[square-wave|square wave]] or step contains high-frequency components in proportion to its slew rate, which is why step response reveals [[overshoot]], [[ringing]] and termination effects that a ramp waveform never shows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup> Fast-rise-time pulse generators exist precisely to exercise this property in a system under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup>

The conversion is direct: an edge of [[rise-time|rise time]] tr has an equivalent bandwidth of roughly 1/(π·tr), so 8 ns slew rates correspond to about 40 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> This is the figure that matters for common-mode rejection — differential probes with excellent [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] at 50 Hz are useless by 10 MHz, so a fast common-mode edge appears in the measurement even though its repetition rate is low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup>

The same reasoning applies to power rails. Ringing and transients on a rail run to hundreds of megahertz, set by the slew rate of the circuit drawing current, and even ordinary microcontroller switching produces such content — which is why rail fidelity measurements demand high bandwidth and are taken at timebases in the tens of nanoseconds per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup>

## Measurement and instrument control

Oscilloscopes expose slew rate as a direct automatic measurement — a rate-of-change parameter alongside rise time, [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]] and voltage extremes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Power-analysis application packages list slew rate among their measurements together with switching loss, power quality, transient response, turn-on and turn-off time, [[output-ripple|output ripple]] and PSRR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup> Mask and limit-test engines can be set to alarm on slow rising edges, catching a slew rate that drifts over a run of minutes, hours or days, alongside runt-pulse and glitch tests.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup>

Programmable sources and loads increasingly make slew rate a settable parameter rather than a fixed characteristic. A DC [[electronic-load|electronic load]] in constant-current mode allows the rising and falling slope to be set — 5 A per microsecond at the high end, and rates as low as 0.01 A per millisecond at the low end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> On some loads slew rate is a licensed option, bundled with high resolution and [[frequency-measurement|frequency measurement]] as the difference between model variants.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> Programmable supplies expose slew rate in volts per second, which is what [[battery]] emulation requires in order to reproduce a source's real dynamic behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y2rcx4vKxlc" title="Unboxing the Keysight E36731A DC Power Supply, Electronic Load, and Battery Emulator">•</a></sup>

## Slew rate outside electronics

The concept carries into electromechanical output stages. A studio monitor's woofer amplifier can be specified at 9 volts per millisecond, and for a given cone and a given power the slew rate sets whether the cone can physically be accelerated fast enough — the cone's inertia is the limit being fought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1156" title="EEVblog #1156 - Focal Professional CMS40 Studio Monitor Teardown">1156</a></sup> Slew rate is one contributor among bandwidth and general amplifier performance, not the sole determinant of one loudspeaker over another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1156" title="EEVblog #1156 - Focal Professional CMS40 Studio Monitor Teardown">1156</a></sup>
