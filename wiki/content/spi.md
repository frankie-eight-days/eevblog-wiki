The Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI) is a synchronous serial bus used to connect a [[microcontroller]] to peripheral chips over a small number of pins: a clock line, a chip select, and two data lines, making four wires in the usual case and occasionally three.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> It is a master-slave arrangement in which all communication is coordinated by a single master; the slave never initiates a transfer, and the only line it drives is MISO, the master-input slave-output, responding synchronously to the clock the master supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Because it costs so few pins and so little silicon, SPI is close to universal on modern microcontrollers — it and a UART are effectively a given on almost any part worth considering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1524" title="EEVblog 1524 - The 10 CENT RISC V Processor! CH32V003">1524</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup>

## Signalling

A typical SPI frame consists of a clock that runs up and down, an active-low select line, and data lines whose transitions are synchronised to the clock — either to the rising edge or to the falling edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Two configuration parameters complete the picture. Polarity describes the idle state of the clock, the logic level it returns to when the master is not communicating with any device: polarity zero idles low before and after frames, polarity one idles high, and the two are simply inversions of each other, so the first edge under polarity zero is a rising edge and under polarity one a falling edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Phase determines when data is clocked in and out; a phase of one shifts the data by half the clock period relative to phase zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> The point at which a receiver should sample is the middle of the bit, not its edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup>

The four combinations give the four SPI modes. Many chips support only two of them, and not modes zero and one but pairs — everything at polarity zero or everything at polarity one — which is why data sheets commonly list the supported configuration as polarity zero with phase zero, or polarity one with phase one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> Getting these modes right is part of the general discipline of reading timing diagrams, and is unavoidable for anyone implementing a serial protocol in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1249" title="EEVblog #1249 - TUTORIAL: Timing Diagrams Explained">1249</a></sup>

Multiple devices normally hang off one common bus and are distinguished by separate chip select lines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup> An unselected device places its pins in high [[impedance]], so from the master's point of view it is as though it were wired directly to the one slave it has chosen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup>

## Three-wire variants

Not every interface described as SPI carries four wires. A three-wire implementation replaces the separate MOSI and MISO pair with a single bidirectional data line that tri-states as direction changes, which costs throughput but saves a pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> The interface remains SPI in every other respect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> Sharp's memory LCDs use a three-wire interface, which is what allows them to be driven from a microcontroller costing a few cents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Where read-back is not needed at all, only clock, MOSI and chip select need be routed, as with the Holtek 1622 [[lcd]] driver — three lines from the microcontroller to the display controller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup>

## Bit banging and hardware peripherals

Hardware SPI peripherals on a microcontroller are frequently multiplexed onto pins wanted for other purposes, in particular the in-system programming pins. Where those pins are needed elsewhere, the interface can be bit banged in software on ordinary I/O with no real penalty, provided the software is fast enough for the [[sample-rate|sampling rate]] required — an ADC and DAC polled at a few hundred hertz to a kilohertz is well within reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> Some very cheap parts have no hardware SPI at all and leave bit banging as the only option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup> Writing a bit banged implementation is also a good way to force a full understanding of the protocol, and a bit banged port can be emulated in C++ on a PC and its output compared against what a logic analyser captures from real hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9hMsNOwY5AQ" title="EEVacademy #3 - Bit Banging &amp; SPI Tutorial">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fm13tIe5wSc" title="EEVacademy #4 -  I²C (I2C) Bit Banging">•</a></sup>

Speed is not unlimited in practice. An SPI port is often deliberately clocked below its maximum to keep the link reliable, and a 3D printer controller feeding an SD card can end up barely keeping up with the card's data rate as a result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-843" title="EEVblog #843 - David&#x27;s rPrint 3D Printer Design">843</a></sup>

## Pin budget and the trade against I²C

The main design tension is pins. An SPI DAC and an SPI ADC each need clock, chip select and data; where enough pins are available they can simply be driven separately, but the clock and data-in lines can also be shared between them to free pins up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> The deeper alternative is to abandon SPI for I²C, which needs only two lines regardless of how many devices are attached. Consolidating a bit banged SPI DAC and other peripherals onto an I²C bus can free three microcontroller pins at once, eliminate a second I/O expander, and cost a few cents less in parts — a change that cascades favourably through a whole design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> The counter-argument is overhead: I²C carries protocol overhead that is unwelcome when a converter is being sampled continuously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup>

Layout is a second consideration in SPI's favour. Because the bus is only three lines, the microcontroller can sit wherever its analog peripherals want it — near the analog section, for instance — while the display controller sits at the far side of the board, with only the SPI bus running between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup>

## Applications

SPI is the default control and data path for a wide range of peripherals. Programmable-gain amplifiers in [[oscilloscope]] front ends take their gain settings over SPI: the LMH6518 is a 900 MHz programmable gain amplifier whose gain is SPI-controlled and which contains internal [[bandwidth]] limiting at roughly 60, 100, 200 and 350 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> The near-universal 20 MHz bandwidth limit found on almost every oscilloscope is very probably implemented by writing to such a chip, and so is software-licensed bandwidth upgrading — an instrument's base 50 MHz and its 300 MHz top end differing only by the commands sent to the front-end amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e4wvxWWMla0" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 - Like a Bought One">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wWymcaiaQXM" title="What happened to my Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202?">•</a></sup> In one such part the interface is three-wire, with chip select, SDIO and S-clock on pins 9, 10 and 11, and the write transaction is 24 bits: an 8-bit command field followed by 16 bits of data, sampled on the positive-going edge of the clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup> Tapping that bus is the entry point for changing the gain — and by extension the bandwidth — to something other than what was paid for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup>

Sensors commonly present an SPI output. Automotive airbag accelerometers such as the Freescale MMA 6821 and MMA 6856 are SPI-based two-axis medium-g over-damped lateral devices spanning ±20 g to ±120 g with 10-bit signed or unsigned SPI output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup> A 14-bit Hall-effect rotational sensor likewise delivers position over SPI.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DX_f0Cg6pHo" title="How a 4-20mA Process Control Current Loop Rotational Sensor Works">•</a></sup> An accelerometer may offer both I²C up to 400 kHz and SPI up to 20 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> The Lepton thermal imaging module streams video over SPI rather than over MIPI, with chip select, clock and MOSI connected but no MISO, since nothing needs to travel back into the module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup>

SPI also serves as a general-purpose expansion path. A microcontroller with no Ethernet MAC can gain one through an ENC28J60 SPI-to-Ethernet converter, for which Linux builds on single-board computers carry a driver; the result works but is limited by the bus, and is nowhere near as fast as native Ethernet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-934" title="EEVblog #934 - Raspberry Pi Supercomputer Cluster PART 1">934</a></sup> A W5200-based module offers the same in a 3.3 V SPI package with a power-down pin — useful in a [[battery]]-powered design — drawing at most 120 mA at full speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Serial ROM and boot flash are frequently on SPI, which is what makes in-circuit reflashing possible through a programming header without desoldering the chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup>

For galvanic isolation, dedicated transceivers such as the LT6820 carry a fully SPI-compliant interface across a single isolated twisted pair, one device at each end, replacing what would otherwise be multiple optocouplers on multiple lines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-bg6QsTT0Plw" title="Laser Driver PCB &amp; Circuit Review">•</a></sup>

## Debugging SPI

Because most digital debugging now involves serial buses rather than wide parallel ones, SPI is one of the two or three protocols that instrument decode support is really bought for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 - Part 2 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Deep [[sample-memory]] is what makes an oscilloscope useful here, allowing long captures to be taken and then zoomed into for fine detail and glitches.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13" title="EEVblog #13 Part 1 of 2 - Digital storage Oscilloscope Tutorial">13</a></sup> Serial decode is very often a paid software option rather than a standard feature, and some otherwise expensive instruments can trigger on serial buses while being unable to decode them at all, leaving that to a separate logic analyser module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> Where decoding is implemented in dedicated hardware rather than on the instrument's CPU, it runs in real time without slowing the scope down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup>

A practical constraint is channel count. A four-channel SPI bus consumes every analog input on a four-channel scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Chip select timeout modes address this: the decoder infers frame boundaries from an idle period on the clock instead of requiring a dedicated chip select channel, so an analog channel need not be sacrificed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> Triggering has a similar wrinkle — a decoder that triggers only on data content may be useless when what is wanted is a trigger on chip select, in which case an ordinary edge trigger on that channel is the answer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>

Low-cost [[usb]] logic analysers are adequate for capturing SPI and similar low-speed serial traffic, and with eight channels can capture several independent buses simultaneously since the decoding happens in software after the fact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-436" title="EEVblog #436 - Saleae USB Logic Analyser Review &amp; Teardown">436</a></sup> Protocol generation is the complementary capability: some instruments will source SPI, I²C and UART patterns, at rates up to 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-991" title="EEVblog #991 - Mailbag">991</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fM4bbKVw2og" title="Uni-T 500MHz 4CH ARB Signal Generator Unboxing">•</a></sup>

## Failure modes

SPI failures are often initialisation or protocol-stack problems rather than electrical ones. In one case a soft processor in an [[fpga]] had to send a command over SPI at power-up to place a codec into the required mode; when nothing appeared on the audio bus, probing showed the SPI bus itself was at fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup> The cause traced to an SPI multiplexer in a second FPGA and a compiler switch that selected multiplexed operation when the two devices were in fact talking directly, with a protocol stack insufficiently robust against FPGA timing variation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup> Repeatedly asserting a reset could provoke activity on the SPI and audio buses, but only garbage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-58" title="EEVblog #58 - Warm and Fuzzy FPGA Troubleshooting">58</a></sup>

Bus-level anomalies are worth taking seriously when captured. Observing write transactions occurring with no chip select assertion, and cycle counts well in excess of the 24 the device's read and write operations are specified to take, is strong evidence that another device is sharing the bus and the chip under observation is simply not being selected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Hacking Extended Version">879</a></sup>

Some scope-side oddities are the instrument's fault rather than the bus's. SPI signals fed into a scope in run mode have been observed to appear and vanish spontaneously while the same signals decode correctly on other instruments, with single-shot capture working every time — a behaviour tied to long intervals between triggers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UDGsZcAWgL8" title="Rigol MSO5000 Bug Bonanza">•</a></sup>
