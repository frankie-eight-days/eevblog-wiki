EEPROM — electrically erasable programmable read-only memory, commonly written E²PROM or E-squared PROM — is non-volatile memory that can be erased and rewritten electrically, in circuit, without the ultraviolet window or the mask step that earlier read-only memories required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> Its defining property in practice is granularity: unlike a mask ROM or an EPROM, an EEPROM can have a single byte changed and retains it with the power removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup> That combination makes it the default place a product keeps the handful of values it must not forget — [[calibration]] constants, user settings, serial numbers, PIN codes — and an 8-pin EEPROM sitting beside a [[microcontroller]] is one of the most reliably identifiable parts on a consumer or instrument board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup>

## Place in the memory lineage

EEPROM arrived between EPROM and flash and was superseded by flash for program storage. Microchip moved first from EPROM to E-squared, producing the PIC16C84 as a byte-programmable part, then introduced flash-based products around the 1995 time frame.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup> The transition mattered because field programmability commanded only a modest price premium over mask ROM while collapsing time to market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d1D3d3YIc9A" title="Talking with Steve Sanghi. 31 years as CEO of Microchip">•</a></sup> E-squared was never made in large capacities, and flash supplanted it within a few years for bulk program memory, but it enabled a generation of early microcontrollers along the way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup>

Before it existed, designs had no solid-state writable store at all: a portable computer of the era kept everything in volatile [[sram]] held up by a backup [[battery]], with a memory-protect switch as the only safeguard against losing a week's work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup> Machines from that period used mask ROM soldered directly to the board — in one case not even socketed, so upgrading the BIOS meant desoldering a chip from a multilayer board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> A one-time-programmable microcontroller with no internal EEPROM forces the same architecture from the other direction: the code has to live somewhere external, which is why an OTP part is so often found paired with a separate serial EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup>

Development parts show the economics of the transition. The 8748, the erasable version of the 8048, was expensive enough that it might have been expected to stay on the bench, yet it went into production because nothing else could do the job at the price — the alternative was a minicomputer costing around 5K in period dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup>

## Serial EEPROMs on a board

The overwhelmingly common form is a small serial device in an SO8 package. The I²C family carries 24C or 24LC part numbers — a 24C01 holding settings in a breast pump, a 24C16 alongside a dSPIC in a [[soldering-iron]] station, a Microchip 24LC128 in an intercom storing the apartment names programmed in from the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Microwire parts carry 93C numbers, such as an Atmel 93C46 in a film camera that remembers which frame the roll is up to so a battery change does not lose the count, or a 93C64 in an electronic safe lock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1440" title="EEVblog 1440 - Pentax Film Camera TEARDOWN">1440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> Older parts predate both conventions: an ER1400 of 1986 vintage is a 1400-bit serial EEPROM from General Instruments, the company that became Microchip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup>

The package alone identifies the function with reasonable confidence, and the surrounding passives confirm it. An 8-pin device with two resistors pulling lines up toward the supply pin, and a bypass cap across pins 4 and 8, is almost certainly an I²C EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Xg_niU86bhI" title="Wens 540 Handheld Oscilloscope Multimeter Teardown">•</a></sup> In one [[multimeter]] the pull-ups were marked 103 — 10k, a typical I²C pull-up value — which together with the pin 8 supply confirmed the interface before the part number was read; the device turned out to be a Rohm BR24L, a 2K part whose LO2 marking is the product code for the SOP8 package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> Because pins 4 and 8 are known power rails on these parts, the EEPROM also makes a convenient probe point for verifying a board's supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

Instruments of the socketed era often left the part removable, either in a plain DIP socket or a zero insertion force socket, which is what allows a field technician to pull the chip and drop in a replacement to upgrade [[firmware]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1412" title="EEVblog 1412 - Argon Ion Laser TEARDOWN!">1412</a></sup>

## What gets stored

**Calibration.** Storing calibration in EEPROM is what makes closed-case, software calibration possible; a multimeter chipset either has EEPROM built in for the calibration functions or uses an external part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> A bare-bones chipset without it is forced back onto physical trim pots, which is a poor choice in a modern meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Calibration values in EEPROM survive with no battery at all, avoiding the maintenance problem of battery-backed calibration RAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1012" title="EEVblog #1012 - Best Bargain Ebay Bench Meter? - Fluke 8842A">1012</a></sup> Instruments across the price range follow the pattern: a Fluke 45 uses a separate National Semiconductor 63C46 to hold the cal values, a Fluke 8842A a Zilog part, an ANENG 626 and a $25 no-name meter both put calibration and setup values in an external EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1012" title="EEVblog #1012 - Best Bargain Ebay Bench Meter? - Fluke 8842A">1012</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> The Prema 6047 goes further and has the factory calibration values programmed into its EEPROM at manufacture, so that when the battery-backed SRAM holding the working calibration fails, a switch on the back triggers a mode that copies the factory contents into the new SRAM — the documented recovery path when the battery dies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup>

The write only happens when the procedure says it does. Exiting a calibration sequence partway through on the assumption that completed steps have already been committed loses them; nothing is stored until the procedure completes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup>

Where calibration lives internally instead, in the protected region of the meter chipset, it becomes inseparable from the part: firmware can be updated without disturbing the constants, but physically swapping the chip loses them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

**Settings and state.** A meter that returns to the range it was left in — DC volts, [[capacitance]] rather than [[diode]] — is reading that back out of EEPROM at power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> A bench supply that comes back with its previous [[voltage]] and [[current]] settings intact, but with the output deliberately left off, is doing the same.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup> A coin validator holds the parameter set for the coins it is configured to accept.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-363" title="EEVblog #363 - Gold Phone Teardown">363</a></sup> A clamp meter's default AC/DC behaviour and auto-zero can be altered by reflashing its EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dv0B_WqL7w4" title="UniT 210E Current Clamp Teardown + Brymen Comparison">•</a></sup> An external EEPROM invites exactly this kind of modification, since its contents can be read and rewritten without touching the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup>

**Identity.** A 1-kilobyte EEPROM identification chip carrying a unique laser-etched ID from the factory, fitted to every board manufactured, gives per-unit traceability with no two chips alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-134" title="EEVblog #134 - The Maxim Manipulation">134</a></sup> A battery analyser adapter module carries an EEPROM purely so the instrument auto-identifies which of over 3,200 modules is plugged in and infers the cell configuration and chemistry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup> Product ID is a common single purpose in itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup> The same idea appears at the consumable level, where a label cartridge tag combines anti-collision and access control with EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1462" title="EEVblog 1462 - Why Dymo Label Printers SUCK!">1462</a></sup>

**Security data.** A high-security electronic lock holds its combination in a secure EEPROM rather than in volatile memory, which is why the on-board supercap is not there to preserve the code.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1484" title="EEVblog 1484 - Kaba Mas X-09 High Security Electronic Lock Teardown">1484</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> Another lock keeps the PIN in an external 1K serial EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> A cryptographic coprocessor allocates 10k bits of EEPROM to key and certificate data alongside high-endurance monotonic counters and a guaranteed unique 72-bit serial number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Yky9yQwZALM" title="Shift Cryptosecurity BitBox02 Crypto Hardware Wallet">•</a></sup> An airbag controller writes crash data to EEPROM for later readout by investigators, and its [[capacitor]] reservoir is protected precisely to guarantee that write completes during the event.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup>

**Bulk logging.** Where the data set is small and slow, EEPROM is the whole storage system: a temperature and voltage logger scaling up to a 512K-bit external EEPROM stores about 65,000 single-byte readings, with sample intervals from 28 seconds to 24 hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-294" title="EEVblog #294 - Mailbag">294</a></sup>

## Endurance, write protection, and attack surface

EEPROM cells wear out, and a design that writes on every user action can exhaust one. Partitioning the data across two devices addresses this: in one multimeter, U4 takes the frequently rewritten last-mode value while U5 holds only the calibration data, so wearing out the mode chip by pressing a button a million times costs the user the mode memory and nothing else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> The second chip additionally has its write-protect pin brought out to a jumper link, so calibration cannot be erased by a software bug regardless of what the firmware does — a deliberate expense on a design that could have saved the part by storing both at different addresses in one device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup>

Finite endurance is also a weak defence against brute force. Where a hardware wallet stores its failed-PIN state in EEPROM and lengthens the retry delay exponentially, resetting that stored value through the debug port would defeat the backoff — though the device may simply exhaust its write endurance before a long PIN space is searched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1006" title="EEVblog #1006 - Trezor Bitcoin Hardware Wallet Teardown">1006</a></sup> The topology matters as much as the contents: if a lock's code could be read out of an EEPROM hanging on the same bus as the keypad, the exploit would already be well known, which is reason enough to expect it is not wired that way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup>

The rewritable-in-place property can also be turned against the user. Because a [[usb]] interface chip stores its product ID in internal EEPROM, driver software that rewrites the PID to zero bricks the part permanently — removing power and replugging does not restore it, and the operating system can no longer enumerate the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-676" title="EEVblog #676 - RANT: FTDI Bricking Counterfeit Chips!">676</a></sup>

## Programming, reading, and reverse engineering

A socketed EEPROM is an open door. Pulling one from a telephone system and reading it in a universal programmer yields the code, and with it embedded serial command strings, debug entry points, and service menus.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-371" title="EEVblog #371 - Universal Programmer Teardown">371</a></sup> Universal programmers are frequently known as EEPROM programmers, though they handle PROMs, microcontrollers, GALs and PALs as well; a dedicated EEPROM programmer covers only the memories and costs a fraction as much.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-371" title="EEVblog #371 - Universal Programmer Teardown">371</a></sup> Cheap universal programmers are marketed heavily into automotive EEPROM reflashing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup> Large legacy systems supported the whole span of E-squared PROM and EPROM parts of their day.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup>

In-system programming removed the socket requirement. Early high-density CPLDs used EEPROM technology and were reprogrammable in place over a serial cable driven from a couple of PC parallel port lines — provided the board designer had the pins to spare for the programming header.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup> [[fpga|FPGAs]] conventionally need an external configuration EEPROM to load from at power-up; a non-volatile FPGA dispenses with it and powers up instantly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-636" title="EEVblog #636 - FPGA Demo Boards - DE0 Nano">636</a></sup>

Diagnostically, the bus traffic is informative. On an intercom that failed to start, the I²C lines idled high from the pull-ups and showed no activity for hundreds of milliseconds after power-up, proving the processor was not even attempting to read the EEPROM and therefore was not running — an uninitialised [[lcd]] being the classic symptom of the same condition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Well-designed firmware would initialise the display before anything else so that an EEPROM read failure could be reported rather than merely inferred.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> EEPROM failure with age is a recognised repair scenario in its own right, though supplies remain the more likely culprit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> In one grid monitoring product an EEPROM fault manifested as continuous [[relay]] hunting for the grid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-877" title="EEVblog #877 - Solar Analytics Home Energy Monitoring Installation">877</a></sup>

## Internal versus external

Whether the EEPROM is inside the microcontroller or beside it is a cost decision that reads directly off the board. An external part signals a cheap processor without on-chip non-volatile storage — an 8051 or similar rather than a PIC or Atmel part that includes it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> Conversely, on-chip EEPROM can be the reason a particular part is selected: a low-power microcontroller chosen for a product for its integrated LCD driver was also chosen for its built-in EEPROM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> Capacities are modest and quoted plainly in the selection tables — 256 bytes on a PIC16F1934, 6K on a Cortex-M4 with 1 MB of flash and 256K of SRAM, 6K embedded on an STM32 with 20K of SRAM.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Relying on that internal store constrains what a product can do; a meter with only limited internal EEPROM cannot data-log meaningfully, and gaining a microSD card later was a stroke of luck rather than a plan.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup>

On platforms where EEPROM is emulated rather than real, other options may be better: an ESP8266 sketch can persist Wi-Fi credentials and API keys to EEPROM, but the flash-backed SPIFFS filesystem is the better choice and requires no external hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yabl87SD-3M" title="Guest Video: Brian Lough - ESP8266 Libraries">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-998" title="EEVblog #998 - How To Program ESP8266 WiFi With Arduino">998</a></sup>

## Vintage boards and the mask ROM contrast

On equipment predating cheap writable memory, the absence of EEPROM is itself the observation, marked with a standing formula — a soldered-down mask ROM is "None of that EEPROM rubbish".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1169" title="EEVblog #1169 - TI 1972 Computer Interfacing">1169</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-791" title="EEVblog #791 - Ebay Fluke 45 Multimeter Teardown">791</a></sup> Machines of that generation integrated RAM and mask ROM only, with flash and EEPROM both yet to be invented, and any development variant would have been a costly windowed part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup> An 8085 design kit of the period had no non-volatile user storage whatsoever, so a program was lost when power was removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1308" title="EEVblog #1308 - 1970&#x27;s Intel MCS-85 8085 Design Kit!">1308</a></sup> Ceramic gold-capped mask ROM ASICs were used in preference to windowed erasable parts where a manufacturer had the fabrication capability in house.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup> By contrast, the appearance of a real EEPROM next to an 8031 and 6264 SRAM on an old board is worth noting as a mark of a design that intended to be configurable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> An older microprocessor-based supply, likely dating to the early 1980s, carried both its own EEPROM and its own EPROM, the latter an external 27C256 holding the program the microprocessor could not store itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup>
