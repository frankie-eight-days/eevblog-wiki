Apparent power, designated *S*, is simply the [[voltage]] multiplied by the [[current]] in an AC circuit, expressed in volt-amps (VA) rather than watts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> It diverges from real power the moment a load contains reactance, because the resulting phase difference between voltage and current means the product of the two no longer equals the heat actually dissipated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The distinction matters everywhere in AC power distribution, [[power-supply|power supply]] design, and mains-powered equipment, and it governs how transformers and supply infrastructure are sized and how industrial customers are billed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

## Definition and the power triangle

Apparent power is voltage times current, both AC RMS, and its units are VA precisely because that is literally what the quantity is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Where real power is computed as I²R, apparent power is I²Z or V²/Z — the total [[impedance]], including both the actual [[resistance]] and the imaginary reactive component, rather than the resistance alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

The relationship between the three powers is a right triangle. Real power *P* lies along the horizontal axis; the [[phase-angle|phase angle]] theta between voltage and current opens up from it; and the hypotenuse is the apparent power vector *S*, which is V times I and is a quantity that can actually be measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The vertical leg joining them is reactive power *Q*, in VAR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> By inspection, S equals the square root of P² plus Q².<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Reactive power uses the same V times I product as apparent power but carries a sine theta term, where real power carries cos theta.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> [[power-factor|Power factor]] follows directly: it is real power divided by apparent power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

As phase angle increases, the apparent power vector extends upward and apparent power increases, whether the reactance is inductive or capacitive — the sign of the angle makes no difference to the magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

## Apparent power is not dissipated

In AC circuits, loads, transmission lines, generators, and transformers, real power is only ever dissipated in resistances — never in capacitances, never in inductors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Apparent power therefore has no physical reality in the load itself and is not dissipated there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The reactive component is imaginary: it is not dissipated anywhere in the circuit or product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

An [[oscilloscope]] demonstrates the gap concretely. At 240 V RMS and roughly 280 mA RMS, the product of voltage and current is about 67, but that reading is 67 VA, not 67 W; the true power drawn is 30 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The back label rates the instrument at a maximum power of 50 W, so a naive voltage-times-current calculation appears to exceed the rating — the discrepancy is entirely the difference between apparent and real power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The cause is that the instrument uses a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching power supply]] with no [[power-factor-correction|power factor correction]] circuit, which introduces the voltage-current phase difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

## Upstream consequences

Although reactive power does not exist as dissipated energy in the load, it manifests upstream in the power delivery system as apparent power in volt-amps, and that has flow-on effects as real I²R copper losses in the rest of the system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The current has to be supplied from the power station regardless, and every conductor along the way — the mains wiring in a house, and even the power cord connecting to the product — has resistance and therefore real copper losses measured in watts, not in VA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> In an industrial setting the high circulating current forces larger copper conductors to be specified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o2NxHu5Bsnk" title="Discussion with Shahriar from The Signal Path">•</a></sup>

This is why transformers and similar components are rated in VA rather than watts: the rating must account for the phase difference and the apparent power the device has to pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> Non-sinusoidal current [[waveform|waveforms]] compound the problem. Peak current spikes drawn by switching supplies are real and must come from the mains and from the entire distribution system back to the generator, they push apparent power above real power, and they bear directly on the fusing design for a product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> Phase lead and lag are not the whole story either — harmonic power factor is the more serious contributor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

## Billing

Residential customers in most countries, including Australia, do not pay for apparent power; they are billed for true power in kilowatt-hours only, and the domestic energy meter is designed to measure that real component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1446" title="EEVblog 1446 - Analog Watt Hour Meters are AMAZING!">1446</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup> The meter installed even at a commercial office rate may be a real-power watt meter rather than an apparent power meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1191" title="EEVblog #1191 - Digital Energy Savers BUSTED!">1191</a></sup> Industrial and some commercial supplies are the exception: where the provider must build out extra capacity to carry the current, the charge is levied in VA, kVA, or MVA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o2NxHu5Bsnk" title="Discussion with Shahriar from The Signal Path">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup> The energy distribution and generation system still has to be sized to deliver the apparent power whether or not the customer is charged for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup>

Because reactive currents flow into the grid on one half cycle and back out on the next, and because domestic inductive and capacitive loads tend to cancel one another and pull the supply closer to a resistive load, the residential situation largely evens itself out and providers generally do not meter power factor at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o2NxHu5Bsnk" title="Discussion with Shahriar from The Signal Path">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1191" title="EEVblog #1191 - Digital Energy Savers BUSTED!">1191</a></sup>

This billing arrangement is what defeats plug-in energy saver devices, which are typically nothing more than a 3 µF power factor correction [[capacitor]] connected across the mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1191" title="EEVblog #1191 - Digital Energy Savers BUSTED!">1191</a></sup> Correcting power factor has legitimate purpose for an inductive load, but with real-power metering it saves nothing, and one such device measured under test made matters worse: power factor dropped to 0.22 and the apparent power drawn rose substantially.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1191" title="EEVblog #1191 - Digital Energy Savers BUSTED!">1191</a></sup>

## Measured examples

Typical figures show how far the two quantities separate in practice:

- An oscilloscope acquiring data drew 54.3 W at 247 V and about 256 mA, giving 63.3 VA and a power factor of about 0.86; switched off, it still drew 6.5 W, and with power factor now down to 0.32 the apparent power rose to 20.5 VA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-164" title="EEVblog #164 - Agilent Fly To The Moon">164</a></sup>
- An energy saver device measured about 9.1 W real against about 32 VA apparent, a power factor of roughly 0.57.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1191" title="EEVblog #1191 - Digital Energy Savers BUSTED!">1191</a></sup>
- A whole-house audit late at night, with everything switched off, showed 109.4 W of real power against 642 VA — some 2.7 A of current that has to come from somewhere, though only the 109.4 W is billed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup>
- A washing machine on standby drew 0.4 W of true power, with the reactive component at 16 VAR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup>
- A monitor's DC plug pack consumed 300 mW with no load, but 4 VA apparent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup>
- A clamp current probe measurement gave 175 mA at 241.5 V for 42.26 VA, against 23.8 W of real power being paid for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup>

## Instrumentation

Measuring apparent power requires only a voltmeter and an ammeter on the load and a multiplication of the two readings, but separating real from apparent power requires an instrument that multiplies voltage and current in phase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1446" title="EEVblog 1446 - Analog Watt Hour Meters are AMAZING!">1446</a></sup> Bench power analysers handle the full set, measuring voltage, current, phase angle, real power, and apparent power across multiple ranges, along with inrush current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> Some mains-connected products carry the necessary sensing hardware — a [[current-transformer|current transformer]] or Hall effect sensor plus voltage sensing — which makes real and apparent power measurement possible even when the user interface exposes only voltage and current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup>
