An oscilloscope probe is the interconnect between an [[oscilloscope]] input and the circuit under test, and it is a designed measurement component rather than a piece of wire: its resistors, capacitors, and deliberately lossy cable are matched to the oscilloscope's 1 MΩ, roughly 15–18 pF front end so that the probe-plus-scope system achieves a specified [[bandwidth]] and a flat [[frequency-response|frequency response]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> Because a probe always loads the circuit it touches, its [[impedance]] characteristics are as much a part of a measurement as the oscilloscope itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

## Construction

A standard ×10 passive probe places a 9 MΩ [[series-resistor|series resistor]] at the tip in parallel with a small compensation [[capacitor]]; together with the oscilloscope's 1 MΩ input [[resistance]] this forms a 10:1 [[resistor-divider|resistive voltage divider]], giving the probe a 10 MΩ DC [[input-impedance|input impedance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The "×10" marking is a misnomer — the probe attenuates by ten rather than multiplying.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

The cable is not ordinary [[coaxial-cable|coax]]. The centre conductor is a single fine strand of a resistive, nichrome-like material, often given deliberate kinks or wiggles, forming a lossy [[transmission-line|transmission line]] with a DC resistance on the order of 230–330 Ω where plain coax would measure near zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> This lossy construction is what allows a ×10 probe to achieve wide and flat bandwidth without the 50 Ω termination that a direct coax connection requires at both ends; an unterminated plain coax run instead exhibits transmission-line reflections that corrupt amplitude measurements as [[frequency]] rises.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> The trade-off is that passive probes of this type top out around 500 MHz, with 700 MHz regarded as pushing the technology and roughly 1 GHz the practical ceiling, while properly terminated coax can go considerably higher.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup>

## The ×1/×10 switch and bandwidth

Many general-purpose probes carry a switch selecting ×1 or ×10. In the ×1 position the switch simply shorts out the 9 MΩ [[resistor]] and compensation capacitor, connecting the tip straight through to the oscilloscope input, so the probe presents the scope's own 1 MΩ DC input impedance with the full cable [[capacitance]] across it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

The consequence is a drastic bandwidth reduction in ×1 mode — typically an order of magnitude or more below the ×10 figure. Representative numbers are 6.5 MHz for a typical switchable probe, 8 MHz ×1 versus 350 MHz ×10 for a Rigol probe, and 5 MHz ×1 versus 100 MHz ×10 on a tablet oscilloscope's supplied probe; a few designs reach 20 MHz in ×1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup> In ×10 mode the divider network reduces the effective capacitance seen by the circuit, which is why ×10 probes deliver the high bandwidth and why many quality probes are supplied as fixed ×10 only.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> A lone ×1 probe simulated in LTspice with 329 Ω series resistance and about 100 pF of cable capacitance over a 1.2 m cable yields about 7.8 MHz bandwidth by itself, dropping lower once the oscilloscope's [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]] is included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

The ×1 position retains two legitimate uses: avoiding the ten-fold attenuation when measuring very low-level signals, and exploiting the low bandwidth as incidental filtering when measuring [[noise]] in a restricted band, since a power-supply noise specification given over 0–20 MHz cannot be honestly measured with a 350 MHz ×10 path picking up everything above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup>

A persistent trap is that no oscilloscope can detect the position of a switchable probe's ×1/×10 switch; professional fixed-attenuation probes omit the switch partly for this reason. The switch is easily bumped while handling probes on the bench, and unless the oscilloscope's software probe attenuation setting matches the physical switch, every vertical reading is out by a factor of ten — an error that catches experienced engineers as well as beginners.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup>

## Input capacitance and probe loading

The figure that matters at high frequency is not the 10 MΩ DC resistance but the probe's input capacitance, sometimes specified as total system capacitance — the capacitance the circuit sees with the probe plugged into the oscilloscope. Typical values run from about 17 pF for a 150 MHz general-purpose probe and 14–18 pF for 200 MHz class probes down to 11–12 pF for 500 MHz probes and 3.9 pF for a 1 GHz passive probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> At frequency, that capacitance presents a [[capacitive-reactance|capacitive reactance]] that loads the circuit far more than the DC resistance suggests.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

Loading effects are readily observed in sensitive circuits: a 10 MΩ ×10 probe is enough to hold an [[led|LED]] trigger circuit permanently on, to pull a floating CMOS clock pin low and stop a counter, to disturb a piezoelectric [[oscillator]]'s tone, and to load a peak-detector capacitor measurably at low frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> Probing the oscillator pin of a crystal circuit with a ×1 probe is a standard demonstration of how severely the added capacitance can upset an oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup> Every probing connection loads the circuit in a complex way, which is why probe loading can and should be included in circuit simulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

## Compensation

A ×10 probe must be compensated to match the individual [[oscilloscope-channel|oscilloscope channel]]'s input capacitance, using the oscilloscope's probe-adjust square-wave output (typically 1 kHz): an under- or over-compensated probe shows rounded or overshooting corners and misrepresents amplitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> The compensation adjustment is a trimmer capacitor, typically with a range on the order of 0–50 pF; lower-bandwidth probes carry the adjustment at the probe head, while high-bandwidth designs place the network at the cable's base near the BNC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup> Some oscilloscopes provide a guided on-screen probe-adjust procedure that displays the live [[waveform]] and indicates whether alignment is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup>

## Grounding technique

The long alligator-clip [[ground]] lead supplied with probes is an [[inductor]], and any length of wire is an inductor; used at speed it produces [[overshoot]], undershoot, and [[ringing]] that belong to the measurement setup rather than the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> Proper high-frequency technique replaces the ground lead and tip with the short low-[[inductance]] spring ground contact supplied in the probe accessory kit, keeping both the signal and ground paths as short as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The ground lead also acts as an [[antenna]], and the probe assembly as a whole will pick up ESD and high-frequency disturbances capacitively even when disconnected from any circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1107" title="EEVblog #1107 - Shocking 4K BENQ Monitor Problem!">1107</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-20" title="EEVblog #20 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon Part 2">20</a></sup>

## Safety

The probe's ground clip is hard-wired through the cable shield and the oscilloscope chassis to [[mains-earth|mains earth]], and on a multi-channel oscilloscope all channel grounds are shorted together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Clipping the ground lead onto any point that is not at earth potential creates a [[short-circuit|short circuit]] capable of carrying substantial [[current]]; the slim ground lead is the weak link and can vaporise in the hand if the shorted node has enough energy behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Equipment assumed to be isolated frequently is not — a [[usb|USB]]-powered board, for example, becomes mains-earth-referenced through the host computer — so ground status must be verified before probing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> A common consequence of learning this the hard way is one destroyed probe per career.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> Active FET probes carry the same restriction: they are not floating, and even expensive units carry warnings of a few volts maximum common-mode rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup> Measurements on floating or mains-referenced circuits require a differential probe, an isolated oscilloscope with fully shrouded connectors and insulated probe accessories, or an optical-fibre probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

## Probe types

Passive probes contain no active electronics — at most compensation trimmers and resistors — and span three broad designs in ascending performance: the switchable ×1/×10 probe, the fixed ×10 probe, and plain coax-based probing, which is technically the highest-bandwidth and lowest-cost option but demands correct termination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> Beyond 500 MHz, active FET probes become the appropriate tool because their input capacitance is far lower than any passive design; commercial examples reach multi-gigahertz bandwidths, and a simple active probe can be built around a single BF998 FET in common-drain configuration feeding a 50 Ω-terminated input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup> Specialised active probes for power-rail measurement add large [[dc-offset|DC offset]] ranges, on the order of ±20 V, so that millivolt-level [[ripple]] on a high DC rail can be examined directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup>

High-[[voltage]] passive probes extend the divider to 100:1 or 1000:1, enabling measurements of hundreds of volts — for example inductor back-EMF spikes approaching 700 V — that would otherwise exceed both the oscilloscope's input rating and the ×10 probe's.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1753" title="EEVblog 1753 - Designing a 2000V Isolated Oscilloscope (Cleverscope)">1753</a></sup> Voltage rating also differs between switch positions: on one tablet oscilloscope the input is rated 40 V in ×1 mode and 400 V only with the ×10 divider engaged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup>

A capable low-cost alternative for high-bandwidth work is the DIY resistive transmission-line probe: a series resistor of about 1 kΩ at the tip over properly terminated coax yields an input capacitance of only 1–2 pF and, with careful construction, signal fidelity comparable to commercial gigahertz-class probes for a few dollars in parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>

Probe linearity itself is a limit in precision work: a good probe may be linear only to about 1%, one part in a hundred, which is inadequate when a measurement demands one part in several thousand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup>

## Measurement artifacts

Two artifacts intrinsic to probes can masquerade as circuit faults. First, passive probes are microphonic: the multilayer ceramic capacitors inside the probe head exhibit the piezoelectric effect, and tapping the probe on the bench produces a damped oscillatory shock response that can exceed 2 V peak-to-peak. The response persists with the tip shorted (at reduced amplitude and altered frequency), depends on the probe's physical orientation, is essentially independent of the ×1/×10 switch position, and is common to all oscilloscopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-162" title="EEVblog #162 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect on an Oscilloscope">162</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - A Shocking Oscilloscope Problem!">983</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - A Shocking Oscilloscope Problem!">983</a></sup> The same effect lets a ×10 probe with its input shorted serve as a crude vibration or acoustic sensor, able to pick up the vibrational modes of a [[pcb|PCB]] at a specific frequency, such as 11 kHz from an excited board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1743" title="EEVblog 1743 - Mechanical Vibration Detection with your Oscilloscope Probe">1743</a></sup>

Second, the probe cable's inductance resonates with the oscilloscope's input capacitance as an LC tank, practically always near 100–120 MHz. Broadband static energy — for example from an operator standing up from a chair — is picked up directly by the cable and rings this tank, producing a clean sinusoidal burst on screen even with the probe tip shorted, with the ground lead disconnected, or with a plain coax cable (terminated or not) substituted for the probe. The pickup is a property of the cable itself, not of the ground-lead loop, and occurs on essentially any scope and probe combination; it can be injected during a real measurement and mistaken for a circuit glitch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14" title="EEVblog #14 - An unusual oscilloscope phenomenon!">14</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-20" title="EEVblog #20 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon Part 2">20</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21" title="EEVblog #21 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon - Part 3">21</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope-side handling

Many oscilloscopes implement probe detection through a contact pin or grounded ring around the BNC, automatically switching the channel's scaling when a matching ×10 probe is attached; third-party probes without the pin require the attenuation to be set manually in the channel menu.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> Because probe attenuation is purely a software scaling factor and does not change the hardware input ranges, the menu setting must match the physical probe or the vertical readout is wrong by the probe ratio.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> For multi-channel work, oscilloscopes also provide per-channel deskew to time-align signals arriving through different probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Hands-free probe holders — weighted or magnetic stands that hold a sharp sprung tip on a fine-pitch SMD pin under their own pressure — address the practical problem of holding several probes on adjacent pins while keeping a hand free, though they inherit the same [[grounding]] and signal-integrity limitations as hand-held probing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1324" title="EEVblog #1324 - Oscilloscope Reference Waveforms are USEFUL">1324</a></sup>

In Australian usage the probe is colloquially a "crow probe", from [[cathode]]-ray oscilloscope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21" title="EEVblog #21 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon - Part 3">21</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>
