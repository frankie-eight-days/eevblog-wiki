Threshold voltage is the input level at which a device stops behaving one way and starts behaving another: the gate [[voltage]] at which a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] channel forms, the base-emitter voltage at which a bipolar junction begins to conduct, the trip point of a [[comparator]] or logic input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> It is the parameter that turns a continuously varying analogue quantity into a decision, and almost every switching, detecting, timing and triggering circuit is organised around one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Because it is a property of the device physics rather than a number the designer picks freely, much of practical circuit design consists of arranging the surrounding resistors, dividers and bias points so that the signal of interest lands where the threshold happens to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup>

## Semiconductor origins

In a field effect [[transistor]] the mechanism is electrostatic. With no gate voltage, the depletion layers at source and drain block conduction entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup> A positive voltage on the gate — only a couple of volts, depending on the device type — establishes an [[electric-field|electric field]] strong enough to overcome the depletion barrier, and the electrons in the heavily doped n-type source and drain regions form a channel between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup> Simulation of a low gate threshold nanowire MOSFET shows the electron channel appearing essentially all at once as the gate passes roughly 0.45 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup>

The bipolar equivalent is the familiar [[diode]] curve of the base-emitter junction: essentially no [[current]] flows until about 0.6 V, above which conduction rises steeply as the depletion region narrows and collapses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup>

Threshold voltage is not permanently fixed. Silicon dioxide traps charge, which makes the gate oxide the weakest element of a MOS device in a radiation environment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> Gamma radiation and charged protons progressively accumulate in the oxide, and each trapped charge drives the threshold voltage further down; once it passes below zero the device no longer functions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup> The damage is cumulative rather than a single-event failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

## Logic-level thresholds and gate drive

A MOSFET with a low voltage threshold can be driven directly from logic outputs, at 5 V or at 3.3 V, without a dedicated gate driver — a capability that was transformative when logic-level parts first appeared in the 1980s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Such parts often trade this for a relatively high on-[[resistance]]; one jellybean device is specified at 2.5 ohms, which is adequate up to a couple of hundred milliamps alongside its low 22 pF [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

On the receiving side, threshold voltage sets the [[noise]] and voltage margin a logic family will tolerate. A TTL circuit's lowest typical threshold voltage of around 4.75 V is why [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]] matters when a [[multimeter]] is inserted in series with a supply rail: a meter dropping 0.3 V, or on some ranges up to a volt, can pull a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] below that threshold and stop the circuit working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup>

## Hysteresis: two thresholds instead of one

A single threshold is fragile. With the trip point at 0 V, a signal a microvolt or a nanovolt above it switches the output, so a slow or noisy input causes repeated switching around the crossing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Positive feedback around a comparator solves this by replacing the single level with an upper and a lower threshold placed either side of the original point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> The feedback divider action means the reference is, for example, +1 V while the output sits high; the first time the input goes even a smidgen over that, the output slams to −10 V, which reconfigures the same divider to −1 V and moves the trip point out of the way of the noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

Inside a CMOS Schmitt trigger the same behaviour is built from the transistors themselves. Raising the input by about a volt switches on a transistor that was previously off, and it forms a [[voltage-divider|voltage divider]] with another device between VCC and [[ground]], biasing the source of the following transistor to a particular threshold level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Pushing the input further transitions through that level and flips the trip point, with the mirror-image arrangement of P-channel devices doing the same job on the way back down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

Hysteresis does not come free. Plotting supply current against input voltage for a Schmitt trigger gate shows current peaking sharply at each of the two threshold voltages, so a slowly ramped input still produces current spikes even though it no longer oscillates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

## Threshold voltages in comparator and timing circuits

The [[555-timer|555 timer]] generates its thresholds from an internal [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]], with the control voltage pin connected directly to one of the taps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The threshold comparator trips at two thirds of the supply, so on a 9 V supply the timing [[capacitor]] must charge to 6 V before the output changes state, which is exactly where the switching is observed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Reaching that two-thirds point turns on the open-collector discharge transistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The threshold is thus supply-referenced, but the usable supply range is not unlimited: the bipolar version of the part only operates down to about 4.5 V, while the CMOS version works lower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup>

Comparator-based detectors follow the same pattern. The LM3914 bar graph driver is essentially a stack of comparators fed by upper and lower threshold voltage pins that sit across the internal divider chain, so those two pins set every individual [[led|LED]]'s trip point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> In an overload detector running from a split rail, changing the supply arrangement moves the threshold to half the split rail, giving expected trip points of plus and minus 0.5 V, with observed operation still holding at around 0.25 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup>

Discrete circuits rely on the transistor's own threshold as the timing element. In a soft latching power switch, an RC network holds a point high for a time constant of perhaps half a second; once the voltage decays to the transistor's threshold voltage the transistor switches on and pulls the node to ground, changing the button's function from on to off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup> A related arrangement uses a [[zener-diode|Zener diode]] to raise the effective threshold: the input has to overcome the Zener drop before the transistor switches on, landing a smidge under 10 V for a nominally 10 V Zener because the parts are imprecise and the result depends on [[resistor]] current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup>

Where the load itself has a threshold, that threshold becomes a compliance requirement. A [[constant-current-source|constant current source]] set to 1 milliamp delivers nothing into a string of LEDs until the [[compliance-voltage|compliance voltage]] is raised past the LEDs' combined threshold; nothing flows even at 33 V, and current only begins around 37 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

## Threshold as a design centring problem

The recurring practical task is placing a signal in the middle of the threshold window rather than near its edges. In a [[mains-voltage|mains voltage]] detection stick, the [[antenna]] signal is AC coupled and pulled high into a Schmitt [[inverter]], and the output transistor and LED turn on only when the input from the antenna exceeds a certain threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup> The slowly varying 50–60 Hz input squares up as it transitions through the Schmitt threshold levels, so a [[sine-wave|sine wave]] in produces a [[square-wave|square wave]] out — provided it actually reaches the thresholds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup> The RC network ahead of the inverter exists precisely for the off-nominal case: at an input [[frequency]] lower than the intended detection frequency, the capacitor's discharge curve carries the node through both thresholds, changing the output [[waveform]] from a simple inversion of the input and altering the smoothed result downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup> The two resistors are chosen to sit the operating point in the middle of the threshold voltage, which suppresses duty-cycle artefacts, since noise arriving with the wrong [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]] then fails to reach the thresholds at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup>

The same centring appears in [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]] work. In a metal detector, the comparator inputs sit at the same DC level of about 2.5 V, right around the threshold voltage as expected, trimmed there by the sensitivity potentiometers; changing the threshold moves the smoothed detector output only a tiny amount, so there is little adjustment range available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-714" title="EEVblog #714 - Metal Detector Reverse Engineering">714</a></sup> In a speech front end built from a quarter of an LM324 used as a crude comparator, a 10 megohm [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]] adds hysteresis and the threshold value must be set lower than the offset value for the clipping to work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup> Threshold levels can also be inferred rather than known: a repair diagnosis rested on a 250 mV change in the sensed probe supply being enough to detect the probe's presence, without the exact detection thresholds being documented.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup>

## Instrument thresholds

Test equipment exposes threshold voltage as a user setting wherever it digitises an analogue input. Serial bus decoders provide comprehensive per-channel threshold voltage settings, and the decoded data can itself be used as a trigger source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Mixed-signal capture requires checking that the digital channel thresholds are correct before triggering off a line such as chip select.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> A [[frequency-counter|frequency counter]]'s threshold interacts with its other input settings; counting reliably required a 1.5 volt threshold voltage together with medium sensitivity, positive rising edge and a 100 kHz filter so that only genuine cycles were registered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-961" title="EEVblog #961 - Monkey Debouncing">961</a></sup> Electronic loads expose high and low threshold voltage settings alongside range and [[slew-rate|slew rate]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup>

Defaults matter here. A pattern generator that defaults its threshold to 0 volts will silently fail to decode a signal that never crosses zero, and a more useful default would be referenced to the current vertical scale — something like one division — rather than a fixed zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qE-SwtYQM6E" title="Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope Pattern Generator">•</a></sup>
