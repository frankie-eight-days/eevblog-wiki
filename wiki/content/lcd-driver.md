An LCD driver is the circuitry that generates the alternating segment and common [[waveform|waveforms]] an [[lcd|LCD]] glass needs in order to display anything. Bare LCD glass is a passive component: it does not drive itself, and connecting it directly to a [[microcontroller]] port and expecting an image is not workable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Whichever of the common glass interconnect methods is used — pins, flat flex, or zebra strips — the glass still requires separate driver circuitry beneath it, supplied either by a microcontroller with a built-in LCD controller, a dedicated driver chip, or a home-grown drive circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup>

## Why dedicated drive circuitry is required

The essential requirement is AC drive. Over time the net DC level across the liquid crystal must be zero, and a driver that fails to achieve this can potentially damage the display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> Meeting that condition needs a set of distinct bias [[voltage]] levels rather than simple logic-level outputs, and generating those levels is precisely what a dedicated driver takes care of.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> The same reasoning applies at the small end of the scale: individual-segment displays of the kind used in wristwatches and calculators — as distinct from dot matrix displays — need special LCD driving circuitry to drive all those segments, which is why segment-LCD projects begin with the question of where the drive is coming from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> Later clones of the classic Hitachi HD44780 character controller integrate an LCD driver for exactly this reason: to supply the bias and AC signals that drive the segments directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup>

## Rows, commons and segments

The complexity of the driver is set by the geometry of the glass. The number of rows and segments dictates not only how complex the LCD is, but how complex its driver must be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup> A custom segment LCD laid out as eight commons by thirty-two segments needs a driver chip with at least that many commons and segments, and forty glass pins to reach it, every one of which must be driven when the glass has no on-board driver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup> A six-digit display is already a large-ish requirement in segment count and pushes the designer toward a physically bigger part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Concrete examples recur: a µSupply front panel used eight commons and thirty-one segments driven by a Holtek HT1622;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> a microcontroller selection exercise put the requirement at seventy-three segments;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup> and an ST ARM part chosen for a product offered eight by forty segments against a need of roughly thirty-six.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup>

Large dot matrix panels multiply the problem rather than change it. A 240 by 64 display required a striking number of driver chips on its own display board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup> a handheld scopemeter carried six Hitachi LCD dot matrix driver chipsets integrated onto the main board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> and a 40-column by 8-line panel used two rows of drivers with each row handling four lines plus five column drivers, eight characters apiece.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> In a graphing calculator the row of Toshiba parts surrounding the main CPU were all LCD drivers, identifiable by the huge tracks fanning out from them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-715" title="EEVblog #715 - Mailbag">715</a></sup> A driver chip may also hold the display data sent to it by the [[processor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup>

## Where the driver lives

Four integration choices recur across designs.

**Built into the microcontroller.** For a relatively small LCD the existing microcontroller can drive it, or a variant of that microcontroller with a built-in LCD driver can be selected — and many families offer one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup> This is the cheapest arrangement because an external LCD controller is an extra BOM item and extra cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup> The presence of an adequate LCD driver was a decisive selection criterion in more than one design: an STM32L1 was specified into the [[121gw|121GW]] [[multimeter]] partly for its LCD driver signals,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> and an STM32L part was chosen for a product because it had an LCD driver with the required rows and columns alongside built-in [[eeprom|EEPROM]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup> The constraint is that suitable parts tend to be large: an STM32L0x3 offering 4 by 52 or 8 by 48 drive also brings 20K of [[sram|SRAM]], 6K of embedded EEPROM and a 32-pin minimum package, none of which is cheap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

**A separate driver chip.** When no suitable integrated part can be found or the integrated option costs too much, a standalone driver is cheaper, which is how a Holtek part ended up in a design after ST candidates were ruled out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> Holtek make both microcontrollers and LCD drivers, and they are inexpensive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> The same trade-off appeared during the 121GW development, where a suggested ARM micro had no built-in LCD driver and would have required a separate one at the cost of extra [[pcb|PCB]] space and extra [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> A separate driver is also the fallback when the main processor simply lacks pins — an 8051-class multimeter micro with no LCD block and not enough pins implies another driver elsewhere on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup>

**Chip on glass.** A COG display carries the driver chip bonded onto the glass itself, so the host talks to it over I²C, [[spi|SPI]] or a Hitachi-style interface instead of driving forty pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup> This is how displays with very high segment counts become practical at all; a triple-digit meter display with many hundreds of circuits would be extremely difficult to drive from a generic micro without a fully custom chip-on-glass part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup> COG is not free: for one custom LCD the quoted difference was $3.71 each against $1.47, which did not justify it once the external driver chip was priced in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup> COG parts appear in a GoPro with a simple [[serial-port|serial interface]] off the glass,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-399" title="EEVblog #399 - GoPro Hero 2 Teardown">399</a></sup> and in handheld consoles where the driver die is visible embedded in the glass under the polariser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-afErPINq8qc" title="Mini Arcade Game Console Repair OOPSIE!">•</a></sup>

**Chip on flex or chip on board.** The driver die may instead be bonded to a flex tape or directly to a small board, hot-bar attached at both ends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1110" title="EEVblog #1110 - Mailbag">1110</a></sup> A calculator LCD module with more segments than its main die could drive used a hot tape bond to a chip-on-tape driver,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1094" title="EEVblog #1094 - Casio FX260 Solar II Calculator Tritium Nuclear Battery Experiments">1094</a></sup> and a Casio assembly put the LCD driver and its full pinout onto flex tape rather than a PCB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1229" title="EEVblog #1229 - Mailbag">1229</a></sup> Chip-on-board driver dies also handled character LCDs and sometimes keypad scanning in early handsets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-492" title="EEVBlog #492 - Vintage Motorola MicroTAC Mobile Phone Teardown">492</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-856" title="EEVblog #856 - Mailbag">856</a></sup>

## In multimeters and instruments

Multimeter architecture splits cleanly along this line. A complete multimeter chipset contains the processor, ADC, measurement switching and the LCD driver in one package, with traces running off to zebra strips and the glass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> All-in-one chipsets appear in clamp meters, where the absence of a separate driver plus an insufficient segment count implies the glass itself carries a COG driver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3kdYGneg9xI" title="Brymen BM037 Clamp Meter Teardown">•</a></sup> Elsewhere the two-chip arrangement dominates: one LCD driver and one multimeter chipset,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1457" title="EEVblog1457 - Old School Mailbag - ESC Burnout">1457</a></sup> with the driver chip often the only other active part on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-884" title="EEVblog #884 - EEVBlog BM235 Multimeter REPAIR">884</a></sup>

Which arrangement a manufacturer picks is not always explicable from outside. The [[bm235|BM235]] uses an external LCD chipset despite its Hycon measurement chip appearing to have enough built-in segments to do the job,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> and a socket tester likewise ignores an available internal LCD driver in favour of an external chip, apparently because the custom segment layout demanded it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> [[reverse-engineering|Reverse engineering]] exploits the split: a separate driver forces a serial interface between the chips, and finding an I²C driver part identifies the SCL and SDA pins on the measurement chipset and confirms the pin mapping, since the segment pins of the internal driver go unused.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> The same logic tempts investigators to look for a serial output on the driver itself when an instrument emits an unusual protocol.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup>

Named driver parts recur across teardowns: the Hitachi HD44780 character controller paired with an HD44100 display driver in the original two-chip arrangement, a combination almost nobody uses now;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> the Hitachi HD44102 in vintage portables;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> Holtek's HT1622 and HT6121B;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1105" title="EEVblog #1105 - $100 Custom LCD Design - Part 3 (µSupply Part 18)">1105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup> the Philips PCF8577 I²C driver and the Philips 8566;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> the Hycon HY2613C in Brymen meters;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup> and a TM1622 in a low-cost meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> Hitachi's dominance of the field is longstanding enough that a Hitachi part beside an LCD is a reasonable first guess.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup> Custom ASICs subsume the function entirely in high-volume consumer gear, handling all the LCD driving alongside other mixed-signal work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1275" title="EEVblog #1275 - Uniden UHF Walkie Talkie Teardowns">1275</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-715" title="EEVblog #715 - Mailbag">715</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1229" title="EEVblog #1229 - Mailbag">1229</a></sup>

At the higher end the driver disappears into the application processor. ARM-class application processors from Freescale and similar vendors integrate the LCD driver along with everything else, which is why some instruments show no separate driver at all;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1286" title="EEVblog #1286 - Mailbag">1286</a></sup> a TMS320 with an integrated LCD driver handles a 640 by 480 panel in a handheld scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Conversely, an instrument with no processor at all can still drive a display, using 74-series and 4000-series CMOS logic to convert the analogue signal and drive the LCD directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1258" title="EEVblog #1258 - MORE Dumpster Wandel And Goltermann!">1258</a></sup>

## Debugging and failure

Driver chips are a plausible but usually incorrect suspect when a display misbehaves. In a vintage portable showing duplicated lines, the column and line drivers were demonstrably fine because the correct characters were appearing — the fault was in the data being clocked into them, garbage in and garbage out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> The candidate causes for that class of fault are a dry joint, a failed driver chip, or something else loading down the shared bus,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> and distinguishing them means probing, or swapping a known-good LCD module to prove the fault is on the module rather than the driving side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> Similarly, a GPS with a dead display could in principle have lost an LCD driver or its bias voltage, but the actual cause was an [[electrolytic-capacitor|electrolytic capacitor]] — a more probable failure from the outset, and a fortunate one, since replacement drivers are generally unobtainable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup> A display that lights up at all after the boot-up and turn-on delay is evidence that the processor and the driver are both alive,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1425" title="EEVblog 1425 - Fluke Repair 2 Electric Boogaloo">1425</a></sup> and an insertion error appearing on screen after reprogramming confirms both processor and LCD driver are working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup>

Where the LCD driver is clearly not the culprit, that fact narrows the search: an intermittent fault in a meter whose display is fine points at the custom measurement [[asic|ASIC]] instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup> An LCD showing all segments at once suggests the processor never booted far enough to send the clear command, leaving the driver in its power-on state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup> When a custom LCD showed a marginal common in prototype testing, suspicion fell on the glass rather than the driver, on the grounds that an industry-standard driver chip that has been available for a long time and is optimised for the application is unlikely to be the weak link; the diagnosis was confirmed by breaking into the HT1622 supply track and sweeping its voltage in 0.1 volt steps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1105" title="EEVblog #1105 - $100 Custom LCD Design - Part 3 (µSupply Part 18)">1105</a></sup>

Driver chips also cause trouble on the bus rather than the panel. An I²C LCD driver was traced as the source of intermediate voltage levels on a bus that should have carried only ones and zeros — isolated to the LCD rather than the other I²C devices or the driver code, which had been written from scratch against the LCD manufacturer's [[datasheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> Parallel-mode graphical LCDs are notoriously awkward to bring up: with sixteen lines to check and only a two-channel scope, separating a wiring fault from a code fault becomes tedious.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup> Even in a modest project, driver code plus string handling accounts for a substantial share of program memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup>

## Selection

Choosing an LCD-capable microcontroller is a [[parametric-search|parametric search]] problem with awkward edges. Distributor search tools have no dedicated LCD column, so candidates must be picked out by looking for LCD in the peripheral descriptions one family at a time, yielding on the order of 2,306 microcontrollers across manufacturers — and the result depends on the distributor having imported the manufacturer data correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> Beyond the segment count the comparison quickly becomes intractable, because driving an LCD interacts with timers, ADCs, comparators and other peripherals in ways that a datasheet [[current]] figure does not capture; a genuine shootout requires spinning boards and writing test software that exercises each peripheral.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> The pragmatic engineering position is that an on-chip driver is preferable when one exists in a part that otherwise fits, and a separate driver chip when it does not, decided on total cost rather than elegance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1055" title="EEVblog #1055 - How to Design a Custom LCD- µSupply Part 16">1055</a></sup>
