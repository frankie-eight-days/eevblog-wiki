A voltage divider is a series string of resistors across a [[voltage-source|voltage source]], tapped at an intermediate node to produce a known, precise fraction of that source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> It is the single most reused piece of theory in practical electronics: dividers appear in the feedback path of essentially every regulator, in the front end of every [[multimeter]] and [[oscilloscope-probe|oscilloscope probe]], in [[comparator]] threshold networks, in biasing and level-shifting circuits, and embedded inside chips wherever a scaled copy of a signal is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Its formal name is the Kelvin divider, after Lord Kelvin, though almost everyone calls it a voltage divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

The concept is broader than the resistive case — capacitive and non-linear dividers exist too — but the [[resistor-divider|resistive divider]] is the canonical form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Division is also unavoidable rather than optional: any real source with series [[resistance]] driving any load is already a divider, and the output is always less than the source [[voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

## The formula

The output across the lower [[resistor]] is R2 divided by (R1 + R2), multiplied by the input voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The labelling convention is not universal, so the identity of R1 and R2 must be checked before the formula is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> A 10 V source across two 10 k resistors gives 5 V, which the formula confirms as 0.5 × 10 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The relation generalises to an arbitrarily long string: the voltage across any single resistor, not just the bottom one, is that resistor over the total, times the input — useful where a circuit taps a mid-string node or takes a [[differential-pair|differential pair]] of taps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

The divider equation has a dual, the [[current]] divider equation, which apportions a total current between parallel branches; both belong alongside Ohm's law in the basic toolkit, and both fall out of nodal and mesh analysis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup>

## Output impedance and loading

A divider is a poor voltage source. Reduced to its Thevenin equivalent, two 10 k resistors on a 10 V rail become a 5 V source behind 5 k of series resistance — behaviour identical to a 5 V [[battery]] with a 5 k resistor in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> That series resistance means the tap can only feed high-[[impedance]] loads. Bipolar op-amp inputs, which draw almost no input current, are untroubled by 5 k of source resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

The design tension is that dividers in regulator feedback paths are deliberately made high in value — hundreds of kilohms — so they do not waste power, and high resistance in turn restricts what they can drive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The working rule of thumb is that the load must exceed the divider impedance by an order of magnitude or more: against the 5 k example, a 50 k load produces roughly 10 % error, while a 500 k load produces about 1 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

Loading error is the mechanism behind the most common bench surprise in precision measurement. A multimeter's nominal 10 M [[input-impedance|input impedance]] is itself the lower leg of a divider once it is connected. Placing a 10 M meter across a divider built from two 1 M resistors visibly shifts the reading, because the divider ratio has changed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The error becomes serious with source impedances in the tens of kilohms and upward — hundreds of kilohms, or megohms in a divider designed for very low [[power-consumption|power consumption]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> A meter with an 11.1 M input reads about 4.78 V where the unloaded divider gives 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Precision work therefore calls for a meter with a high-impedance (effectively infinite input) mode; on many instruments that mode is available only on the lower ranges, sometimes only on millivolts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> Even a nominally infinite input is finite, and at extreme source impedances that matters too.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup>

Analog moving-coil meters are far worse, and predictably so. At 20 kΩ/V on the 10 V range the meter presents 200 k; across a 100 k/100 k divider that 200 k parallels the lower leg to 66.666 k, and the divider formula predicts 0.4 V against an expected 4 V — an enormous error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> The same arithmetic runs the other way: high-sensitivity analog meters at 50 or 100 kΩ/V can outperform a 10 M digital meter on their highest voltage ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

Unintended dividers appear wherever series resistance is overlooked. A lossy [[coaxial-cable|coax]] cable with roughly 330 Ω of centre-conductor resistance, terminated into a 50 Ω scope input, divides the signal by about 7.5 and explains an inexplicably small measured amplitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> A [[function-generator|function generator]]'s 50 Ω output resistance must be counted as part of any external divider hung on it, or the resulting voltages will be wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> A degraded [[input-jack|input jack]] that develops a high-resistance path forms a divider with a meter's 10 M input, producing readings that are low rather than absent — a failure mode more confusing than an open circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> An [[inductor]]'s DC winding resistance likewise forms a divider with the rest of the circuit once the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] has saturated, so the settled current is set by that resistance rather than by an ideal short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup>

## Regulator and converter feedback

The traditional three-terminal [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] is a series pass [[transistor]], an [[op-amp|op amp]], a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]], and two feedback divider resistors — nothing more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Fixed devices such as the 7805 have the divider integrated to set the [[output-voltage|output voltage]]; adjustable parts such as the [[lm317|LM317]] bring the divider outside the package and expose a feedback pin, but the mechanism is identical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The classic LM317 application places a divider on the output and returns the tap to the adjust pin, with the output voltage set by R2 in the lower leg; the same topology and the same formula recur on the 7805 and LT1117 families, along with a reverse-protection [[diode]] and an optional [[capacitor]] across the adjust pin to improve the [[ripple]] rejection ratio.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

The divider values in that standard circuit are not arbitrary. [[datasheet|Data sheet]] values are chosen low enough that the divider itself satisfies the regulator's minimum load requirement; a design that drives the adjust pin directly instead of through a divider loses that load and must add one deliberately for stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Discrete regulators follow the same pattern — a typical output divider feeding back into the regulator with an external pass transistor is standard practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Switching converters use the same arrangement, which is why a wrong part in the divider network, such as a 10 k resistor fitted where a 100 pF bypass cap for the upper divider resistor belonged, breaks a DC-DC converter outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup> A tap taken off the output filter capacitors of an unmarked SMD regulator is the identifying mark of an adjustable part rather than a fixed one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> Boost converters with a fixed-output option let the designer delete the two external voltage-set resistors entirely and reduce the component count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The general error-amplifier architecture is the same regardless: the amplifier compares the divider tap on the output against a reference — which may itself come from a DAC or a pot — and drives the pass element to hold them equal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup>

Feedback dividers are equally fundamental in amplifiers. A non-inverting op-amp stage is precisely a divider in the feedback path, with the [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]] always larger than the lower resistor so that a smaller portion of the output is returned to the inverting input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> Discrete amplifier stages set their DC operating points the same way, with a 330 k into 6k2 network for AC and 330 k into 6k2 plus 100 k for DC, biased against a 15 V reference derived by a 47 k from the 120 V rail into 6k2.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>

## Instrument front ends

Every multimeter voltage range is a divider. A 7.5-digit meter's high-voltage input divider is essentially two resistors, 9.9 M and 100 k, giving a large division ratio in one step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Power meters rated to 600 V use a nominal 100:1 divider on the input, often with several taps for different ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> Socket testers built on multimeter chipsets follow the same architecture — input divider, multiplexers, ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> In practice the divider and the ADC front end together carry a great deal of range- and protection-related complexity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup>

Because the divider defines the ratio, it is also the prime suspect when ranges misbehave. A meter reading out by a similar amount on both AC and DC volts across every voltage range, while the millivolt range — which bypasses the divider — reads within spec, points squarely at a drifted input divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup> Where [[calibration]] allows individual ranges to be trimmed independently, a single out-of-spec range can be corrected without disturbing the others.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> A single well-behaved divider is also why calibrating a meter on one range usually leaves the other ranges acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup>

[[oscilloscope|Oscilloscope]] front ends face the harder version of the problem. A ×10 probe is a voltage divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Designing an attenuator front end whose divider gives good pulse response at both low and high frequencies is genuinely difficult, and the response can differ with the volts-per-division setting depending on where in the divider ladder the tap sits — a tap at the top of the ladder can behave better than one further down, since each tap brings its own resistive-divider compensation problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6XpyOGw6RFM" title="Rohde &amp; Schwarz RTB2000 Scope Pulse Response">•</a></sup>

High-voltage dividers are built as long series strings rather than as single parts. An insulation resistance meter runs its high-voltage divider as a chain of resistors separated by an [[isolation-slot|isolation slot]], stepping the voltage down along the board until the final tap is low enough to be carried back to the main board on an ordinary low-voltage [[connector]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup> An AC voltage standard uses a staggered 100:1 network so that each element sees a voltage within its own rating, which is better engineering practice than trusting a single resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-709" title="EEVblog #709 - EDC 4601 AC Voltage Standard Teardown">709</a></sup> A defibrillator front end achieves a 101:1 division ratio with high-value dropper resistors handling ±700 V common mode, with no [[transformer]] isolation at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup> Precision laboratory Kelvin-Varley dividers extend the idea into decade strings, tapping successive decades; a seven-decade instrument reaches 0.1 ppm resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

## Biasing, level shifting and virtual grounds

Single-supply analog circuits need a mid-rail reference, and a divider is the standard way to make it: a resistor to V+ and a resistor to [[ground]], tapped at half rail, maximises headroom for an op-amp stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> A signal riding on ground would clip, so it is shifted up onto a [[dc-offset|DC offset]] set by a resistor divider — half or two-thirds of the supply — with filtering on the tap to keep it clean.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup> Where the pseudo-ground must supply current, the divider tap is buffered with a voltage follower to make it low impedance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

In battery-powered low-[[noise]] designs the divider approach beats the alternatives on cost and noise: a switching element is a bad idea in a microvolt-level design and adds bill-of-materials parts and capacitors, whereas splitting a 3 V battery with two series resistors of around 100 k draws negligible current and taps 1.5 V directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> The op amp buffering such a splitter is itself selected for lowest possible power consumption.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> The divider resistors that create a virtual reference can be a stability liability, though: bypassing the divider leg with a 0.1 µF ceramic is one route to killing [[oscillation]] in a circuit driving a capacitive load through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup>

## Thresholds, comparators and references

Comparator threshold networks are dividers. In a Schmitt trigger the upper threshold is R2 over R1 plus R2 times the maximum output swing — the voltage divider formula unchanged — so 1 k over a 10 k total gives 0.1 × Vout, or +1 V for a ±10 V comparator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> Referencing the divider to something other than ground shifts the trip points wholesale; the formula becomes messier but the mechanism is unchanged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> The same structure appears inside CMOS Schmitt triggers, where transistors rather than resistors do the dividing: as the input rises, N1 turns on and forms a divider with N3 between VCC and ground, biasing the source of N2 to a threshold level, with the mirror behaviour occurring in the P-channel devices on the way back down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

The LM3914 bar-graph driver exposes its internal divider string directly: the upper and lower threshold pins sit across the divider resistors that set each comparator's threshold and hence each [[led|LED]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> Interfacing it to a battery is a divider problem in both directions — an external divider halves the [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] so it falls inside the workable common-mode range of the internal comparators, and a further divider on R1 is needed if the reference span must be smaller than the native 1.25 V range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> The same reference-plus-divider pattern sets adjustable outputs on comparator-based charger circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>

Divider networks are also the only practical route to non-standard voltage references. A bandgap cannot be built below about 1.2 V, so a 0.3 V reference must be divided down from a higher one and buffered with an op-amp follower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> The choice is between 0.1 % precision resistors and ordinary resistors trimmed with a pot; either way the resistor [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] dominates the result, and there is no way to do it other than a divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Commercial DMM check sources pair a nominal 0.05 % 10 V reference with a divider for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup>

## Precision, trimming and failure

Divider accuracy is resistor accuracy. Standard 1 % resistors in a panel-meter input divider produced a 16 mV offset that had to be trimmed out with an added component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> Trim range is itself a design parameter: a divider of 100 k and 47 k with a trim across it can offer an absurdly large adjustment range, which is poor practice and should be limited.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Where an ADC feeds from a divider, the ratio is chosen to land the full-scale signal on the reference — a divider scaled so the ADC sees exactly one fifth of the output voltage, matching a 2.04 V reference and a ×5 gain elsewhere in the chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Scaling a 200 mV panel meter to read 0 to 2 V or 0 to 2 A means a divide-by-ten input divider, and rescaling the instrument to a finer range is done by adjusting the divider resistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9lIC3ZzIht4" title="Junk box Digital Panel Meter Analysis CX101">•</a></sup>

Dividers also fail. In a resistor-capacitor divider, a 220 nF capacitor that has degraded to 100 nF changes the division ratio and takes the circuit with it — a failure with no bulging or other visible sign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> Divider values that do not reconcile with the measured tap voltage are a diagnostic signal in themselves: 47 k, 330 and 680 that cannot produce the 2.8 V present at a test point indicates something else in the circuit is off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup>

## Configuration, monitoring and other roles

Dividers are used as passive identity straps. An oscilloscope reads a voltage divider at boot to determine which [[bandwidth]] model it is, and the corresponding selection resistors on the main board are the target when the model configuration is investigated — a pot substituted for a divider resistor avoids [[desoldering]] resistor values repeatedly during experiments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1418" title="EEVblog 1418 - The Most EMBARRASSING Repair!">1418</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-978" title="EEVblog #978 - Keysight 1000X Oscilloscope Hacked!">978</a></sup> Instruments spanning a wide model range likewise differ in hardware, with different dividers and ADC ranges between an 8 V and a 600 V [[power-supply|power supply]] variant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>

Rail monitoring is a divider per rail: a supply board scaling −12, +12, −5.2, 105, 80 and 120 V rails down for measurement uses one divider for each incoming voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup> Multi-channel measurement follows the same pattern — dividers ahead of a multiplexer feeding a single ADC, with the [[microcontroller]] selecting which cell voltage to measure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup> Dividers scale current-sense and battery-tap voltages into ADC range in chargers and dummy loads,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> set up a thermistor for [[temperature-measurement|temperature measurement]] in a lower-impedance arrangement,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> and, with a [[parallel-resistor|parallel resistor]] added for the open-circuit case, let an ADC compute switch [[contact-resistance|contact resistance]] around 0.9 Ω in an automated cycling jig.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup>

Beyond DC, a divider straight off the rectified mains feeds the multiplier input of a [[power-factor-correction|power factor correction]] controller so the loop can track the rectified [[waveform]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup> Three-phase energy monitors use MELF resistor pairs as the input divider, one pair per phase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> Overload detection scales an input down before it reaches an op amp so that headroom above the positive supply is always preserved, with a 1 M and 270 k pair and a matching 100 k and 22 k elsewhere in the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Digital systems use dividers too — midpoint termination in fast DDR memory is built from them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Small dividers also drive discrete stages directly, as in an EPIRB where a divider drives an NPN transistor into a microcontroller pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup>

## Standing

Dividers occupy about one page in a typical textbook, which understates them: the formula is used in practically every aspect of electronics, unlike much of the theory an engineer learns and never touches again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Introductory hardware teaching aids group them with Kirchhoff's laws and series-parallel combinations as core material.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1695" title="EEVblog 1695 - Mailbag: Robin RCD Tester, PCB Book, USB Knob, Geekom IT12 Mini PC">1695</a></sup> The practical test is simple — using a ×10 oscilloscope probe correctly is not possible without understanding voltage division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>
