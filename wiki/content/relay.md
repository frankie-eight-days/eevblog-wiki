A relay is an electromechanical switch in which energizing a coil of wire pulls an armature across, moving a contact from one position to another, so that a low-power control circuit switches a separate, often mains-level or high-[[current]], circuit with full galvanic isolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> Despite being one of the oldest switching technologies in electronics, relays remain everywhere in modern equipment: they select ranges and input paths in oscilloscopes and multimeters, switch [[transformer]] taps in linear power supplies, connect mains to heating elements and EV charge cables, and provide mandated disconnection in grid-tied inverters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1643" title="EEVblog 1643 - DON&#x27;T BUY this Cheap 7kW 32A EV EVSE Charger">1643</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>

## Construction and coil drive

Inside a typical sealed relay are the coil and the contacts; activating the coil pulls the armature across and transfers the contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> A relay has a minimum coil turn-on [[voltage]], typically specified on the [[datasheet|data sheet]] as a percentage of the nominal coil voltage, on the order of 80 percent; a 24 V relay starved of coil voltage will fail to actuate even while the rest of the control electronics works normally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> Coils are driven from [[transistor]] stages, from high-voltage Darlington driver arrays such as the DS2004 (which integrate the catch diodes), or directly from open-collector [[comparator]] outputs rated to tens of volts and tens of milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-606" title="EEVblog #606 - Mailbag">606</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>

A relay coil is an [[inductor]] storing energy in its [[magnetic-field]], so interrupting the coil current produces back EMF: by Lenz's law the induced voltage opposes the change that caused it, and the resulting spike can destroy the drive transistor unless a freewheeling (clamp, flyback, or back EMF) [[diode]] is fitted across the coil.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> A demonstration of this uses a 2SC2610 transistor rated at 300 V specifically to survive the spike when the diode is removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> The same mechanism appears at the contacts when a relay switches an inductive load such as a motor: opening the contacts can produce high-voltage arcing across them, which is why a clamp or a [[resistor]]-[[capacitor]] snubber may be fitted across the switching element as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup>

## Signal switching in test and measurement equipment

[[oscilloscope]] front ends use relays to select the 50 ohm versus 1 megohm input path and to switch AC or DC coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup> The attenuation range changeover is audible as a relay click — for example at the 100 to 200 mV/div boundary on both a GW Instek GDS-1000B and a Keysight HD3, where two major attenuation ranges cover the input span.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> Models without [[50-ohm-termination]] simply omit the relay, as on the Rigol DHO800 relative to its stablemates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup> Relay counts in scope front ends run high: four relays per channel in the Siglent SDS2000X, and relays "everywhere" in the Owon XDS3202A, including eight for the arbitrary [[waveform]] generator and pass/fail outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup> A current probe switches between its 10 A and 100 A ranges with a relay rather than electronic switching because of the parasitics a solid-state switch would introduce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> Selecting 50 ohm output [[impedance]] on an arbitrary waveform generator does not insert a physical 50 ohm resistor with a relay; the [[resistance]] is always in circuit and the high-impedance option only rescales the permitted output voltage in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup>

Precision instruments place the heaviest demands on relay quality. The Fluke 5450A resistance calibrator contains 34 relays, documented on an internal relay map listing which relays energize per range; the low-value decades require top-quality low-contact-resistance relays, with multiple contacts paralleled to bring [[contact-resistance]] and long-term error within specification, while the megaohm ranges use Coto reed relays where contact resistance no longer matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-545" title="EEVblog #545 - Vintage Design Rant">545</a></sup> Reed relays also serve RF and switching-matrix applications, with Pickering relays specified for switching from DC to 65 GHz at prices up to $10 per relay.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup>

In multimeters the [[auto-ranging|autoranging]] or jack-switching relay is often audible and can dominate measurement speed: the Agilent 34461A's fast ohms reading includes the time to physically switch its relay, without which it would be near-instantaneous.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> Autoranging relays that flicker back and forth under 50 Hz mains pickup are a genuine design defect — a meter needs a settable number of power line cycles so the relays are not worn by continuous hunting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup>

## Power switching

At the mains end, relays switch heating elements directly across the line in fan heaters and ovens — a 3 kW oven element visibly cycles on and off under relay control — and a pair of relays reverses polarity across a Peltier device to select heating or cooling in a thermal chamber.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gAu8CvMjDrU" title="15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery - First Analysis">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup> The Sinclair C5 used a single relay as its entire motor speed control, giving strictly binary on/off drive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-501" title="EEVblog #501 - Sinclair C5 Electric Car Teardown &amp; Test Drive">501</a></sup> Linear bench supplies use relays to select transformer secondary taps, keeping headroom across the pass transistor reasonable; the Rigol DP832 substitutes triacs for this function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-30" title="EEVblog #30 - Jaycar Bench Lab Power Supply Review">30</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TQcV3ftPLgA" title="Matrix PSU Unboxings &amp; Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Multi-output supplies use relays for internal series/parallel and tracking interconnection so channels can be combined without external shorting bars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup>

An EVSE is not a charger; it is essentially a controller, a [[pwm]] pilot signal, and relays that connect mains straight through to the vehicle, which contains the actual charger — both active and neutral are switched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1643" title="EEVblog 1643 - DON&#x27;T BUY this Cheap 7kW 32A EV EVSE Charger">1643</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5rZk8mNvYUc" title="2020 Hyundai IONIQ AC Charger FAIL?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup> In grid-tied hybrid inverters, two relays are wired in series on the grid connection because one failure mode of a relay is the contacts arcing over and welding permanently short; the redundant series contact is a regulatory disconnection requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup> A mechanical automatic transfer switch rated at 63 A transfers in 50 milliseconds with an audible clunk, whereas critical loads such as server racks use electronic versions with effectively zero switchover time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1500" title="EEVblog 1500 - Automatic Transfer Switch REVERSE ENGINEERED">1500</a></sup> In a defibrillator, relays hold the patient terminals isolated until the SCRs and IGBTs sequence the discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup>

## Latching relays

Latching relays retain their state with no coil power, which suits [[battery]]-powered instruments: the ANENG Q1 [[multimeter]] uses a latching Hongfa relay specifically so no current is wasted holding the coil energized.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup> In mains equipment the choice is more debatable; an EVSE fitted with latching relays holds its contacts closed through a power loss, and the engineering preference there is a normally open non-latching relay that drops out and requires the control electronics to actively maintain the energized state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1507" title="EEVblog 1507 - Mida: A surprisingly good cheap portable Electric Car EVSE Charger">1507</a></sup>

## Reliability, failure modes, and troubleshooting

Relays are the classic electromechanical weak link, yet in at least one long-term railway PA amplifier application they proved "ridiculously reliable," even when socketed in a high-vibration environment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup> Wear-out is real under rapid cycling: a stepper motor sequenced at speed by PLC relays in ladder logic would destroy the relays within days.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-940" title="EEVblog #940 - Mailbag">940</a></sup> Particulate contamination can prevent contact closure entirely — a single grain of dust in a relay was among the failure mechanisms behind NASA's adoption of clean rooms around 1958.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Uk5F3rQNUkU" title="ESD Explained &amp; Demonstrated + History of the Pink Polyethylene ESD Bag">•</a></sup> Contacts can also be damaged by breaking substantial current or short circuits, an experiment that destroyed one channel of a lab [[power-supply]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup>

The audible click is a first-line diagnostic. Its absence when a load should energize points immediately to missing coil drive — in one heater repair, a degraded X2 capacitor in a capacitive-dropper supply left the 24 V rail too low to pull the relay in while the micro and display kept working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup> Conversely, hearing relays click during power-on tells the troubleshooter the standby supply, soft-power logic, and much of the digital chain are alive before any display appears.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup>

## Components and construction practice

Brand matters: NEC, Omron, and other Japanese relays are consistently noted as the quality choice, with shielded Omron parts, TE Connectivity Schrack types, and Japanese relays in instrument front ends singled out favorably, while unbranded or obscure relays attract suspicion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1263" title="EEVblog #1263 - Mailbag">1263</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Relays are also a significant bill-of-materials item, and removing them is a recognized cost-saving move in lower-tier instrument variants.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup> Magnetically shielded relays with mu-metal shields exist for sensitive front ends; stick-on shields over unshielded relays are a cheaper afterthought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup> A stated bench preference favors designs that solve switching problems with relays rather than solid-state alternatives — a self-described "relay fanboy" position — on the grounds that an audible clunk of relays at power-up instills confidence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup> Relays are also among the worthwhile salvage items from scrapped power supplies, printers, and UPS units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1302" title="EEVblog #1302 - Scrapping a Dumpster Laser Printer For Parts">1302</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1168" title="EEVblog #1168 - Dumpster Diving UPS">1168</a></sup>
