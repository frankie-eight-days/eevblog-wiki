An accelerometer is a sensor that measures acceleration, and it appears in electronics in two quite different guises: as a precision instrumentation transducer used to characterise how a product responds to shock and vibration,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> and as a cheap integrated MEMS device buried inside consumer hardware to sense motion, tilt and orientation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> The instrumentation variety is expensive, fussy about mounting, and requires specialised signal conditioning;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> the MEMS variety costs a few dollars, talks over I²C or [[spi|SPI]], and is now so ubiquitous that its presence in a teardown is unremarkable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fm13tIe5wSc" title="EEVacademy #4 -  I²C (I2C) Bit Banging">•</a></sup>

For a design engineer, the accelerometer's most consequential role is in qualification testing: strapping one to a [[pcb|PCB]] on a shaker table and sweeping [[frequency]] is how a product's mechanical resonances and long-term reliability risks under transport are discovered before the product ships.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

## Vibration and shock testing

Products destined for military, automotive or industrial markets are tested against standards that specify the vibrational modes they must survive, typically simulating transport by road, rail and air.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> The method is to mount the product to a plate on an electrodynamic shaker, attach a reference accelerometer to the plate as the input channel and a second accelerometer to the product as the output channel, and sweep the drive over the frequency range the standard demands — from around 5 Hz up to many kilohertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> Facilities with car-sized shaker tables capable of shaking whole products are hired for this work, with custom mounts designed for each unit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup>

The instrument on the receiving end is a dynamic signal analyser, or [[fft|FFT]] analyser: two wide-dynamic-range inputs with high-gain, low-[[noise]] front ends, adequate for sound, vibration, mechanical and shock response work, plus a built-in source for sine and noise excitation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup> Such an analyser can extract cross-correlation between the two channels and other derived parameters, which is what makes it the right tool rather than a general-purpose scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup>

What the second accelerometer reveals is board flex. A small board is not worth instrumenting, but a large PCB mounted only at its corners or on rails — in a rack, say — will flex in the middle, so the accelerometer goes in the middle to capture it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> Mounting-point selection therefore matters a great deal mechanically, not just structurally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> The same principle applies to sub-assemblies: a daughter board with a single unsoldered tab locating it at one point gets a low-mass accelerometer attached and is shaken in multiple axes to find the vibrational mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1239" title="EEVblog #1239 - Mailbag">1239</a></sup> A board deliberately weakened by a spiral cutout, as in some instrument mechanical designs, will have a mechanical resonant frequency that ought to be characterised with a reference accelerometer swept across both operating and non-operating transport conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

Vibration is not only a mechanical concern. Crystal and MEMS oscillators are susceptible to shock and vibration, and dropping and shocking crystal oscillators on a purpose-built jig with accelerometers attached shows that a shock resets the drift characteristic of the [[oscillator]] — a very small effect, but decisive for high-stability oscillators such as those used in underwater seismic recorders.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> The same class of failure shows up in high-performance computing hardware, where loss of clock output from oscillators was traced to a vibration mode and modelled with accelerometers; remedies include specifying automotive-grade anti-vibration capacitors or selecting a different MEMS oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> Accelerometer boards also serve as the payload itself: three-axis accelerometer boards inside ocean-bottom seismic recorders for oil exploration, deployed in time-synced arrays on the seafloor by remotely operated vehicles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NX2mOOpPs2I" title="eevBLAB 119 - The History of the EEVBlog &quot;Hand&quot; Photo">•</a></sup>

## Instrumentation accelerometers in practice

The dominant names in the field are PCB Piezotronics — a company name unrelated to printed circuit boards, despite the coincidence — and Brüel & Kjær, which acquired LDS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> Some units sold under one brand are manufactured by another and rebadged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup> Low-mass shear accelerometers designed for small systems are tiny, carry BNC connectors, and are supplied as [[adhesive]] mounts to be superglued to the item under test, often in several locations and orientations to capture different axes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Alternatives exist with magnetic mounts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>

The cost structure is severe. A calibrated electrodynamic shaker runs to around $10,000, a single accelerometer to around $1,000, and NIST-traceable [[calibration]] of that accelerometer another thousand on top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Charge amplifiers, shakers, power amplifiers and test systems at the high end are budgeted in the five- to ten-thousand-dollar range per item as a matter of course in military and comparable fields.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> Recalibration can cost more than replacement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup> Uncalibrated units remain useful, however: without calibration the measurements are not quantitative and cannot be held against a standard, but they still reveal where a product has problems.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup>

ICP-type accelerometers are, as bench experience has it, "touchy little beasties" — they need a [[constant-current-source|constant current source]], typically a couple of milliamps up to about 10 mA with 5 mA a reasonable setting, and a [[compliance-voltage|compliance voltage]] somewhere between 18 V and 30 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>

## Mounting, coherence and measurement validity

The single largest source of bad vibration data is the jig, not the instrument. Calibrated gear does not guarantee valid measurements; if the fixture is not set up properly the results are useless, and the check must be performed before any serious measurement is taken.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> The check is a coherence measurement between the shaker drive signal and the accelerometer output. Good coherence sits close to one across the whole frequency range; uncorrelated noise drives it down — electrical pickup, or triboelectric noise generated when vibration couples into the signal cables themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>

Concrete demonstrations of how badly this can go wrong:

- An accelerometer taped to the plate right at the armature gives coherence close to one across the range; gluing it down improves matters only marginally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Moved out to the edge of the plate where it is unsupported, the response degrades badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>
- Screwing a PCB onto the fixture, with nothing else changed, introduced a deep notch around 32 Hz. Without the coherence check, the resulting weird results from the product accelerometer would be mistaken for a modal mechanical problem in the product itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>
- Swapping the tiny low-mass sensor for a much heavier one — a Vibra Metrics Model 7000, old but still serviceable, designed for larger systems — changes the loading on the structure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Mass loading is why low-mass accelerometers exist for PCB work in the first place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1239" title="EEVblog #1239 - Mailbag">1239</a></sup>
- Not every artefact is mechanical. A dramatic feature at around 65 Hz turned out to be the analyser's auto-range, not the jig and not the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>

The practical rule is that a suspicious response should be blamed on the setup until coherence proves otherwise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>

## MEMS accelerometers in products

The modern integrated part is a MEMS accelerometer, frequently combined with other sensors on one die. Older devices offered an analog output or a simple serial output; [[current]] parts integrate a three-axis gyroscope and a three-axis accelerometer with a digital motion [[processor]] on the same die, capable of running nine-axis motion fusion algorithms and providing an external I²C bus for a magnetometer, along with motion-fusion and runtime-calibration [[firmware]] that removes the cost and qualification burden of selecting and integrating discrete devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> Comparable single-package IMUs include a Bosch BMI160 with triaxial accelerometer and triaxial gyroscope at 16 bits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup> A robot vacuum's navigation module combined a MEMS gyroscope with a three-axis accelerometer and Kalman filtering to measure angular rates, heading angle, orientation and acceleration under dynamic conditions — the gyroscope being the part that matters for returning to a known starting point such as a charging pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1523" title="EEVblog 1523 - Dumpster Samsung Cleaning Robot TEARDOWN">1523</a></sup> Modules of this kind, along with a tablet's GPS, accelerometer, magnetometer and gyroscope complement, are conventional system-level inventory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-322" title="EEVblog #322 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Review">322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-551" title="EEVblog #551 - Sydney Mini Maker Faire 2013">551</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup>

Automotive safety is a demanding application. An airbag controller used SPI-based two-axis medium-G over-damped lateral accelerometers designed specifically for airbag systems, spanning ±20 g to ±120 g, single supply, with 10-bit signed or unsigned SPI output and twelve low-pass filter options from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz plus optional offset cancellation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup> One variant covered 120 g on the x-axis and ±25 g on the y-axis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup> Older airbag controllers used mechanical inertial switches; modern ones use MEMS accelerometers, and the deployment decision draws on multiple sensors distributed around the vehicle — buckle switches, pressure sensors and several accelerometers routed into a dedicated interface chip — with more than one required to agree before the squib drivers fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup>

## Bandwidth and interfacing

Mechanical sensors are slow by digital standards. Strain gauges on bridges need only a few hertz of [[sample-rate|sample rate]], and accelerometers rarely exceed tens of kilohertz, so an I²C bus operating in the megabit range comfortably accommodates them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fm13tIe5wSc" title="EEVacademy #4 -  I²C (I2C) Bit Banging">•</a></sup> Taking an unrealistically fast case — an 8-bit accelerometer sampled at 32 kHz — the raw payload is 32 kHz × 8 bits = 256 kbit/s, and even doubling that to account for address overhead in each frame leaves ample margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fm13tIe5wSc" title="EEVacademy #4 -  I²C (I2C) Bit Banging">•</a></sup> This is why accelerometers are routinely hung off I²C rather than a faster bus.

## Motion sensing as a user interface

Once the part costs almost nothing, the accelerometer becomes an input device rather than an instrument:

- **Sleep and wake.** [[soldering|Soldering]] stations detect that the handpiece has been picked up or set down. One iron went into setback mode exactly 30 seconds after being released and came back on with a small wiggle; another timed out to sleep on a settable 0–59 minute interval using an accelerometer in the handle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1645" title="EEVblog 1645 - $80 Quick TS11 Soldering Station REVIEW">1645</a></sup> An accelerometer-driven display colour change on idle versus movement is the same mechanism in another form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>
- **Knock input.** A solar [[inverter]]'s front panel used the accelerometer as a knock sensor: knocking on the case changes mode, avoiding external buttons entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1JgdM0GsCjk" title="Sunny Boy Solar Inverter Removal">•</a></sup>
- **Orientation and screen flip.** A camera detects its articulated screen being flipped up while the camera body itself does not move, which requires the sensor to be in the screen assembly rather than the body — the alternative being a contact or microswitch triggered past a certain angle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1429" title="EEVblog 1429 - Sony RX100 Camera Troubleshooting Part 2">1429</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1428" title="EEVblog 1428 - Sony RX100 IV Camera Repair and then WTF!">1428</a></sup> Some instruments lack the sensor and require the display to be rotated in software instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>
- **Step counting and wearables.** Pedometers and fitness bands pair an optical pulse sensor with an accelerometer for step counting and sleep tracking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-809" title="EEVblog #809 - Mailbag">809</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-412" title="EEVblog #412 - Jawbone UP Pedometer Teardown">412</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup> Teardowns show the accelerometer as a small package sitting on a flex membrane among several stacked boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-412" title="EEVblog #412 - Jawbone UP Pedometer Teardown">412</a></sup>
- **Toys and novelty devices.** Tilt and tip-over detection complements capacitive touch pads in interactive toys.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-756" title="EEVblog #756 - Mailbag">756</a></sup> A persistence-of-vision [[led|LED]] wand without an accelerometer must free-run rather than trigger from a defined start point in the swipe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> A screwdriver's smart mode uses an accelerometer to select rotation direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1463" title="EEVblog 1463 - Mailbag">1463</a></sup>

## Motion compensation

Two applications use accelerometer data to cancel unwanted motion after the fact. High-speed 3D printers carry an accelerometer in the print head and correct on the fly for [[overshoot]], whiplash and related mechanical artefacts of a fast-moving mass; without that correction the achievable print speeds — up to 600 mm/s printing, higher when travelling — would not be usable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-jFapDuowXKU" title="Mailbag: Creality K1 Max 3D Printer: Unboxing + Setup + First Print">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fRlugAWeWR4" title="Creality K1 Max, Print Software, Remote View, Print &amp; Capture FAIL">•</a></sup>

The second is video stabilisation. Certain compact cameras with an internal accelerometer but no optical stabilisation in the lens will, when internal digital stabilisation is switched off, embed the raw accelerometer data into the MP4 file instead of discarding it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-c167h_7feyM" title="Sony&#x27;s Catalyst Image Stabilisation Software is AMAZING!">•</a></sup> Post-processing software reads that embedded data and stabilises in software, which yields visibly better results than the in-body digital stabilisation because the software is not constrained to work in real time and can be applied at a chosen strength.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-c167h_7feyM" title="Sony&#x27;s Catalyst Image Stabilisation Software is AMAZING!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DqurmxiW4mM" title="Sony Catalyst Image Stabilisation 4K Test">•</a></sup> The engineering judgement that follows is to disable in-camera digital stabilisation on any camera that supports embedding the data, and stabilise afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-c167h_7feyM" title="Sony&#x27;s Catalyst Image Stabilisation Software is AMAZING!">•</a></sup>

## When it is not an accelerometer

Motion detection in cheap products is often cruder than it appears. A knock-to-wake lamp turned out to use a ball-contact movement sensor — audible as a rattle when shaken — rather than an accelerometer, with the [[microcontroller]] sleeping until the contact changes state.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-705" title="EEVblog #705 - Mailbag">705</a></sup> Mercury tilt switches serve a similar function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1155" title="EEVblog #1155 - Mailbag">1155</a></sup> And a parachute automatic activation device contained only a pressure sensor and a microprocessor, with no accelerometer at all, inferring altitude and rate of fall from pressure alone before firing its pyrotechnic charge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-339" title="EEVblog #339 - Cypres Parachute AAD Teardown">339</a></sup>

Identification from a teardown photograph is correspondingly uncertain: an unmarked small package near a [[battery]] in a fitness tracker is a plausible accelerometer but not a confirmed one without a marking code lookup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup> A DIP16 package is an unusual home for one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1155" title="EEVblog #1155 - Mailbag">1155</a></sup>

## Development boards

Accelerometers are common on evaluation hardware because they give a beginner something physical to interact with. An [[fpga|FPGA]] development board pairs a Cyclone IV with an accelerometer, ADC, [[eeprom|EEPROM]], DIP switches, push buttons, 40-pin GPIO headers and 32 Mbit of SDRAM, and ships an accelerometer demo project that can be opened directly in the vendor toolchain, compiled and reprogrammed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-636" title="EEVblog #636 - FPGA Demo Boards - DE0 Nano">636</a></sup> A microcontroller contest platform included three MEMS sensors — a three-axis accelerometer, a digital thermometer and a microphone — alongside its Ethernet, [[usb|USB]] and CAN peripherals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-122" title="EEVblog #122 - Renesas RX Design Contest Announcement">122</a></sup> Analog parts kits bundle an accelerometer with instrumentation amps, precision op-amps, [[voltage]] references, [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] sensors and [[current-shunt|current shunt]] monitors, with the surface-mount parts pre-populated on SMD-to-DIP adapters so they can be used on a [[breadboard]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup> Sensor-laden [[arduino|Arduino]]-compatible boards and model rocket flight computers follow the same pattern, the latter combining accelerometers and compasses for attitude with altitude measurement and two servo outputs for staging and parachute deployment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-899" title="EEVblog #899 - Extreme Mailbag!">899</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1558" title="EEVblog 1558 - Mailbag">1558</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-592" title="EEVblog #592 - Mailbag">592</a></sup>
