A pick and place machine is the automated placement stage of a surface-mount assembly line: it takes components from reels, tubes and trays and sets them onto a solder-pasted board, one after another, with no manual handling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> It exists to remove labour from board assembly — the through-hole alternative is a line of workers installing parts by hand, which costs time, effort and money — and a modern placement machine churns out boards far faster with far less effort, set up once and then run at the push of a button.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> The machine does not solder anything: it presses each part into the wet solder paste, which is tacky enough to hold the component in position until the board reaches the reflow oven, where the pre-applied paste actually forms the joints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup>

Because the machine sits at the centre of the manufacturing cost model, its constraints propagate backwards into [[schematic]] capture, bill-of-materials selection, footprint choice and panelisation. Much of what is called design for manufacture is really design for the placement machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

## Head, nozzles and placement speed

The head picks a part up with a vacuum nozzle, carries it across the board and sets it down; the component is held only by suction for the whole of that traverse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-197" title="RAW! Testing µCurrent&#x27;s, Keithley Current/Voltage Sources, DIY Pick And Place - EEVblog #197">197</a></sup> Cycle time is dominated by the XY return trip between the feeder bank and the board, so machines carry multiple nozzles to amortise that travel: a dual-nozzle head picks two components before moving to the board, eliminating one round trip, and a six-nozzle head can pick six passives at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup> Feeders arranged so that two parts can be picked in succession without returning to the start are a real time advantage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup>

Part mass sets the speed limit. Lighter components can be accelerated harder without being flung off the nozzle, so small passives move quickly while a large power [[resistor]] forces the head to slow down for both the pick and the traverse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The very smallest parts also demand different hardware — an ultra-high-speed head capable of 0201 is a physically different head that has to be fitted or selected on the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

Two broad technology classes exist. Cheaper and older machines place 0603 and larger reliably; they may manage 0402 but with worse yield, so an assembler may charge more for 0402 placement because the job has to go on one of their more advanced machines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Components mounted at 45 degrees are handled without difficulty and need no special accommodation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

Throughput is quoted in components per minute on production equipment,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1468" title="EEVblog 1468 - Electronex Show Tour 2022">1468</a></sup> in components per hour on prototyping machines — a small-run machine of that class runs around 3,500 components an hour while still handling micro BGAs and 0201 packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup> A complete panel of ten small boards has been timed at just under one minute forty seconds from entering the head to leaving the far side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup>

## Alignment: fiducials and vision

The machine locates the board optically. A fiducial is a reference mark read by the placement camera so the machine knows where to place components accurately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> The typical form is a 1 mm diameter circle with the [[solder-mask]] cleared around it, acting as a vision alignment point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Fiducials belong on the copper layer, never on [[silkscreen]]: silkscreen registers imperfectly against copper, and any offset between the two would be transferred directly into placement error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> A panel usually carries three fiducials, top and bottom, along with tooling holes in the corners.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup> Fiducials are normally placed outside a package, though a single mark centred under a large chip is also used, with the vision system finding that mark as the chip centre.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

Component-level vision closes the loop on the part itself: a fixed downward-looking camera images the picked component, finds its centre and computes an offset, so a part that came out of the tape at the wrong angle is autocorrected before placement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup> Higher-end machines add laser alignment on each head alongside full camera alignment and a nozzle changer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup> Adding vision to a home-built machine is now easy and cheap, which was not the case ten or fifteen years earlier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-740" title="EEVblog #740 - LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine Kit Unboxing">740</a></sup>

## Feeders and reel capacity

Every distinct part number on a board occupies its own feeder. Components arrive on reels — a thousand or three thousand parts to a reel is ordinary — and the reels mount into feeders carried on wheel-in carts, front and back of the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> A large machine might carry sixty or a hundred feeders,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> a mid-range one twenty to fifty reels,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> and a semi-automatic twin-head machine up to 120.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup> ICs can be supplied in tubes that slip into the machine and shoot the chips out,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> and parts made in waffle trays are picked directly from the tray instead of from tape, at the cost of holding fewer parts and needing changing more often.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup>

Feeder count is a hard ceiling on a design. A machine that supports only forty reels is already half consumed by a design carrying twenty-six distinct [[capacitor]] types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Wide tape occupies several slots at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Once a design exceeds the feeder capacity of a single machine — around eighty line items on one particular machine — the board is not unmanufacturable, it simply has to go through multiple passes, or through two or more machines in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Production lines are built this way deliberately: three physical machines in series can present something like 300 feeders, with one machine often loaded entirely with passives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> A board too big for one machine's feeder complement is routed through the first, second and third machines in turn before reaching the reflow oven.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> Even a modest board can require loading up several machines because the reels will not fit on one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>

Parts must be supplied in a form the machine can accept. Cut tape with no reel and no leader tape is useless to an assembly house.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Loose tape can be handled by some machines, but many assemblers will have to re-reel it and will charge for doing so.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup> Machines also lose parts during placement, so consigned stock should include a few percent overage — a rule of thumb of around 5% for cheap passives, with expensive precision parts worth flagging for extra care.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup>

## Bill-of-materials consolidation

Because a feeder slot is a scarce resource, consolidating part values is a direct manufacturing saving. Seven different resistor values on one small board is worth questioning: each one eliminated is one less reel to put on the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup> Where a calculation calls for an odd value such as 4.99 k, two 10 k resistors in parallel reuse an existing line item instead of buying another reel and consuming another slot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Two identical MOSFETs placed back to back give a second body [[diode]] from the same bill-of-materials item, saving a reel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> Choosing a 1 A [[transistor]] already on the machine over a lower-rated part that saves two cents is normally the correct trade, once the extra reel, the sourcing effort and the machine slot are counted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The same logic favours standing jellybean support values: keeping a 240 ohm resistor permanently loaded for [[lm317]] designs and reusing it elsewhere rather than adding a 220 ohm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Where several bulk capacitors of one value are used in parallel, the design gets bulk pricing and still occupies only one feeder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Companies that manufacture in house apply this across their whole catalogue, since a new component means new stock to purchase, store, load onto the machines and change over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ag-MjKAfATw" title="Rode AI-1 USB Audio Interface Teardown">•</a></sup>

## Board handling and panelisation

The machine grips the board by a tooling strip along the top and bottom edges, about 10 mm wide, which lets it sit in rails and be moved through automatically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Tooling holes of around 3.2 mm hold the board in place and move it along the conveyor into the machine and out to the reflow oven.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1158" title="EEVblog #1158 - How To Create PCB Mod Boards">1158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> The panel frame is otherwise dead area — a mounting frame carrying tooling holes and fiducials and nothing else — though it can be used to carry test or other functional circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

Panelising is what makes small boards economic. A single small [[pcb]] fed through on its own leaves the machine badly under-utilised, since it can only place one board's worth of parts before the conveyor moves on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Stepping twelve boards into one panel lets all twelve be assembled in one loading; the parts still go down one at a time, but the process overhead is amortised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> With two boards on a panel the head, having already picked a part, can place it on both without a separate board pass — not quite double throughput, but close.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1299" title="EEVblog #1299 - Dumpster Laptop REPAIR">1299</a></sup> The assembler, not the bare-board fabricator, defines the ceiling: the PCB house states its maximum panel size and the assembly house states the maximum panel its machine can take.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup>

Stiffness matters as much as size. A panel held only at the top and bottom rails will sag in the middle when the head comes down to place parts, and a 0.8 mm panel warps visibly under its own weight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1158" title="EEVblog #1158 - How To Create PCB Mod Boards">1158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> The usual remedy is a custom tray from the assembler that the panel sits snugly inside, supported across its whole area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1158" title="EEVblog #1158 - How To Create PCB Mod Boards">1158</a></sup> Boards that get too thin become a persistent nuisance, particularly for double-sided loads where the assembled first side prevents the board from simply being laid on a flat carrier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup>

Length is rarely the binding constraint — height and width inside the machine are.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> A board over a metre long can be assembled either in one pass on a machine big enough to take it, or in halves, moving the board along and realigning between passes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup> Comparable limits govern [[led]] tape, where the placement machine's working area sets the strip length and longer runs are joined at the factory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> Fully routed boards with no breakaway tabs cannot ride the rails at all and are run in a custom jig instead, sometimes several at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-335" title="EEVblog #335 - Carbon Printed Resistors">335</a></sup>

## Design consequences

Single-sided loading is cheaper, so putting every component on one face is worth real effort — mounting power devices on the underside, or flipping the whole board, avoids a second placement operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> A double-sided load costs more and takes longer because the board must go through the machine at least twice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-219" title="Amazon Kindle Fire Tablet Teardown - EEVblog #219">219</a></sup> The same reasoning favours a light pipe through a hole in the board over a top-mounted LED that would force a second pass just for that part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-547" title="EEVblog #547 - Dumpster Phone Teardown">547</a></sup>

Anything that cannot go through the machine is an extra production step with its own cost: once a design commits to surface mount, the aim is for as much of it as possible to be surface mount so the whole board comes out of the machine finished, with no bolts, washers or bolted-on heatsinks to add afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> Surface-mount versions of normally through-hole parts serve this end — pin headers and connectors given SMD pads are placed like any other component and reflowed with everything else,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup> and surface-mount cells in an 1812 case can be placed with an ordinary machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> A cheap all-SMD display that eliminates hand [[soldering]] removes the hand-assembly labour from the price entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> Surface-mount switches are effectively machine-only parts: hand placement cannot achieve the alignment needed for keys to protrude cleanly through a front panel, and closely spaced switches leave no room for an iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11" title="EEVblog #11 Part 2 of 2 - More on DIY product design">11</a></sup>

Placement constraints can also show up as apparently odd layout. Bypass capacitors routed around the outside of a chip rather than tucked against its pins, together with unusually large footprints, are consistent with an assembly house's own placement requirements rather than an electrical choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Unpopulated alternative footprints let a product be re-spun quickly by changing only the placement program, so a different display or connector can be fitted to whatever is available that month.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup>

## The surrounding line

Placement sits between paste printing and reflow. Paste is applied through a stencil or dispensed automatically, inspected, then placed and reflowed with a thermal profile set individually for each board type.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 1 of 2 - Shanghai Special - PCB Assembly Factory Tour">12</a></sup> Pasted boards must reach the machine quickly — beyond roughly an hour the paste dries out and is no good.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-163" title="EEVblog #163 - Solder Paste Porn">163</a></sup> Fiducials serve the paste dispenser as well as the placement machine, giving it the same two-corner reference before it imports the paste file.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-163" title="EEVblog #163 - Solder Paste Porn">163</a></sup> After reflow the board normally goes to optical inspection, which compares it against a golden reference board and flags components that fell off the nozzle, were misplaced, or tombstoned; those are then reworked by hand, which is why an otherwise machine-built board sometimes carries a handful of visibly hand-soldered parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup>

The data handed to the assembler is a placement file listing each designator, its footprint and its XY location relative to a chosen reference point, usually the bottom-left corner, in CSV or a similar format; this is what gets fed into the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> It should be accompanied by the overlay diagram showing reference designators and by a physical sample built exactly as intended, because the assembler cannot infer intent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Programming the machine is a real job with a real cost — an emergency assembly that requires bumping other customers' jobs to program the machine immediately can be quoted at several times the normal price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VnrtneFP_6A" title="eevBLAB #51- Industry Story - Design Deadlines &amp; Unusual PCB Designs">•</a></sup>

## Machine cost and the small-volume question

Production equipment is expensive in a way that is not obvious from the machine price alone. A machine on its own may be around 100K, but each feeder costs about $2,000, so a fully equipped line comes to roughly half a million.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup> Prototyping-class equipment is cheaper but not cheap: an entry manual machine at about $19,000 Australian rises to roughly $22,000 with manual feeders and over $40,000 with a good number of automatic feeders, since automatic feeders are the expensive part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup> A desktop production machine sits around 15,000 Australian fully loaded with 48 feeders, against a 10K base price before feeders, freight and clearances.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uR69Vmh7qnI" title="NeoDen Pick &amp; Place Machine">•</a></sup> Do-it-yourself kits reach much lower — around $1,300 — but at 200 to 400 components per hour they are well short of professional throughput, and the kit in question shipped with no feeder support at all, requiring cut tape to be laid on the table for the camera to find.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-740" title="EEVblog #740 - LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine Kit Unboxing">740</a></sup> Feeders, not motion, are what make a machine genuinely usable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-740" title="EEVblog #740 - LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine Kit Unboxing">740</a></sup>

The practical judgment is that owning one has a narrow window of usefulness: the boards, the part count and the volume all have to line up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-740" title="EEVblog #740 - LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine Kit Unboxing">740</a></sup> Below that window, setup time exceeds the time to place parts by hand, and above small batches the bottleneck moves to workflow — paste, oven, handling — rather than to the placer itself, since twenty panels cannot simply be pasted in advance and queued without the paste drying.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-740" title="EEVblog #740 - LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine Kit Unboxing">740</a></sup> Reported experience with sub-$10,000 desktop machines centres on feeder reliability and absent vendor support rather than on placement accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5WxyCvXBoSY" title="LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine - Should I build it?">•</a></sup> For batches of around fifty boards, hand assembly remains the rational choice, and the deciding factor is not only the purchase price: as Ian Johnston puts it, "It's not just the cost of the pick and place, it's the learning."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a4Xpsenpd6E" title="The Amp Hour #643 - Calibration &amp; Repair with Ian Johnston">•</a></sup> The alternative is to hand the work to an assembler and pay per board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-a4Xpsenpd6E" title="The Amp Hour #643 - Calibration &amp; Repair with Ian Johnston">•</a></sup>

## Beyond PCBs

The same architecture — vacuum end effector, machine vision for orientation, computer-planned placement pattern — is applied outside electronics assembly. In flexible [[solar-panel]] production a robotic pick and place picks up individual die-cut modules, checks their orientation and lays them onto a fabric substrate in a computer-determined pattern before the fabric is laser-cut to outline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-S-UqkM8iDKM" title="#1179 - PowerFilm Flexible Indoor Solar Cell">•</a></sup> Vacuum-assisted manual lifting rigs used for handling boxes are described in the same terms, as the manufacturing equivalent of a placement machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1685" title="EEVblog 1685 - Australian Manufacturing Week 2025: Walkaround">1685</a></sup>
