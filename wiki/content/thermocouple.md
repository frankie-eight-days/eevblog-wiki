A thermocouple is two dissimilar metals joined at one end, which produces a differential [[voltage]] when the joined end and the free end sit at different temperatures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> It is the standard temperature probe supplied with multimeters, built into [[soldering]] irons and reflow ovens, and stuck onto heat sinks on the bench, because two bits of metal can cover ranges of many thousands of degrees Celsius and are extremely rugged, where the thermistors and other internal sensors an instrument already carries are accurate only over a narrow band.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> The price of that range is that a thermocouple never reports an absolute temperature: it reports a difference, and turning that difference into a reading takes compensation the user has to understand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup>

## Principle of operation

The effect is the Seebeck effect, named for Thomas Seebeck.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Every metal and alloy has its own Seebeck coefficient — output voltage in proportion to temperature, expressed in microvolts per degree C — so joining two of them and imposing a temperature gradient yields a voltage that depends on the pair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> A single metal such as a plain copper wire does produce the effect, but so inefficiently that it is useless as a sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Combining two materials in different ways gives different temperature ranges and different characteristics, which is why several thermocouple types exist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> The T-type is one simple case: copper runs from the sensing point all the way back through the connector and into the meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup>

The probe body is not a sensor in the usual sense. It is two different wires — commonly a red and a yellow inside the sheath, of different metals or alloys — running down to a junction at the tip, and what the instrument sees is a voltage differential rather than the absolute temperature at that tip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Because every metal or alloy has a known Seebeck coefficient, the effect also runs in reverse as an identification technique: the voltage a junction produces against a known material indicates what the unknown material is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Twisting two wires of different materials together is enough to make a working thermocouple to experiment with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup>

## Types, curves and accuracy

K-type dominates in general instrumentation, and almost all multimeters that measure temperature use it, a consequence of the metal composition chosen for the two wires.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Other types exist alongside it — a dumpster-recovered controller offered K-type, a WRE05 and a BSRTJ among its sensor selections — differing in their mixture of metals and therefore in temperature response, range, and junction behaviour, which is precisely why compensation is type-specific.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup>

The K-type curve reaches roughly 50 mV at about 1250 °C and looks encouragingly linear at first glance, as though the meter need only measure the junction voltage and scale it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> It is not that simple: plotted as Seebeck coefficient against temperature, the response is only approximately constant, and real accuracy demands the correction data be taken into account.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> A K-type probe is typically good to around 1.5 to 2.5 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> Cheap thermocouples generally carry a nominal ±2% accuracy figure, which is all that is available at that price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-612" title="EEVblog #612 - Mailbag">612</a></sup> The probe bundled with a Fluke 28 Series II is on the order of 1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> Even the flimsiest bundled probe, on a $50 meter, works to a couple of degrees and reads ambient correctly once removed from the object under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Immersion-style probes are supplied with some meters and are useful in their own right, but they are not interchangeable with a pinpoint thermocouple tip for spot measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup> Thermocouple probes also have a rated ceiling that can be exceeded when characterising a [[soldering-iron]] at the top of its range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup>

## Cold junction compensation

Any decent [[multimeter]] that measures temperature through an external thermocouple must contain an internal [[temperature-sensor]], because cold junction compensation is unavoidable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The reference point for the measurement is the condition in which the leads are shorted and 0 V appears across the input; the meter then displays its own internal reference sensor temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-48J0m3dvpgA" title="Multimeter Fridge Test">•</a></sup> That gives a useful diagnostic trick: with no probe to hand, shorting the temperature inputs with a link or the test probes makes the meter read its internal sensor, which may sit on the [[pcb]] or even inside the processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> More advanced meters expose the internal temperature directly, and plugging in a K-type probe switches the reading to the external junction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup> Thermocouple front ends built as accessories or data-logger boards implement the same compensation internally, alongside typical accuracy of about ±2%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1244" title="EEVblog #1244 - Mailbag">1244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-986" title="EEVblog #986 - Mailbag">986</a></sup>

Not every instrument accepts a thermocouple at all. The Agilent 34461A supports two- and four-wire RTD and two- and four-wire thermistor but no standard K-type input, and ships with no temperature probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> A Philips/Fluke PM2534 requires its own probe rather than a K-type in the jacks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1189" title="EEVblog #1189 - Ebay $140 6.5 digit DMM Bargain!?">1189</a></sup> The Agilent U1253A has a temperature function but no probe in the box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup> Where a probe is included — as with the Fluke 233 and the 28 Series II — the meter reads temperature on what is effectively the DC millivolt path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-43" title="EEVblog #43 - Fluke 233 Multimeter Review">43</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> Front-end silicon reflects the demand: an LT6010 precision op-amp, chosen for low input bias [[current]], appears in a Fluke CNX3000 specifically for thermocouple service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup> A Keithley 2302 senses its [[heat-sink]] with a crimp lug thermocouple wired back to a dedicated thermocouple amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup>

## Parasitic thermocouples inside instruments

The same physics that makes the probe work makes it a nuisance inside precision equipment. Any dissimilar metal junction within a unit — solder to copper, copper to board, every interconnect — is a thermocouple and generates an EMF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> Interconnected systems in practice contain a multitude of different metals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> The design consequence is that thermal gradients, not absolute temperature, are the enemy: a unit that warms up and stays evenly warm is preferable to one whose fan drags air across a [[voltage-reference]] and sets up gradients over the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> The effect is large enough to shape [[datasheet]] practice, with op-amp [[input-offset-voltage]] specified as guaranteed by design because thermocouple and leakage effects prevent measuring the parameter in production test; parts are instead screened against the maximum limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1325" title="EEVblog #1325 - OPAMP Shootout - Datasheet Deep Dive">1325</a></sup>

## Bench practice

A thermocouple is the default instrument for characterising thermal behaviour of a prototype. Attaching one to a DC-DC converter, or to its heat sink, allows temperature to be plotted against [[efficiency]] and load dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Measuring the [[thermal-resistance]] from a junction point to heat sink fins requires only a thermocouple on the fins, the known input power and the difference in temperature; the arithmetic from there is trivial.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> [[electrolytic-capacitor]] lifetime work follows the same route: ripple current and internal temperature rise can be calculated, but empirical measurements with a thermocouple and a thermal camera on prototypes decide whether the capacitors are the reliability weak point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> The technique extends to crude but useful checks such as measuring the rise on a PCB [[via]] carrying 2 A against a 26.4 °C ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup>

A thermal camera gives an overall thermal map without having to attach anything, but thermocouple attachment remains the most accurate method, and a camera pointed at clear anodised aluminium yields poor readings because of emissivity, making a thermocouple the better choice on such a heat sink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> Contact is often the difficulty: there is frequently no easy way to get a good connection onto the part of interest, and probing live circuitry with a thermocouple risks shorting adjacent nodes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup>

Multi-channel logging is the natural extension. Eight-channel and larger thermocouple loggers give 0.25 °C resolution over a couple of hundred degrees at 14 bits, and 36-channel units function as loop controllers over a 0 to 1600 °C range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-612" title="EEVblog #612 - Mailbag">612</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-651" title="EEVblog #651 - Mailbag">651</a></sup> Industrial analog front ends handle load cells, RTDs and thermocouples together with 16 to 24 bit inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> Products under development have been instrumented with a dozen thermal couples or independent temperature logger buttons scattered through the enclosure to build thermal profiles of different regions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup> A multimeter with two or three blade-type thermocouple inputs on the top of the case, rather than adapters occupying the measurement jacks, would allow simultaneous multi-point temperature logging alongside the voltage and current channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup>

Reflow profiling shows the practical detail. Learning an oven profile requires a dummy board of similar material and type to the intended work — standard 1.6 mm FR4, with the thermocouple wire threaded through so it cannot shake loose — placed in the chamber to take heat from.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> Reference thermocouples taped in with Kapton next to the oven's own sensor allow the built-in reading to be checked against a multimeter at temperature, and separate Fluke and Agilent probes agreeing to within 0.1 °C at ambient give confidence in the comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> Sitting the reflow controller box on top of the running oven heats its base and can corrupt the accuracy of its built-in thermocouple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> A thermocouple resting on a reflow board can also mislead an investigation, as one touching a pad near component leads explained an apparent effect that had nothing to do with the parts under study.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering! WTF!">1455</a></sup>

## Temperature sensing in products

Where a thermocouple is placed determines what it actually measures. In a coffee machine heating block the sensor is tied into the block itself rather than onto the element, so it senses the thing being heated rather than the heater.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1161" title="EEVblog #1161- Automated Coffee Machine Dumpster Teardown">1161</a></sup> Placing a thermal couple directly in the air flow path of a hot air rework station is likewise sound.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1171" title="EEVblog #1171 - Yihau WEP SMD Rework Station Meltdown!">1171</a></sup> The absence of a thermocouple on the heat sink of a 2000 W supply means the heat sink temperature is not being measured, which is what a top-quality [[power-supply]] would do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Better designs make the opposite choice: a Xantrex supply monitors [[bridge-rectifier]] temperature so it can shut down under overload, a Rohde & Schwarz NGP800 mounts a thermocouple among the devices thermally bonded to its heat sinks, and a GW Instek AC source carries one on the underside for fan control and probable over-temperature protection alongside a 105 °C mechanical thermostat cutout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1698" title="EEVblog 1698 - Awesome Ebay AC Power Source: GW Instek APS-7050">1698</a></sup>

Sensor count scales with [[calibration]] ambition. A Moku instrument couples two 52 mm fans to two dozen thermal sensors — one thermocouple per channel on the low and high power amplifiers, one per board, per main chip, and for different parts of the [[fpga]] — driven by an algorithm that sets fan speed to hold the instrument at its calibration temperature rather than as cold as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1624" title="EEVblog 1624 - Electronex: Liquid Instruments Moku Oscilloscope">1624</a></sup> A Tektronix 2 Series prototype was instrumented with so many thermal couples that the wiring resembled spaghetti, in order to find every hot spot in a design required to run at up to 50 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

Sensing alone is not protection. A rework station with a thermocouple control feedback loop and a failed fan should have detected the rising temperature, backed off the power to the element and shut down in software; the meltdown followed from a mechanical fan failure combined with [[firmware]] that did not act on what the sensor was telling it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1171" title="EEVblog #1171 - Yihau WEP SMD Rework Station Meltdown!">1171</a></sup> In safety-critical applications the loop is explicit: an ultrasound probe head contains a thermocouple so the software can detect that too much power is going into it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1315" title="EEVblog #1315 - Ultrasound Probe Extreme Teardown!">1315</a></sup> Thermocouples also appear on 3D printer hot ends, where the wire must be routed clear of the mounting plate or it will be pinched and destroyed during assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-318" title="EEVblog #318 - Makerbot Replicator 3D Printer Unboxing &amp; Review">318</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-257" title="EEVblog #257 - Makerbot Troubleshooting">257</a></sup> Other sightings include a [[battery]] analyser adapter, a rubidium standard's oscillator can, a solar hybrid [[inverter]] [[transformer]], and a tab on an IBM multi-chip module dissipating up to 600 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-236" title="EEVblog #236 - FE-5680A Rubidium Standard Teardown">236</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup>

## Thermoelectric power generation

Run in reverse of its sensing role, the same junction is a [[voltage-source]], listed alongside batteries, solar cells and Peltier devices as a source of electrical potential difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup> Radioisotope thermoelectric generators exploit this at scale. The New Horizons spacecraft carries plutonium whose radioactive decay makes its cylinder generate roughly 5 kW of heat; thermocouples driving a voltage difference between the hot and cold sides of the skin produce about 250 W of electrical power at launch — roughly four household light bulbs — which runs guidance, command and data handling, communications, thermal control, star trackers, gyros and every scientific instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tWW56LAnT1Y" title="Pluto: New Horizons Talk">•</a></sup> The Mars rover's multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator holds 10.6 pounds of plutonium dioxide sitting at about 600 °C with 420 thermocouples wrapped around it, generating about 100 W continuously; with a 600 °C oven adjacent to the vehicle, the thermal design problem is shedding heat rather than retaining it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-r7LbLwnYVZE" title="Making robots for Mars with NASA scientist Dave Lavery">•</a></sup> The same thermoelectric principle underlies bench energy-harvesting experiments with Seebeck modules, where poor mechanical coupling to the module directly limits the millivolts available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup>

Thermocouples also serve as the sensing element in RF power measurement, where a power meter zeroes a pair of them — one measuring ambient, one measuring the incoming RF — before calibration against a reference source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EVcxrYnY1ok" title="Guest Video: TGSoapbox - RF Crystal Detectors">•</a></sup>

## Soldering iron construction

Cartridge-style irons integrate the thermocouple, the tip and the heating element into a single assembly, which is why such a tip carries three terminals and why the station can display tip temperature in real time on the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> Modelled as a network of thermal resistances, a conventional iron has the element inside a ceramic former with the thermocouple up near the tip and everything only loosely coupled, with mechanical play in the fit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> Direct-heat designs remove those intermediate resistances, so the tip heats faster for equal power and the control loop detects heat being drawn out of the tip far sooner; the advantage attributed to the JBC lies less in raw wattage — 130 W versus 65 W — than in tip design, element coupling and the integration of the temperature sensor into the thermal response loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> Because the sensor is not at the working surface, comparing an iron's displayed temperature against an external thermocouple shows a differential in both directions and leaves open which figure is the true tip temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>

## Practical judgement

Thermocouples are tricky to use accurately, and getting a trustworthy number requires understanding the physics rather than trusting the display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup> The failure modes are mundane: cold junction compensation ignored, the sensor mounted on the heater instead of the load, the reference electronics sitting somewhere hot, the probe operated above its rating, or the tip-to-sensor coupling inside a soldering iron making it genuinely ambiguous which temperature is the true one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1161" title="EEVblog #1161- Automated Coffee Machine Dumpster Teardown">1161</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> Even a low-power iron can weld its own tip to a thermocouple during a thermal test rather than heat it, when the probe's mass exceeds what 8 W can drive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup>
