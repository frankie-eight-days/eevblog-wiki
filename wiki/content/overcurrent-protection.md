Overcurrent protection (OCP) is the set of mechanisms that detect [[current]] above a defined threshold and act on it — by limiting, shutting down, retrying, or physically opening the path — before the excess current destroys the source, the wiring, or the load. It appears at nearly every level of an electronic system: inside regulator and [[battery]]-management silicon, as discrete sense resistors and comparators on a board, as a settable trip point on a bench instrument, and as a passive device such as a [[ptc-thermistor|PTC thermistor]] in a mains input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> The stakes are direct: short the load of a [[voltage-source|voltage source]] and the source itself heats up and blows up unless something protects it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>

## Overcurrent protection versus current limiting

The two are commonly confused because both respond to the same event. A conventional bench supply that hits its current setting enters constant-current mode and stays there, continuously regulating current down while holding the output alive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> Overcurrent protection is the alternative behaviour: instead of dropping into [[current-limiting|current limiting]] when the [[current-limit|current limit]] is reached, it switches the output off entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> On supplies that expose OCP as a separate enable, turning it on converts the current setting from a limit into a trip point — the channel shuts down the moment it goes overcurrent, and shuts down again on any attempt to switch it back on into the same fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup>

The trip threshold is also distinct from the ordinary [[output-voltage|output voltage]] and current settings, and that separation is the point of it: the protection limits stand above the working setpoints so that someone else adjusting the panel cannot take the output past what the load can survive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> Trip resolution on a mid-range supply can be fine — 1 mA steps on the overcurrent setting of one 3-channel supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup>

## Why it is set on the bench

The usual justification is the first power-up of a prototype. Setting overvoltage and overcurrent protection just above the expected operating point means that a bumped knob, a mis-entered value, or another person at the bench trips the supply instead of destroying the board — the difference between a protection event and a dead assembly on a board that may be worth tens of thousands of dollars and carry the entire project schedule.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> In practice the supply simply switches the output off when the set point is exceeded, and the board survives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup>

Instruments other than supplies carry the same protection for the same reason. Electronic loads allow a maximum power, maximum current and maximum time to be configured so the device under test cannot be damaged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> One programmable load exposes overcurrent protection as an operating mode of its own, running a stepped sequence for testing supplies against power and current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

A cruder version of the same idea is a resettable trip in place of a [[fuse]]: a supply fitted with a red button that pops on excess current and is simply pushed back in once the fault is found, adopted specifically to stop the constant replacement of fuses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-874" title="Extended Cut: EEVblog #874 - Ted Wale">874</a></sup>

## Hardware versus software implementation

Whether the protection is real hardware or a [[firmware]] comparison matters, and on inexpensive equipment it is usually the latter. Overvoltage and overcurrent limits on low-cost bench supplies are typically software protections with no independent hardware crowbar behind them, so a firmware lockup or crash removes the protection entirely — the very failure mode the protection exists to cover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> Higher-end instruments do it in silicon: a precision battery emulator carries hardware overvoltage and overcurrent protection trip and DAC-set threshold circuitry alongside its current monitors, arranged so the protection is done in hardware rather than software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup>

Even where the protection is analog, a local [[processor]] often owns the loop. In one battery emulator the main applications processor is deliberately kept out of it; a dedicated local processor handles overcurrent and [[overvoltage-protection|overvoltage protection]] along with ESR emulation and the constant-power and constant-current modes, for speed, consistency, and separation from the rest of the design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> A high-[[voltage]] [[inverter]] controller adds microprocessor H-bridge overcurrent protection on top of the protection inherent in the analog design of the bridge, monitoring the current through the switching devices and reducing the commanded output magnitude on detecting an overload to bring the transient currents down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1170" title="EEVblog #1170 - TRUE Mystery Teardown! (not even Dave knows)">1170</a></sup>

## Hiccup mode

A well-behaved switching supply that detects an overcurrent does not simply latch off or blow a fuse — it shuts down, waits, and retries, repeatedly, producing the characteristic hiccup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> A reversed [[electrolytic-capacitor|electrolytic capacitor]] loading a rail beyond its protection current produced exactly that behaviour on a scope repair: the supply hiccupped rather than dying.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> The same signature appears in fault diagnosis, where a switching controller that is producing gate drive and seeing input voltage but restarting on a roughly one-second cadence is entering an overvoltage or overcurrent protection hiccup mode — a common behaviour for switching controllers, and one usually documented in the controller [[datasheet|data sheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup>

## Protection devices and integrated protection

A PTC thermistor is a self-acting overcurrent protection device. Passing more current through it heats it, and because its [[resistance]] rises with temperature, a short or fault drawing excess current downstream causes the PTC to heat and limit the current flow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup>

Overcurrent protection is also routinely built into the silicon. Linear regulators, LDOs and switching regulators alike carry internal overcurrent, overvoltage and overtemperature protection circuitry — additions that do not change the basic operation of the regulator but make the parts more useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Lithium cell protection boards implement overvoltage, overcurrent and low-voltage protection together; with all three present, a well-designed protection circuit should make the cell effectively impossible to kill, though it will certainly switch the cell off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1613" title="EEVblog 1613 - Audiophile REPAIR: $4500 Chord Hugo DAC">1613</a></sup> Battery-management and charge-control parts combine overvoltage and overcurrent detection with charge detection and on-die overtemperature protection using an integrated silicon [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup> Integrated power stages followed the same path: a monolithic half-bridge gained driver, level-shift and protection circuitry in a later generation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1737" title="EEVblog 1737 - Alex Lidow: Inventor of the Power MOSFET">1737</a></sup>

[[usb|USB]] power paths use dedicated hot-swap controllers for the job. One instrument's USB input uses a hot-swap controller with a settable current threshold, a series [[current-shunt|current shunt]] and a 12-bit ADC, giving both overcurrent protection and [[current-measurement|current measurement]] on the port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup> Properly designed USB power products negotiate the bus rather than feeding 5 V straight through a buck converter, and provide overcurrent protection in the path as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-827" title="EEVblog #827 - Mailbag">827</a></sup>

## Other applications

Device programmers use overcurrent detection as a diagnostic rather than only a safety feature: an overcurrent trip on the VPP or VCC rail is reported as an external [[short-circuit|short circuit]], a reversed IC, or a damaged device, and the programmer can self-test the function by deliberately shorting the rail through a [[transistor]] to confirm the current limit works.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup> A [[soldering]] station's tip current-sense [[resistor]] exists to raise an overcurrent error if the tip shorts out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> Automotive body-controller I/O nodes carry overcurrent protection, overvoltage protection and automotive transient protection on every input and output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1627" title="EEVblog 1627 - Electronex: Engineering Consulting with Xentronics">1627</a></sup> An insulation resistance tester driving 2,500 V shut itself down on what appeared to be an overcurrent event during arcing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup>

Overcurrent protection also bounds measurements. Sweeping a 9 V DC-DC brick rated at 100 mA output, the [[efficiency]] curve could be extended only to about 320 mA before the converter's own overcurrent protection kicked in and ended the sweep.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup>

## Interaction with electronic loads

Testing a supply with an [[electronic-load|electronic load]] can trip protection on either side, and the two can fight. An electronic load takes time to respond, and when it steps in it sets its output transistors to a very low [[impedance]] — effectively a short across the supply's [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]]. The stored charge dumps into the load and trips the supply's input overcurrent protection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> The load's own overcurrent and overvoltage protections are what make such loads hard to kill under that abuse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

The reverse coupling produces [[oscillation]]. If a load switch-on transient drives the supply briefly into constant-current mode, the load detects the mode change and switches its own mode in response, and the two chase each other until the operating point settles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> The consequence is not confined to the bench: the same current dump that trips the protection is available to be delivered into whatever real product the supply is powering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>
