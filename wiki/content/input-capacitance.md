Input capacitance is the shunt [[capacitance]] presented at the input terminal of a device — an [[oscilloscope-probe|oscilloscope probe]] tip, a scope's front end, a logic gate pin, a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] gate, or the DC bus of a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]]. It is a parasitic in most contexts and a design parameter in a few, and because [[capacitive-reactance|capacitive reactance]] falls as 1 / (2 · π · f · C), a few picofarads that are irrelevant at kilohertz become a dominant load at hundreds of megahertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup> Wherever a signal has to be measured at speed, driven fast, or supplied with surge [[current]], input capacitance is usually the limiting term.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1707" title="EEVblog 1707 - REPAIR: Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery">1707</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope probes and scope inputs

The capacitance a probe presents is specified either on the probe body or in its [[datasheet]], and it is often quoted as total system capacitance — the value seen at the tip once the probe is plugged into a scope, so probe and scope input combine into one number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> That figure is what the circuit under test actually sees, and it loads the circuit; the headline 10 MΩ [[input-impedance|input impedance]] is a DC figure only, and at [[frequency]] the relevant quantity is capacitive reactance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

Typical passive-probe values cluster in the low tens of picofarads. Rigol's RP2200 is a switchable ×1/×10 150 MHz probe with a nominal ×10 input capacitance of 17 pF; the 350 MHz RP3300 is marginally lower at 16 pF, and in ×10 mode the two are essentially the same.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup> A 500 MHz passive probe comes in at 11 pF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> The Tektronix TPP1000, a 1 GHz passive probe at roughly 800 dollars each, reaches 3.9 pF while keeping the same 10 MΩ input impedance and 300 V rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> One 1 GHz-rated passive probe carries 13 pF, which undercuts the point of the [[bandwidth]] rating once high-frequency loading is accounted for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>

Scope front ends vary similarly: 16 pF on a 1 MΩ-only GW Instek GDS-2000A input,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> a nominal 15 pF on a Siglent,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> and 24 pF on a Keysight Megazoom V 1 GHz instrument, notably higher than the 14 pF of the previous generation and enough to raise questions about [[probe-compensation|probe compensation]] range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup>

The attenuation setting dominates everything else. In ×1 mode a probe is close to a length of [[coaxial-cable|coax]] straight through to the scope and presents about 100 pF; switching to ×10 drops it to roughly 16 pF, equivalent to the tip capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> That difference is why ×10 is the default working mode and why many probes ship as ×10 only: bandwidth on one Rigol probe is DC to 8 MHz at ×1 against DC to 350 MHz at ×10, and the input capacitance is the reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup> The compensation trimmer at one end of the probe exists to match the probe network to the scope's input capacitance so the combination gives a flat total system response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

Capacitance also accumulates inside the instrument. A [[relay]]-switched attenuator chain contributes the capacitance of every tap and every switch in the divider, on top of the deliberate compensation caps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup>

## Active and low-capacitance probes

Above roughly 500 MHz, where practical passive probes stop, an active FET probe is the answer, and the reason is input capacitance — as the design tradeoff is usually framed, "it's all about the input capacitance."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> Even a 1 GHz passive probe at a few picofarads loads a fast node too heavily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> The Euler Precision eSAP-30, a 3 GHz active probe, specifies 1 MΩ input impedance with under 3 pF, and measurement confirms the effective capacitance is well under the claimed 3 pF; at 1 GHz its input impedance is around 159 Ω, consistent with slightly over 1 pF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup> Agilent's N2796A active probe reaches 1 pF, as does a 2 GHz Agilent active probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup> A 100-dollar 2 GHz [[power-rail|power rail]] probe does better still at roughly half a picofarad, with a 50 kΩ DC load that does not hold at frequency precisely because of that capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup> Layout inside such a probe matters for the same reason: putting the input amplifier as close to the tip as possible, rather than at the end of a long microstrip, is what keeps input capacitance down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup>

Very low probe capacitance also removes an error source in high-[[voltage]] work — a 1 pF probe input contributes nothing meaningful to the charging of a [[capacitor]] bank being ramped up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup>

Input capacitance is not always benign in a purely resistive sense. Together with the [[inductance]] of a probe's coax cable it forms an LC tank at the scope input, which broadband [[electrostatic-discharge|static discharge]] — from something as ordinary as standing up out of a chair — can ring.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14" title="EEVblog #14 - An unusual oscilloscope phenomenon!">14</a></sup> On the digital side, input capacitance sits alongside inductance, skew, crosstalk, and metastability among the signal-integrity problems that make logic-analyzer inputs hard to get right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 - Part 2 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup>

## Logic gate inputs

Digital logic datasheets specify input capacitance as C<sub>I</sub>, in picofarads, over the full operating voltage range; manufacturers frequently give only a maximum, with no typical figure and no characteristic curves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> A working rule of thumb puts the typical value around 3 pF per input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Because ordinary system design has one output driving several gates, the load capacitance C<sub>L</sub> at the driver is the sum of those inputs, and that total sets the achievable [[slew-rate|slew rate]] and the input transition rise and fall times downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> This was the historical basis of the maximum fan-out limit in 74-series designs, where one chip might drive twenty or thirty others and pin capacitance genuinely constrained the architecture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

C<sub>I</sub> is only the static part. Datasheets also give C<sub>PD</sub>, the dynamic [[power-dissipation|power dissipation]] capacitance per gate — quoted in picofarads despite describing power dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

The gate input capacitance is also loaded by the interconnect: a trace driving a CMOS or TTL input has its own capacitance to [[ground]] in parallel with the gate's, and during a fast transition that combination behaves briefly as a low [[impedance]], drawing a current spike through the signal trace and the [[ground-plane|ground plane]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> In DDR memory layout the problem becomes a timing one — input capacitance variation across devices, documented in the datasheet, adds to the propagation-delay budget alongside crosstalk between signals and the termination [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

## MOSFET gates

For MOSFETs, input capacitance is the datasheet parameter C<sub>ISS</sub>. It is loosely called gate capacitance but is not strictly the gate-to-source capacitance: it is measured with drain and source shorted, so it includes gate-to-drain, and it represents the total capacitance the gate driver actually sees.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> Its magnitude tracks die area, so the higher the current rating, the larger the input capacitance — a 2N7002-class small-signal part sits around 20 to 22 pF, while a power device runs to 630 pF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> C<sub>ISS</sub> is quoted at a specified V<sub>DS</sub>, for instance 25 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup>

Driving the gate is where large input capacitance causes trouble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup> When selecting a replacement, the input capacitance of the candidate is compared against the original: an ON Semi part at a nominal 2200 pF against a MagnaChip original at 2400 pF is a lower figure, and lower is the safe direction — it will not hurt, though reverse transfer capacitance still has to be checked separately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup> In [[parametric-search|parametric search]], input capacitance appears next to gate charge, [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]], and reverse transfer capacitance as one of the dynamic characteristics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> These matter mainly in switching converters; for logic-level or power switching in a repair context they are usually not the deciding parameter, whereas a low gate threshold and low input capacitance are what allow direct drive from 5 V or 3.3 V logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

Low input capacitance can also be the headline feature of an analog input device, as it was for the JFET-type parts used in a bootstrapped microphone preamplifier input stage, where it displaced the need for an expensive very-high-value bias [[resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup>

## Bulk input capacitance in power circuits

In power conversion the term refers to real bulk capacitance on the input rail, and here more is generally wanted. In a non-isolated [[led|LED]] driver, an input capacitance of only 330 nF let the rectified input rail sag almost to ground between half-cycles and produced visible flicker; adding 33 µF of input capacitance eliminated it outright and left the [[output-ripple|output ripple]] in good shape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup> A commercial flicker-free driver of otherwise identical topology carries 12 µF at 450 V — the voltage rating following from the full-wave bridge sitting directly across the filter cap — which is enough input capacitance to do the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dLlhoUHlnjE" title="LED Flicker 3 - More Electric Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Controller datasheets may assume far less; one specifies input capacitance of only 47 nF while giving typical output capacitance figures and a worked example showing that 470 µF of output capacitance still leaves 30 percent [[ripple]] current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup>

Excessive input capacitance is a fault mode in its own right. A mains [[inverter]] with too large an input capacitance drew enough inrush charging current at switch-on to upset the host controller in a lithium power station, which repeatedly attempted to start, shut down, and retry — a hiccup loop visible as flashing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1707" title="EEVblog 1707 - REPAIR: Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery">1707</a></sup> A load with very little input capacitance, and therefore very little surge current at turn-on, gave the same controller no trouble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1707" title="EEVblog 1707 - REPAIR: Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery">1707</a></sup>

Input capacitance also participates in stability. In a precision 1 A [[current-source|current source]] built around an LTC6655 reference, the combination of output capacitance, load current, and input capacitance is enough to make the loop oscillate under some conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>
