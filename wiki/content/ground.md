Ground is the reference point from which every other [[voltage]] in a circuit is measured. It is zero volts by definition rather than by physics: the node becomes ground because it has been designated as the reference, and every other potential in the circuit is then expressed relative to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> In practice the same word covers several distinct things — the analytical reference node, the negative rail or return path of a supply, a copper plane on a board, and the earthed metalwork of a chassis or [[connector]] shell — and confusion between them is a common source of error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GoKbPDADG0c" title="The Incredible Switch Replacement Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup>

## Ground as a reference, not a place

Where a voltage reading lands depends entirely on where the reference lead sits. Moving the black lead of a voltmeter along a stack of batteries changes every measured voltage in the circuit without changing the circuit at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup> Placing the reference in the middle of the stack gives voltages both above and below ground, so that "zero volts is not the absence of voltage" — a node ten volts above the lowest potential and ten volts below the highest is nonetheless zero by definition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup> Ground is therefore not necessarily the lowest potential in a system: in a circuit run from a negative rail, ground sits at a higher potential than the −15 V rail, which is what determines the correct polarity for an indicator [[led|LED]] referred to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup>

Choosing the reference badly makes analysis harder without making it wrong. Referencing an op-amp circuit to the bottom of a split supply leaves both inputs sitting at some large positive offset, which is why the reference is conventionally placed in the middle of the supply stack where positive and negative signal excursions fall naturally either side of zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup>

In nodal analysis the choice is explicit: one node is nominated as the reference, marked with the ground symbol, and removed from the set of unknowns, leaving the remaining node voltages to be solved relative to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> A branch that returns directly to the reference node simplifies immediately, since its voltage difference is just the unknown node voltage itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup>

## Virtual ground

An op-amp in the inverting configuration has its non-inverting input tied to ground, fixing that input at zero volts regardless of what the circuit does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The amplifier then drives its output to whatever value forces the inverting input to the same potential, so that node also sits at zero volts without being connected to ground at all — a [[virtual-ground|virtual ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HbMnQdRzD8A" title="Guest Video: Bob DuHamel - How Opamp Virtual Grounds Work">•</a></sup> This explains the result that surprises people probing an inverting stage for the first time: the signal is present on the input side of the input [[resistor]] and has apparently vanished on the op-amp side, because that node is being actively held at ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

A related construction is the rail splitter, which manufactures a mid-supply reference so that a single supply can be treated as a split one, with the created node serving as ground for the signal circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-476" title="EEVblog #476 - Opamp Offset Voltage Measurement">476</a></sup>

## Ground in schematics

Ground and VCC are conventionally drawn at fixed positions on a symbol — supply at the top, ground at the bottom — so that pins which are simply tied to one rail or the other can be terminated with a symbol placed right at the pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> Placing a ground pin on the wrong side of a symbol forces an extra ground symbol and a wire run across the drawing to reach it, for no benefit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DNlA4X5_S30" title="Schematic Component Library Drawing Tip">•</a></sup> Symbols should also be drawn the right way up and duplicated ground symbols on a single net removed; upside-down ground symbols and redundant duplicates are among the marks of a [[schematic]] that has not been laid out with care.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup>

Many library symbols hide their power and ground pins entirely, so a schematic can pass [[electrical-rules-checking|electrical rules checking]] with no visible ground or VCC connection anywhere on the sheet; the hidden pins can be revealed by enabling display of all pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> Ground is a power net, and connecting an output pin to it is a hard error that electrical rules checking exists to catch, alongside shorted outputs and floating inputs — the sort of mistake that releases the magic smoke if it reaches hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-953" title="EEVblog #953 - What Is Schematic ERC?">953</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5eJorvyA708" title="Altium Designer Serious ERC bug">•</a></sup>

## Ground planes and PCB layout

On a [[multilayer-pcb|multilayer board]], ground and power planes are treated as equivalent at signal frequencies: "Ground and VCC are effectively the same. The bypass capacitors ensure that."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> The consequence for stackup is that signal layers should be pressed as close as possible to their nearest plane, with thin prepregs between the outer signal layers and the planes and the bulk of the thickness put into the inner core.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup>

Ground routing sometimes drives mechanical decisions. Where a trace cannot be brought around the outside of a board, the shielded metal case of a component can be pressed into service as the ground connection between two points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GoKbPDADG0c" title="The Incredible Switch Replacement Repair">•</a></sup> The exposed [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] under a QFN package is normally ground, which means post-reflow inspection includes checking that no signal pin has been bridged to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> Similarly, the tab of a TO-220 regulator is connected to its centre pin, so on a 7805 the tab is at ground and can be screwed directly to a grounded case with no insulating washer; the majority of other cases require the tab to be isolated with a mica washer or sil pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup>

Protective structures are referred to ground and belong at the point of entry. A [[pcb|PCB]] spark gap is placed right at the input connector, running down to ground, so that the impulse energy is dissipated before it can travel down the trace toward the chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-678" title="EEVblog #678 - What is a PCB Spark Gap?">678</a></sup> Inside CMOS devices, every I/O pin carries reverse-biased protection diodes to both VCC and the ground pin, conducting only when the input is overdriven beyond the rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

## Current return paths

Because ground is the return path, it carries real [[current]] and obeys Ohm's law like any other conductor, and unaccounted return paths show up as measurement error. A parasitic path to ground alongside the intended one diverts current away from the load: a 2 mA error current returning through such a path represents a 0.2 % error, which is fatal to a design aiming at 0.02 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> Current-sense arrangements make the return explicit, with the low side of the sense resistors carried back to ground so the shunt develops its voltage across a defined path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

Datasheets impose limits on the ground pin itself, not just on individual outputs. A logic device rated at 25 mA per output does not permit four outputs to draw 100 mA simultaneously, because the continuous current through VCC or ground is limited to ±50 mA for the package as a whole.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Output stages are not zero-ohm either: the [[transistor]] and [[resistance]] between the output pin and ground produce a measurable drop — 0.2 V at 5.2 mA in one case — from which the output resistance can be calculated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

## Ground as a signal

Pulling a node to ground is itself a control action across many circuits. Digital logic is defined by a chip connected between ground and a supply rail of 5 V, 3.3 V, or lower, with every signal interpreted as one or zero relative to those two references.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7bVnsXHO6Uw" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 1 - Introduction To Digital Logic">•</a></sup> An external trigger input held high by a pull-up is asserted simply by [[grounding]] it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-947" title="EEVblog #947 - Chronos High Speed Camera Review">947</a></sup> A reset pin that is active low is at reset when it sits at ground and running when it is at the rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1144" title="EEVblog #1144 - Padauk Programmer Reverse Engineering">1144</a></sup> Where a [[capacitor]] ties a reset line to ground only during power-on, a mod wire shorting the line permanently to ground holds the part in reset thereafter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Cutting a jumper on a pin with a pull-down resistor leaves that pin pulled to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1246" title="EEVblog #1246 - Dumpster TV to Retro RGB Monitor Conversion">1246</a></sup> In an open-collector driver arrangement, pulling the base node to ground cuts the transistor off entirely, removing it from the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup>

Ground also serves as the destination for deliberate shunts. The low-[[impedance]] mode of a [[multimeter]] that operates permanently is implemented by shunting the input path straight down to ground through a PTC element, bypassing the Zener clamping diodes altogether; the measurement is then read from a different path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>

## Finding ground on an unknown board

Identifying ground is usually the first step in [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]] a board, and it is normally done by measurement rather than assumption. Pins that all read shorted together are likely ground, but the assumption is verified with a meter before anything is connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> A pin that reads as connected to ground on both the board and the device under test confirms that the two share the same ground, establishing the sanity of the rest of the investigation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> Package pinouts provide the starting hypothesis — VSS or ground and the positive rail located from a [[datasheet]], then cross-checked against the physical part — but package variants shift the numbering, so a QFN version of the same die can have its ground on a different pin from the DIP.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1111" title="EEVblog #1111 - World&#x27;s First Microcontroller &amp; Electronic Game">1111</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-V0RWwSw96Sw" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL - Part 2">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (4 of 5): Open Source SDCC C Compiler">1306</a></sup>

Assumptions about ground fail often enough to be worth checking every time. On one analog instrument chip, the pin that a conventional pinout would place at ground turned out to be the +15 V input for the internal reference, because the part does not follow the usual conventions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup> On a multimeter chipset, a pin identified as ground on the basis of a misread [[silkscreen]] label proved not to be ground at all, but the common terminal of the chipset, which would have sent a parts search in the wrong direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kU7zSSuy9WQ" title="How to ID a Mystery Microcontroller">•</a></sup> A pin that measures as floating — not connected to ground and not connected to any semiconductor like its neighbours — is itself diagnostic, in one instance revealing an unconnected [[lcd|LCD]] contrast pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup>

Connector metalwork is a convenient and reliable ground access point. The shell of an HDMI connector is at ground, as are the outer pins of the socket, leaving the centre pin as the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1279" title="EEVblog #1279 - Best Dumpster PC Find Yet!">1279</a></sup> The shield of a [[usb|USB]] connector is connected through to the ground point of the board, which makes it usable as a [[soldering]] surface for a [[decoupling-capacitor|decoupling capacitor]]'s ground end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> On coaxial connectors, everything outside the centre conductor is ground, and in RF test infrastructure that ground is bonded to [[mains-earth|mains earth]] for safety.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup> On DC jacks, the centre pin is the positive input and the remaining contacts return to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-278" title="EEVblog #278 - Elmo Visual Presenter Teardown">278</a></sup> Where a connector is unlabelled, the ground lead is identified by measurement and then marked before use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-992" title="EEVblog #992 (Part 2) - How To Clean &amp; Service A Microscope">992</a></sup>

## Grounding for measurement

Probe ground return matters as much as the probe tip. Ideally a ground pin and a supply pin sit adjacent on the header so the probe can be connected directly across them with minimal loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup> Where no convenient ground point exists, a header pin soldered to a known ground pin creates one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup> Probing a 25 MHz signal calls for a low-[[inductance]] probe with a short ground connection to keep the displayed [[waveform]] representative of the signal rather than of the probe arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1288" title="EEVblog #1288 - Synology NAS Dumpster Find! (REPAIR)">1288</a></sup>

Ground connections inside instruments can themselves be shaped for the measurement. In one scopemeter input, the ground of the coaxial input is wrapped in a capacitor that AC-couples the negative input of the BNC through to the internal [[shielding]] and signal common.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup>
