Propagation delay is the finite time a signal takes to travel from one point in a system to another: through the internal logic of a chip from input to output, or along a length of [[pcb-trace|PCB trace]] or cable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> It is the parameter that ultimately sets the maximum clock [[frequency]] of a digital design, because every stage of gates a signal passes through adds its own delay and slows the whole machine down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lJ3q9RHIatU" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 5 - Karnaugh Maps">•</a></sup> It also governs whether two signals that are supposed to arrive together actually do, which is why it dominates both high-speed [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]] and multi-channel [[oscilloscope]] measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1530" title="EEVblog 1530 - Siglent SDS2000X HD 12 Bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1530</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup>

## The datasheet parameter

In logic datasheets the figure appears as TPD — T for time, P for propagation, D for delay — expressed in nanoseconds, and the [[datasheet]] states exactly which pins it applies to, such as from the A or B input to the Y output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Treating the input edge as infinitely fast, TPD is how long the output takes to change in response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> More detailed parts break the figure down by transition direction: TPHL is the propagation delay for a high-to-low output transition, and such combinatorial parameters fold the output transition time in with the propagation time itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

The summary table at the front of a datasheet gives a typical operating [[voltage]] range and a typical TPD alongside the output drive capability, but it does not tell the full story; the real specifications further in, with their asterisks and footnotes, are where the usable numbers live.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> A 74HC part might list 12 nanoseconds typical, and that figure varies with supply voltage, with temperature, and with other conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> For worst-case system design the maximum column is what matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Designing to the typical figure produces a system that works at 25 °C on the bench and then develops weird, hard-to-diagnose errors once it is boxed up and running at 40 °C, or at 0 °C in winter — a failure whose root cause is unaccounted-for propagation delay.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Alongside TPD, logic datasheets carry the related timing specifications a design has to respect, including setup time and maximum input rise and fall time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

Delay figures differ by logic family through process technology, though parts of the same family are broadly consistent between manufacturers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> The practical consequence is that a family swap is a legitimate speed fix: 74F fast TTL has a shorter propagation delay than 74HC, and boards otherwise built from 74LS jelly-bean logic sometimes carry one or two 74F parts dropped in exactly where a fast-propagation-delay problem needed solving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> Historically, a propagation delay of 12 nanoseconds was respectable for early [[resistor]]-[[transistor]] logic operating at a nominal 3.6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-867" title="EEVblog #867 - The Search For The First TTL Chip">867</a></sup>

## Delay through a system of chips

In a discrete-logic machine — a TTL computer where the [[processor]] is built up from individual packages — every chip and every register contributes its own propagation delay, and those delays accumulate along whatever path the data takes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> With a 25 nanosecond window to move data across, plus setup time at the destination, the budget disappears quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> In a [[ripple]] counter the effect is structural rather than incidental: after a clock edge, the delay through the internal gates must elapse before the Q output changes and before the stage can be clocked again, and that Q output then ripples on into the next stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> Propagation delay in that context is a system-implementation concern, distinct from parameters like clock pulse width or input [[slew-rate|slew rate]] that constrain the part in isolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

It is possible in principle to simulate a discrete design, work out the worst-case propagation delay, and calculate the clock frequency at which the architecture breaks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> For a machine of a few dozen chips this is not worth doing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> The practical method is to build it, run it, and wind the clock up until it stops working — a design that runs at 10 MHz but not 11 has answered the question empirically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

Inside programmable logic the same principle shapes how clocks are brought on chip. Routing a clock through general-purpose I/O and the logic fabric adds extra delay and consumes logic resources that the design could otherwise use; feeding it into a dedicated global clock pin distributes it efficiently across the device, giving lower propagation delays and higher achievable speeds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

There is a corresponding argument for logic minimisation that has nothing to do with parts count. Every extra stage of gates adds propagation delay and lowers the maximum frequency, which is why simplification techniques remain worth knowing even when the target is an [[fpga|FPGA]], an [[asic|ASIC]], or a processor rather than a board full of discrete gates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lJ3q9RHIatU" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 5 - Karnaugh Maps">•</a></sup>

## Delay on the PCB

Copper imposes its own delay independent of the silicon. A working rule of thumb for signal propagation is about 15 centimetres per nanosecond, which is why trace length matters once speeds get high.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> A [[pcb|PCB]] calculator gives the figure precisely: for a typical trace on the top layer of a board with a dielectric constant of 4.5, propagation delay works out to roughly 57.6 picoseconds per centimetre.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> The number is remarkably insensitive to the dielectric — dropping Er to 4 moves it only from about 57 to 54 picoseconds per centimetre, and even an extreme value of 3 does not shift it enormously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> The engineering judgement that follows is that a design whose function hinges on that level of precision in the dielectric constant is either operating at the bleeding edge or is simply being done wrong; ordinary designs should carry enough margin that a rule of thumb suffices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

Critically, propagation delay along a trace does not change with frequency. Sweeping a calculator from 500 MHz down to 100 MHz leaves the delay untouched, because the delay belongs to the signal's transit, not to its rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> What does change it is how the signal is routed — which layer it runs on, and what reference plane sits beneath it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> Even the glass weave of the dielectric matters: a trace running along the direction of one set of weave fibres sees a different effective dielectric constant than one crossing them, which shifts the propagation delay and the [[signal-integrity|signal integrity]] with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

The standard remedy where arrival time must be equal is length matching. Parallel data buses running to a sampling ASIC or FPGA are routed with serpentine sections so that all the output data reaches the destination and is latched at exactly the same instant; if the lengths differed, so would the propagation delays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> The same technique appears on scope front ends, where channel 1 is snaked around rather than taking the direct path so that its propagation delay matches channel 2 exactly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1530" title="EEVblog 1530 - Siglent SDS2000X HD 12 Bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1530</a></sup>

Timing skew can also arise where a design changes signals on two different [[microcontroller]] ports in sequence, since the port writes do not happen simultaneously. Where the update rate is low — around 100 Hz for an [[lcd|LCD]] backplane — a fast microcontroller makes that delay negligible in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1045" title="EEVblog #1045 - How To Drive an LCD">1045</a></sup>

## Delay in measurement

Every probe and cable adds propagation delay, and two probes on the same point will show a timing difference if their delays differ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup> Correcting for this is called probe skew correction, and it is a prerequisite for time-correlating channels in any serious multi-channel measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup> Scopes provide the adjustment directly: a per-probe propagation delay is entered — 5.3 nanoseconds in one instance — and many instruments perform the de-skew automatically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The compensation can be applied for a probe of different length, or used to cancel skew originating on the board under test instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

The magnitude is easy to underestimate. Two ordinary cables can differ by around 10 nanoseconds, visible as roughly half a division at 20 nanoseconds per division, all of which calibrates out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup> The figures also predict well: taking a datasheet propagation delay of 5.05 nanoseconds per metre, a 45 mm length difference between two coaxial cables works out to about 227 picoseconds, against a measured correction of around 262 picoseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>

Delay is not always a parasitic. Glass delay lines exploit acoustic transit time as their function, with a nominal 64 microsecond delay measured on a scope at 63.83 microseconds using cursors against the trigger point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup> Time of flight is likewise the measurement itself in pulsed LIDAR: a laser-encoded signal is fired at the [[ground]] and the propagation delay of the return gives the range, with encoding allowing several units to operate in the same arena without interfering — usable from about 20 centimetres out to 40 metres before other sensors take over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-766" title="EEVblog #766 - Vivid Sydney 2015 Light Festival">766</a></sup>
