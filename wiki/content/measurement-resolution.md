Measurement resolution is the smallest change in a quantity that an instrument can display or record — the size of its least significant step, expressed as counts, digits, or an absolute increment such as 10 µV or 100 nA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> It is a separate property from accuracy: an instrument can resolve 100 nanoamps and still be badly wrong about the value it shows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> Resolution determines what changes are visible; accuracy determines whether the number itself can be trusted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup>

The distinction matters most when the two are mismatched. A 6,000 count meter with 0.5% basic accuracy has more resolution than a smaller-count meter, which lets a user see changes, but the displayed figure is not correspondingly accurate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> The same mismatch appears at the high end: an instrument may display all the digits it likes while the underlying measurement is nowhere near accurate enough to justify them, in which case the trailing digits exist for show.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup> Conversely, a 5½-digit reading obtained from an instrument whose specified accuracy is an order of magnitude worse than the last digit simply means that digit is insignificant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup>

## Counts and digits

Handheld meters quote resolution as a count — the maximum number the display can reach before ranging up. A 2,000 count meter is 3½ digits, an old-fashioned figure by modern standards; 4,000 or 6,000 counts is a reasonable baseline for a general-purpose instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Above that sit 10,000, 25,000, and 50,000 count designs, where the extra resolution rather than any improvement in accuracy is the selling point — a 25,000 count meter specified at 0.05% plus three counts is not a precision lab instrument, it simply resolves finer than a 10,000 count meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yzO1PsscjOI" title="WENS 540 Handheld 10MHz Oscilloscope and Debug Meter">•</a></sup>

Doubling the counts buys roughly an extra digit over a useful part of the range: a 40,000 count [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] measuring a 39 nF [[capacitor]] gains a digit over a 20,000 count instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup> The digit count quoted for bench meters does not by itself imply a count value, and the relationship between digits and accuracy is not linear — an 8½-digit meter specified around 0.0005% on DC volts is about ten times better than a top-of-the-line 6½-digit meter, so two extra digits bought only one order of accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup>

## Range selection

Resolution is a property of the selected range, not of the meter alone. Stepping a 6,000 count meter manually from the 6 V range to 60 V, then 600 V, loses one digit of resolution at each step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> The same applies to extra low ranges: a 60 mV range gives 10 µV resolution on a 10,000 count meter, and a meter that lacks such a range simply cannot offer that resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

Missing ranges are a substantive design failure rather than a minor omission. A meter that provides microamps and then jumps straight to amps, skipping the 100 mA and 10 mA ranges, yields essentially no usable resolution for a 9 mA measurement — a [[current]] typical of everyday electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup> Auto-ranging can also fail to select the finest available range, leaving the correct value displayed at coarser resolution than the instrument is capable of until the range is set manually; this was a [[firmware]] defect corrected in later releases of the [[121gw|121GW]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-q4gXnpFPFzQ" title="121GW Multimeter 2.00 Firmware">•</a></sup>

## The current-range trade-off

[[current-measurement|Current measurement]] forces an explicit trade between resolution and circuit disturbance. The amps jack uses a much lower shunt [[resistance]] than the milliamp or microamp jacks, so it perturbs the circuit less but returns fewer digits — three decimal places on the milliamp range collapse to two on amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> The shunt on the amps range is lower, not zero, and [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]] remains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> Where fine resolution is required at low current without the burden penalty, a dedicated low-burden front end such as the µCurrent is the alternative to accepting the coarser range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> Compensating for burden voltage by raising the source [[voltage]] restores the operating point but does nothing for the lost resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

Clamp meters show the same pattern: a 2 A range provides an extra digit of resolution over the 20 A range, though on a poorly performing instrument that extra digit is swamped by a large constant offset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dv0B_WqL7w4" title="UniT 210E Current Clamp Teardown + Brymen Comparison">•</a></sup>

## Integration time and resolution

In integrating ADCs, resolution is bought with time. A dual-slope or multi-slope converter charges an integrator to a known reference and measures ramp durations against a fixed clock; the longer the ramp is allowed to run, the more precisely the time — and therefore the input voltage — can be resolved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup> This is why bench multimeters take longer to produce a 6½-digit result than a 3½- or 4½-digit one: the ramp time is changed to suit the requested resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup>

The user-facing control is the aperture, usually expressed in number of power line cycles. One NPLC is one 50 Hz or 60 Hz mains period — 20 ms at 50 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> At very low NPLC settings, few significant digits are available and the reading is visibly noisy; at 0.02, 0.06, and 0.2 power line cycles the [[noise]] remains substantial.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Raising the aperture to 10 power line cycles yields an extra digit of resolution and a stable zero, and simultaneously rejects mains hum, since integrating over a whole number of line cycles cancels it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> Instruments default to 10 NPLC for this reason; faster display updates are available but cost resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> The improvement is a property of the measurement integration, not of post-measurement mathematical averaging, which does not do the same thing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Older bench instruments expose the same mechanism through a settable integration time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup>

## Sampling systems

In any sampling system the achievable time resolution follows from the [[sample-rate|sample rate]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup> Software that prints more decimal places than the sample rate can support is misleading: a capture at 8 MHz displayed to 1 ns resolution cannot actually resolve 1 ns, and a pulse width shown as 14.00 microseconds while the underlying reading jumps between 2 and 14 microseconds is a display artefact, not a measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup> The practical test is whether the resolution is good enough to determine the quantity of interest — 8 MHz is more than adequate for decoding infrared remote timing, while resampling the same signal at 500 kHz quantises the same pulse to 2 µs steps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup>

The same constraint governs [[frequency]]-domain work: an [[fft|FFT]] with 400 lines of resolution produces coarse steps across a 100 kHz span and cannot characterise a [[noise-floor|noise floor]] accurately over that full [[bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1328" title="EEVblog #1328 - uCurrent OPA189 Measurements">1328</a></sup> Logging systems inherit it too — reducing a solar monitoring interval from 5 minutes to 5 seconds is purely a gain in data resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k2_mJtAeaog" title="SNEAKY Enphase Solar Monitoring Modification">•</a></sup>

[[oscilloscope|Oscilloscope]] measurements are limited by the effective number of bits available at the chosen vertical setting, which some scopes will display directly, and a low bit count is what makes the standard deviation of a measurement large.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup> Extra resolution can nonetheless be extracted from the measurement statistics on practically any oscilloscope that computes them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup> Where a scope's resolution is inadequate, derived quantities suffer accordingly: computing [[phase-angle|phase angle]] from cursor amplitudes [[via]] an arcsine returns 30.12° against an expected 33° when the amplitude resolution is poor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup>

## Extracting resolution over remote interfaces

Instruments frequently compute internally to greater precision than they display, and the remote interface can expose it. A bench [[multimeter]]'s logged data can carry better resolution than its own screen because the value is taken from the ADC's floating-point result rather than the formatted display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> The same instrument's single-precision internal math may hold more resolution than the panel shows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> An LCR meter read over its remote interface returns 0.1 fF steps on [[capacitance]] and an extra digit over the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup> Adding network or GPIB access to older instruments recovers digits that the front panel never showed — an electrometer limited to four decimal places on its display yields more over the bus, and some HP multimeters effectively turn from 7½-digit into 8½-digit instruments when read over GPIB, though not all instruments behave this way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1232" title="EEVblog #1232 - Add Web Access To Old Instruments!">1232</a></sup>

## Resolution as the goal in itself

There are measurements where accuracy is irrelevant and resolution is the entire requirement. Tracking down a [[short-circuit|short circuit]] on a board is one: whether the meter is 5% accurate or 0.005% accurate does not matter, because only the ability to see small relative differences in trace resistance is being used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Relative-change measurements generally fall into this class, and more digits help even on an inaccurate instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Matching resistors is another — the task calls for both resolution and stability, which justifies moving from a handheld to a warmed-up bench meter in five-digit mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup>

Choosing test conditions to maximise resolution is part of the technique. Large-value capacitors are measured on an LCR meter's lowest test frequency, 100 Hz or 120 Hz, which gives the greatest resolution on the capacitance value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Setting a bench meter's aperture high, or an instrument's range low, follows the same principle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

## Resolution in analog instruments

An analog meter's resolution is bounded by needle width against scale. Estimating something like 250 to 500 distinguishable needle positions across a full sweep gives a best-case resolution of roughly 0.2%, or 0.4% on a coarser scale.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Expressed in digital terms, that is equivalent to a digital meter losing its two least significant digits and having the remaining digit step in increments of four counts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup> Even a 3½-digit, 2,000 count digital meter therefore resolves far better than any analog meter, and the ambiguity of reading a needle — whether a value is 5 or 5.05 — has no digital equivalent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

## Resolution in sources and loads

Programmable supplies and electronic loads carry the same specification on their outputs and readbacks. Enabling a licensed option on a lab supply can unlock 1 mV and 1 mA setting resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup> Instrument families are commonly differentiated on this axis alone, with a three-digit voltage and four-digit current display on one model against six digits for both on another, each suited to different tasks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Higher-end supplies reach 100 µV and 100 µA resolution, sometimes varying by mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y2rcx4vKxlc" title="Unboxing the Keysight E36731A DC Power Supply, Electronic Load, and Battery Emulator">•</a></sup> Electronic loads may show a 0.1 mA display digit whose underlying resolution is uncertain, since a firmware implementation counting in steps of ten would present the same digit with only 1 mA of real resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

Instruments operated near the bottom of a range work down in the noise and the resolution floor of their own specification, which is not necessarily a fault but does bound what can be concluded — a supply reading close enough to 10 mA at the extreme low end of its current range is behaving as specified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> Where resolution is coarse relative to the quantity, as with a [[usb|USB]] power meter reporting charge current in 10 mA increments, the reading serves only as a rough ballpark.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-513" title="EEVblog #513 - Mailbag">513</a></sup> Similarly, a low-power [[battery]] discharge test conducted at 10 mW sits right at the resolution limit of the setup; better instruments and better techniques exist, but the measurement is adequate for the purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

## Where resolution is the headline feature

Resolution is often what distinguishes instruments that are otherwise comparable. A meter with an extra digit reaches an intermediate value on a slowly changing input that a lower-resolution meter skips over entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BuFoA-qt1PY" title="Brymen BM2257 Intelligent Auto Power Off (iAPO) tested">•</a></sup> An LCR tweezer that lacks the range, resolution, and accuracy of a bench instrument is nonetheless useful as a convenience tool rather than a precision device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> Handheld LCR meters vary widely, and 100 µΩ resolution on low resistance marks out the better ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> Component-level figures such as 1 pF resolution on capacitance are quoted the same way as voltage and current figures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yzO1PsscjOI" title="WENS 540 Handheld 10MHz Oscilloscope and Debug Meter">•</a></sup> Bench instruments allow the displayed digit count and update rate to be selected directly, trading one against the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup> The count of an older bench meter, in the region of 200,000, remains its defining resolution specification decades after manufacture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1012" title="EEVblog #1012 - Best Bargain Ebay Bench Meter? - Fluke 8842A">1012</a></sup>
