A voltage reference is a circuit element that produces a fixed [[voltage]] whose defining requirement is stability over time and temperature rather than absolute accuracy, and it serves as the yardstick against which analog-to-digital converters, digital-to-analog converters, multimeters, power supplies, and [[comparator]] thresholds are defined.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> Because a [[power-supply]]'s set value and its output noise both originate from its reference, "a power supply is only as good as its voltage reference".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

## Accuracy versus stability

Initial absolute accuracy of a reference is largely irrelevant because it can be calibrated out in production or in software; what cannot be calibrated out is drift with temperature and time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-709" title="EEVblog #709 - EDC 4601 AC Voltage Standard Teardown">709</a></sup> A 0.25% reference is adequate inside a 0.05% power supply, and even a ±5% absolute tolerance is acceptable in a precision standard, provided the device is stable — aged Zener references in voltage standards are hand-selected and measured at a particular bias [[current]], then left untouched thereafter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-709" title="EEVblog #709 - EDC 4601 AC Voltage Standard Teardown">709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> Consequently, parametric part selection should sort by [[temperature-coefficient]] rather than by initial accuracy: the reference chips used in top-end multimeters have unremarkable initial tolerance but the lowest drift in the industry, and would be filtered out entirely by a search sorted on percentage accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Initial-accuracy and tempco grades of the same part are sold at different price points, such as the 5 ppm/°C A-grade of the MAX6198 versus lower grades, and the 100 ppm versus 50 ppm versions of the ICL8069.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup>

## Topologies and standard voltages

Bandgap physics sets a floor near 1.2 V, so a reference below that — a 0.3 V reference, for example — must be produced by dividing a higher reference down with precision resistors and buffering the result with an op-amp follower, or by using ordinary resistors plus a trim pot while watching the tempco.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

Common standard values cluster around a few figures:

- **1.2–1.25 V**: the internal references of the [[lm317]] adjustable regulator and the LM3914 bar-graph driver. The LM317's internal 1.25 V sets the lowest output the regulator can produce in a single-supply circuit and appears in the design equation R2 = (Vout/1.25 − 1) × R1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The LM3914's 1.25 V reference is not internally ground-referenced — it exits through a "ref adjust" pin — and its adjust-pin current of 75 µA typical (120 µA maximum) enters calculations as an error term.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> Micropower shunt parts such as the LM285/LM385 occupy the 1.2 V point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1424" title="EEVblog 1424 - Fluke 23 Multimeter Repair">1424</a></sup>
- **2.5 V**: dominated by the TL431 adjustable shunt reference, "one of the cheapest jelly bean voltage references on the market" and the standard feedback element on the secondary side of mains switch-mode supplies; the LM336 2.5 V part is the other common choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1435" title="EEVblog 1435 - The World&#x27;s Most EXPENSIVE Catalog IC ?">1435</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>
- **2.048 V and 4.096 V**: power-of-two values chosen so that a 12-bit converter yields a round 500 µV per LSB, avoiding software scaling; the ISL2107 2.048 V part at 30 ppm and ±0.25% is a typical example.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>
- **5 V and 10 V**: the AD586 precision 5 V reference, the REF102 and LT1031 10 V parts, and the AD584 programmable reference appear throughout precision instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1366" title="EEVblog #1366 - Mystery Bunker Item Teardown">1366</a></sup>

References divide into series (voltage-driven) and shunt (current-driven) types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Series parts specify a minimum supply voltage above the output — roughly 2 V of headroom, so a 2.5 V part needs at least about 4.5 V in — and specify line regulation on the order of 1–1.5 ppm/V rather than using the term "[[dropout-voltage]]".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Shunt parts such as the 4040/4041 family are used where better precision and tempco than a [[voltage-regulator]] are needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

At the top end, buried-Zener references with on-chip heaters dominate: the LM399 is the classic reference of 6.5-digit multimeters, the LTFLU variant (without built-in heater) appears in the Fluke 8842A and the Fluke 732 voltage reference standard, and the LTZ1000 class sits in the highest-resolution instruments, with its oven-driver circuitry deliberately placed outside the reference's thermal enclosure so driver heat does not disturb the die.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1012" title="EEVblog #1012 - Best Bargain Ebay Bench Meter? - Fluke 8842A">1012</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

## Environmental and layout sensitivities

High-precision references are mechanically sensitive. Board flexure and thermal expansion couple stress through the package leads into the die and cause drift, so precision references are commonly surrounded by a routed [[isolation-slot]], or even suspended by a spiral cutout that leaves the reference board section sprung.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1037" title="EEVblog #1037 - Solving Ceramic Capacitor Cracking">1037</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> [[reflow-soldering]] itself shifts reference output, and some data sheets publish before-and-after-reflow mean-shift graphs characterised at the recommended profile.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup> Temperature control extends to the instrument level: heated references require warm-up time, and enclosures or plastic cans are used to keep fan [[airflow]] from changing the reference's ambient temperature abruptly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

Electrically, references have loop-stability constraints. A precision reference driving a series-pass [[transistor]] in a 1 A current-source configuration oscillated badly until output-[[capacitor]] ESR (specified as below 0.1 Ω for a 10 µF load) and input-bypass placement were brought within the data-sheet envelope; the stability is set by the phase margin of the reference's internal output op-amp, not by input [[capacitance]] tweaks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> A damaged downstream IC can also load a reference output and pull it down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-727" title="EEVblog #727 - How To Kill An Opamp">727</a></sup> In switching regulators, the controller's internal reference is corrupted by dynamic layout errors: a few millivolts of load-switching ground bounce can pull a 0.9 V output down toward 0.8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Substituting adjustable regulators is hazardous because the internal reference voltage and feedback formula differ between brands even on pin-compatible parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup>

## Applications

- **Data conversion**: the reference sets ADC and DAC full-scale; it also determines whether results come out as round numbers, hence the power-of-two family.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>
- **Supply rails**: a precision reference in 2.5, 3.3, or 5 V at 0.1–0.2% accuracy (about a dollar, some with 40–50 mA output capability) can power a small [[microcontroller]] directly, giving it an accurate ADC reference for free; some tight-tolerance LDOs such as the MCP1700 can serve the same trick at fixed ambient temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>
- **Threshold detection**: [[battery]] cut-off and low-battery indicators are a comparator plus a reference, available as integrated supervisors such as the 2.65 V TPS3809, and it is this internal reference — not battery internal [[resistance]] — that determines where a product shuts down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>
- **Current sources**: the canonical constant-current formula is the reference voltage divided by the [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]], with an op-amp closing the loop; the REF102 with an OPA227 implements sub-10 mA precision sources this way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup>
- **DDR termination**: dedicated mid-rail Vref termination chips exist because reference noise directly consumes the memory interface's timing budget.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>
- **Combination parts**: the TSM102 packs a dual op-amp, dual comparator, and adjustable reference into one package for power-supply and battery-charger supervision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>
- **[[calibration]] and checking**: inexpensive 10 V reference modules of about 0.05% class suffice to spot-check handheld DMMs, while aged-Zener standards, the Fluke 732, and 8.5-digit multimeters serve as transfer standards for serious work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1012" title="EEVblog #1012 - Best Bargain Ebay Bench Meter? - Fluke 8842A">1012</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1330" title="EEVblog #1330 - Mailbag with GUEST LABS!">1330</a></sup>
