A current sense resistor is a low-value [[resistor]] placed in series with a [[current]] path so that the [[voltage]] developed across it can be measured and converted back into a current reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Lk2KRc6Bm4I" title="Mystery Dumpster Diving Teardown - 2020-07-11">•</a></sup> It is the element that closes the loop in almost every constant-current circuit — switchmode converters, [[led|LED]] drivers, [[battery]] chargers, electronic loads, bench power supplies and [[multimeter]] current ranges all depend on one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup> Because the [[resistance]] is deliberately small, everything about its use is a fight against the small signal it produces: the tapping arrangement, the tolerance, the [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] and the voltage it steals from the rail.

## Sizing and value

Values in real hardware span four orders of magnitude, set by how much current flows and how much [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] the design can afford. A 5 mΩ four-terminal part thermally mounted onto a [[heat-sink|heat sink]] handles the output of an 800 W supply;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> a defibrillator's [[transformer]] primary is sensed with 68 mΩ;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup> a scope meter uses 0.1 Ω 5% in series with its [[battery-pack|battery pack]];<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> an LED headlamp's high range is set by a 0.33 Ω 0805;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup> a charger's dummy-load leg uses 0.5 Ω;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup> a benchtop lab supply design uses 1 Ω purely because it makes the arithmetic trivial;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> and a 10 kW laser supply gives each paralleled pass [[transistor]] its own 2 Ω power resistor as the sense element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup>

Where a controller specifies the resistor, the value follows directly from a [[datasheet]] formula rather than from judgement. One switchmode controller sets its sense resistor as 0.3 divided by the calculated peak current, which for a worked example yields 0.4 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> That figure is then rounded to a purchasable preferred value — 0.39 Ω from the E24 series — since 0.4 Ω is not a stock part and the value is not hugely critical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

On a board, the sense resistor is often identifiable by physical size alone: it is conspicuously larger than the surrounding resistors because it has to dissipate real power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup> The other identification route is to follow the current path and look for two thin traces leaving a fat resistor and heading for an amplifier — the tell-tale of a differential amp measuring across a shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-720" title="EEVblog #720 - Mailbag">720</a></sup>

## Four-terminal connection

The measurement is only as good as the connection to it. A two-terminal resistor's own [[solder]] joints and pad resistance sit inside the measured drop, so the correct construction is a four-terminal part: two terminals carry the load current and two carry only the sense voltage, tapped directly off the resistor's own terminals or pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> One energy multimeter uses a proper surface-mount four-terminal current sense resistor rather than "the bent piece of metal basically that almost every other meter uses", with the voltage sense terminals tapping straight off the pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup> The equivalent practice on a general [[pcb|PCB]] is a Kelvin connection to an ordinary shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup> Where a design offers no four-terminal part and no visible sense tap, that is a recognised shortcoming of the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Xg_niU86bhI" title="Wens 540 Handheld Oscilloscope Multimeter Teardown">•</a></sup>

The same principle appears inside components. Some MOSFETs are sold with a Kelvin sense pin and a dedicated current-sense pin that tap the die directly, specifically so that an external sense resistor in the source leg can be read without the package's own parasitics corrupting the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup>

## Accuracy, tolerance and tempco

Tolerance matters less than stability, because a fixed error can be removed in [[calibration]] but a drifting one cannot. A battery emulator uses a pair of 10 Ω Dale resistors in parallel that are only 1% rated, on the reasoning that the gain error is calibrated out later; what they are chosen for is a very low temperature coefficient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> The same requirement drives shunt selection in electronic loads, where the resistor runs hot by design and a poor tempco would make the measured current wander with dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

## Burden voltage and headroom

The drop across the sense resistor is subtracted from the supply's available headroom. With a 1 Ω sense resistor at 1 A, a full volt appears across it, and that volt adds to the dropout budget of everything downstream — it stacks with the pass element's own minimum drop and must be accounted for at design time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> This is the main reason production designs push the value as low as the sense amplifier's input range allows rather than choosing a convenient round number.

The resistor also needs protecting. In one 600 V, 5.1 kW supply a large [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] is placed across the current sense resistor specifically to clamp it against fault conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>

## Where it sits in the circuit

In a linear supply the sense resistor sits in the output path in series with the series pass transistor, with its drop tapped off to an amplifier that feeds the current-limit loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-272" title="EEVblog #272 - Manson 9400 40A 3-15V Switchmode PSU Teardown">272</a></sup> Regulator ICs of the LM723 generation take that sensed voltage directly on a [[current-limit|current limit]] pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The same arrangement in reverse defines the constant-current lab supply: converting 0 to 1 V from a [[microcontroller]] or pot into 0 to 1 A of limit current is exactly what a 1 Ω sense resistor buys.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> [[output-voltage|Output voltage]] sensing is kept separate and taken right at the output connectors or binding posts so the sense resistor's drop does not appear in the regulated voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup>

Switchmode controllers carry a dedicated [[current-sensing|current sense]] input pin, and the resistor's placement is identical across step-down and inverting topologies even when the [[inductor]] and [[diode]] swap places.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Fully integrated linear battery-charger chips move the whole arrangement on-die: a built-in shunt, a [[differential-amplifier|differential amplifier]] across it, and a series pass transistor or [[mosfet|MOSFET]] driven by an [[op-amp|op amp]], with an external programming resistor setting the constant-current value from a datasheet formula.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Charge-pump LED drivers use an external sense resistor to hold [[led-current|LED current]] constant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> Motor driver ICs expose the sense resistors as explicit pins — RS1 and RS2 on a dual H-bridge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup>

An [[electronic-load|electronic load]] is the most reduced form of the idea: an op amp, a transistor and a current sense resistor, with the resistor's drop used as the feedback signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1381" title="EEVblog 1381 - Argon Ion Laser 10kW PSU Teardown">1381</a></sup> Source measure units and multi-range loads extend this by switching between several sense resistors with MOSFETs — one design offers a 1 Ω plus two separate 1.0 Ω resistors and parallels four such sections, each with its own shunt and its own pass MOSFET.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> The same range-switching trick sets the current levels in a multi-mode LED headlamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup>

Dedicated high-side current monitor ICs sense across an external resistor and output a current, which a single load resistor converts back to a voltage — a two-external-component solution, though the part's 2.5 V minimum supply rules out low-voltage rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup>

## Other uses

Beyond regulation, sense resistors serve as fault detectors and as measurement access points. A [[soldering]] station places one in the tip drive so it can flag over-current errors if the tip shorts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> [[usb|USB]] output ports in a mains-powered pack each get their own sense resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> Multimeters place them in the current ranges behind the diode-bridge clamp and the [[fuse]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1578" title="EEVblog 1578 - No one can be told what the Metrix is.">1578</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup> Chargers use one to measure discharge current where cells are stacked in series by a DC-DC converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

A sense resistor is also the standard way to get a current [[waveform]] onto an [[oscilloscope]]. Inserting one in a transistor's emitter leg shows the emitter current directly, which need not match the current in the load it drives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1409" title="EEVblog 1409 - The DANGERS of Inductor Back EMF">1409</a></sup> For side-channel power analysis, breaking a 10 Ω resistor into the [[ground]] line of a USB connection makes the supply current visible — done with a 10-bit ADC scope in high-resolution average mode at maximum [[memory-depth|memory depth]], and with care over where the ground clip goes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1006" title="EEVblog #1006 - Trezor Bitcoin Hardware Wallet Teardown">1006</a></sup>
