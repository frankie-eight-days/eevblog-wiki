A boost converter is a switch-mode DC-to-DC converter that produces an [[output-voltage|output voltage]] higher than its input, storing energy in an [[inductor]] during each switch-on period and dumping that energy into the [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] when the switch opens.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup> It is the standard answer whenever a circuit needs a rail above whatever the supply or [[battery]] can provide — a [[vacuum-fluorescent-display|vacuum fluorescent display]] running from 5 V logic, white LEDs whose forward [[voltage]] exceeds a flat cell, or a 5 V [[usb|USB]] output taken from a single lithium cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1665" title="EEVblog 1665 - Keithley VFD REPAIR 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1665</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RasOXxxEhCk" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL Investigation - Part 1">•</a></sup> Because it cannot step down, its inability to regulate below the input voltage is the single most consequential limitation in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup>

## Topology and parts

The canonical circuit is a series inductor, a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] pulling the inductor's far end to [[ground]], and an output [[diode]] feeding the output capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup> The FET stores energy in the inductor and the inductor then dumps that energy into the output capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The configuration is general enough that a mains [[power-factor-correction|power factor correction]] pre-converter such as the MC34262 is exactly the same arrangement, operated to shape the input [[current]] into a half sinusoid rather than to produce a fixed step-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup>

Integrated controllers vary in how much of this they absorb. Most single-chip solutions contain the switching FET but still require an external inductor, diode and capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1" title="EEVblog #1 - Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope Review">1</a></sup> An AVR ATtiny variant advertised as having a built-in boost converter still needed all the usual external parts — an inductor, a diode and some caps — so the parts-count saving was modest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1" title="EEVblog #1 - Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope Review">1</a></sup> Some converter chips do integrate the diode, which lowers both parts count and component cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Others, such as the LM2623, explicitly require an external one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

Output voltage is set by a [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]] divider, and this is the standard access point for making the output adjustable: replacing or trimming the lower feedback resistor moves the regulation point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1596" title="EEVblog 1596 - NEW Digilent Analog Discovery Pro ADP2230 TEARDOWN">1596</a></sup> In one supply design the lower feedback resistor of a Micrel 2253 boost converter was an [[i2c|I2C]]-interfaced 5 kΩ digital [[potentiometer]], letting [[firmware]] set the pre-regulator voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup>

Switching frequencies run from tens of kilohertz through hundreds of kilohertz and up into the megahertz region, with [[output-ripple|output ripple]] of tens of millivolts and sometimes hundreds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> A 3 MHz part such as the LTC3424 sits at the extreme high end of that range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Electrolytic capacitors are commonly placed in parallel on the output of such converters rather than as a single bulk part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

## The step-up-only constraint

A boost converter that is not switching does not block — it passes the input straight through to the output, minus a diode drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup> A module with a 12 V input sitting at 11.5 V out is therefore behaving exactly as expected when its enable pin has not been driven; it needs an external 3.3 V or 5 V logic signal before it will boost at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup>

The same asymmetry shows up as a hard ceiling in products that omit a boost stage. A 100 W USB-C bench supply fed 12 V can only reach 11.7 V out, because there is no internal boost converter to bump the input up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> A $53 360 W bench module is step-down only, so reaching higher voltages means feeding in a higher supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> A $20 supply built from step-down modules is limited to 11.26 V maximum on a 12 V input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> An [[oscilloscope]]-and-generator instrument taking 5 V from USB offers only ±4 V rails because no boost or SEPIC converter was fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup> Where both directions are needed, a SEPIC accepts input voltages above or below the output — but the premium is real: an LT1512 SEPIC constant-current constant-voltage battery charger cost $3.85 even at hundred-piece quantity, making it the most expensive semiconductor in the design it was considered for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Combined buck-boost and SEPIC modules exist as an alternative to plain step-down units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup>

## Efficiency and part selection

[[efficiency|Efficiency]] is a strong function of [[output-current|output current]], not a headline number. A typical boost converter chip may reach 90% efficiency, but only over a narrow band of output current; at the tens or hundreds of microamps a remote control actually draws, efficiency can fall to around 50%, and some converters are worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

Selecting a part therefore means reading the graphs rather than the front page. Taking a single cell up to 5 V at 2.5 W is a demanding application, and most candidates fail on one axis or another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> [[parametric-search|Parametric search]] narrowed to step-up devices, then to output power, is the practical route — output current alone is deceptive because these chips are specified over very wide input voltage ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Concrete failures from one such search: the LTC3424 delivers 1 A at 5 V from a 3.6 V input at 90% efficiency, but from low input voltages its curve tails off completely by 250 mA and reaches only 50 mA at a 1 V input; a Maxim 1947 offers 0.7 V minimum input and 94% efficiency but a fixed 3.3 V output that will not reach 5 V at all; a Texas Instruments candidate at 1.2 V input runs out near 100 mA, about five times short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

## Applications

Displays and high-voltage loads are the classic case. A vacuum fluorescent display cannot run from 5 V TTL levels, so a small boost converter generates the display rail; the input side carries a 100 µF cap rated 10 V while the output carries a 10 µF cap rated 50 V, and if that output electrolytic loses its [[electrolyte]] its ESR goes through the roof.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1665" title="EEVblog 1665 - Keithley VFD REPAIR 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1665</a></sup> A module built to drive old Soviet VFDs takes 12 V in and produces 32 to 54 V adjustable [[via]] a trimmer, drawing 4.6 mA quiescent from an 8 to 16 V input and holding up under a 10 W constant-power load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup> Valve headphone amplifiers running from a 6 V plug pack likewise need boost circuitry to reach valve operating voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-837" title="EEVblog #837 - Reverse Engineering A Valve Headphone Amplifier">837</a></sup> An electric fence controller stacks two stages: a first switcher generating perhaps 700–800 V across parallel 900 V caps, then a second switching stage taking energy from those caps to boost to the high output voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup>

[[led|LED]] drive is another staple: a Texas Instruments 61500 constant-current boost LED driver with dimming runs five LEDs in a series string from 5 V DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1010" title="EEVblog #1010 - Zyntony Ra LED Strap Light Teardown">1010</a></sup> A TPS61175 3 A high-voltage boost converter with soft start and programmable [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] provides the adjustable positive and negative rails in a portable instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1596" title="EEVblog 1596 - NEW Digilent Analog Discovery Pro ADP2230 TEARDOWN">1596</a></sup> A current-mode boost using a TPS40211 with a switching MOSFET and dual inductors produces 180 V out at a claimed 40 W RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1263" title="EEVblog #1263 - Mailbag">1263</a></sup>

Battery-powered equipment relies on boost conversion to use more of the cell. A [[multimeter]] running from a 9 V battery with a 6.5 V or 7 V cut-out throws away a large fraction of the battery's capacity, and a boost front end recovers it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> Two 9 V cells in series start at 18 V but drop under load below the 15 V needed for diode mode, so a boost stage is required regardless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> An LTC1304 with a 1.5 V minimum input and 10 µA standby current is the sort of device expected in a two-alkaline-cell product, here backing up a 0.1 F 5.5 V [[supercapacitor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> A 34063 serves as the boost converter for a meter's [[transistor]] tester.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> [[backlight|Backlight]] whine in an instrument traces to the boost converter driving LEDs that need more voltage than the flat batteries can supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> Energy harvesting takes the same approach at lower power, whether from a [[solar-cell|solar cell]] or from the boost stage in an isotope-powered cell concept.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup>

Elsewhere, boost stages appear inside an airbag power management controller alongside a buck, a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] and reset watchdog on one die<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-517" title="EEVblog #517 - Car Airbag Controller Teardown">517</a></sup>; in a lithium pack charger that must generate voltages above its 12 V input<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup>; in a robot base so a netbook can run from the same battery<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-429" title="EEVblog #429 - TurtleBot &amp; CineMoco">429</a></sup>; in USB-powered modules producing 9 V or 12 V<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup>; in a two-AA converter giving selectable 5 V or 3.3 V at up to 350 mA on fresh cells<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup>; and inside flash microcontrollers, which generate their own programming voltage internally rather than requiring an external high-voltage pulse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup>

## Pre-regulation

A boost converter followed by a linear regulator makes a tracking pre-regulator: the switcher holds its output only a couple of volts above the linear regulator's output so that dissipation in the pass device stays low while the output retains the lower [[noise]] inherent in linear regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-329" title="EEVblog #329 - Tracking Pre-Regulator LTspice Simulation Part 2">329</a></sup> The drawback of a firmware-driven implementation is that software must continuously measure the output and compensate the pre-regulator voltage to match.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup> Post-regulation is also the general remedy for the high-[[frequency]] [[ripple]] a DC-DC converter leaves on its output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

## Diagnosis and EMC

On the bench a boost stage identifies itself by voltage relationships: a [[capacitor]] sitting at 7.7 V when the battery is lower marks a boost output, and finding 5.2 V at one node and 3.5 V at another distinguishes input from regulated output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup> Single-shot capture of a converter starting up shows the ramp: one speaker amplifier boosts from just under 4 V of battery to 5 V and then steps deliberately to 12 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1605" title="EEVblog 1605 - JBL Charge Speaker REPAIR">1605</a></sup> A failure appears as the absence of that behaviour — a 5.143 V input and 4.98 V after the output diode means the converter is not switching<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup>, and a battery bank showing 3.3 V and then 4.4 V on its boost node with a live input points at a faulty controller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-V0RWwSw96Sw" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL - Part 2">•</a></sup>

Boost converters are a standard EMC demonstration vehicle, with paired good and bad layouts of the same 300 kHz converter boosting 19 V to 24 V into an [[electronic-load|electronic load]] at around 420 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-oVkrc3gF7ns" title="Conducted Mode EMC Emissions Demo">•</a></sup> As a design task the topology is comparatively tractable: an LLC resonant converter is vastly more difficult to design than the traditional [[pwm|PWM]] boost, buck or SEPIC converters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1294" title="EEVblog #1294 - LLC Resonant Mode Converter Design">1294</a></sup>

## Limits on what boosting can achieve

A boost converter cannot create energy that is not in the source. Products marketed on the premise that a boost sleeve unlocks most of a discarded battery's capacity founder on this point: manufacturers' discharge curves stop at 0.8 V because there is very little energy left below it at ordinary loads, though ultra-low-power boost converters can reach some of it at very small drains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Holding the output at a fixed 1.5 V as the cell sags means drawing progressively more current from the battery, and the converter's own efficiency loss compounds that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-963" title="EEVblog #963 - Batteriser / Batteroo Passive Toy Test!">963</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> On passive loads the result was a toy train running faster but dying at 94 minutes against 169 minutes unassisted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-963" title="EEVblog #963 - Batteriser / Batteroo Passive Toy Test!">963</a></sup> Where the cells were genuinely flat, no converter helped at all — it does not matter how good the boost converter is if there is no energy left in the batteries.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> The realistic gain from adding such a stage to a product that already discharges its cells reasonably far is an extra couple of percent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup> A converter small enough to fit inside a battery sleeve also carries very limited [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] and peak current capability, leaving pulse loads to be handled by the product's own decoupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> The engineering judgment is direct: a boost converter is not magic, and what it can deliver is bounded by the energy remaining in the source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-789" title="EEVblog #789 - Batteriser Monkey BUSTED!">789</a></sup>
