Signal averaging is an acquisition mode in which an instrument captures the same [[waveform]] many times and combines the captures point by point, so that [[noise]] uncorrelated with the trigger cancels while the repeating signal survives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> Its practical value is that it pulls signals out of the [[noise-floor|noise floor]] that are otherwise invisible — a 10 mA reading from a [[current]] probe specified for 50 mA, a 100 microvolt trace, a [[sine-wave|sine wave]] buried at 10 mV per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> The cost is that the result is no longer a real-time picture of what the probe tip is doing, which constrains where averaging can be used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

## Mechanism and the analog precedent

Averaging works because random noise on the analogue front end or on the incoming signal is not synchronised to the sweep or the trigger, while the signal of interest is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> An [[analog-oscilloscope|analogue oscilloscope]] achieves the same effect optically: components uncorrelated with the sweep appear dim or not at all, so the phosphor performs an average by way of brightness, which is why an analogue instrument shows a clean trace where a digital one shows a noisy one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> A digital scope samples and displays each acquisition instead, so in plain sample mode the display shows every noisy record as it arrives, with the waveform changing constantly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup>

The same reasoning applies to noise-driven [[frequency]]-domain measurement. Random noise injected across a band deposits energy at every frequency in that band, and repeated averaging of the spectrum builds up a smooth, flat response curve that a swept sine generator would otherwise be needed to produce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

## Averaging depth

The number of averages is the primary control, and useful values in bench practice range over more than an order of magnitude: 8 for a lightly coupled pickup loop, 16 for a [[bandwidth]] check or a bypass-[[capacitor]] waveform, 20 to 40 for a differential probe response, 50 to 80 for very low level probe work, 64 for a 100 microvolt signal, and 100 for op-amp noise plots and filter sweeps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> The improvement is progressive rather than sudden: with a single average nothing is visible, at four averages a sine wave begins to emerge, at ten it is already usable, and at eighty it is clearly resolved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

Averaging also buys effective resolution beyond the converter's native width. A 12-bit acquisition in high-definition mode gives 16 bits effective, and adding 20 averages on top takes it to 18 bits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> A 12-bit ADC running with 100 MHz acquisition bandwidth and 50 averages yields around 15 bits, which is the practical reason a high-resolution instrument is needed for low-level differential probe measurements at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

Depth is not free of side effects. At 100 averages, drift in the [[dc-offset|DC offset]] of the measurement chain becomes visible as a slow wavering of the averaged trace, which is why instruments in this class provide a zero-offset control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> The average must also be restarted whenever the measurement conditions change, since the accumulated record is otherwise stale.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1329" title="EEVblog #1329 - Magnetic Field Shielding DEMONSTRATED">1329</a></sup>

## Limits and confusions with other acquisition modes

Averaging requires a stable trigger. A trace that refuses to clean up under a large average count is usually not triggering properly rather than genuinely noisy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup> It is also unavailable at very slow time bases and does not function in roll mode, which rules it out for slow physiological traces; high-resolution mode, which applies a boxcar rolling average, remains available in those conditions but does not deliver the same cleanup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>

Averaging is one of several distinct acquisition modes and should not be confused with them. High-resolution mode, peak detect, digital phosphor, and fast acquisition all coexist with averaging on modern instruments and serve different purposes — fast acquisition, for instance, displays waveforms at rates of a hundred thousand or more per second rather than combining them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> Not all instruments provide the full set: some offer averaging without any high-resolution mode at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #858 - Red Pitaya Extended Cut">2</a></sup>

Because averaging destroys the ability to probe in real time, it is switched off when the operator needs to see what is actually happening at the moment of contact, and switched back on to read a clean number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup>

## Applications

Averaging is a routine step in bench measurement wherever the quantity of interest is close to the noise floor:

- **Bandwidth measurement.** Locating the point at which a 1 V peak-to-peak sine drops to 0.707 of its amplitude requires a stable amplitude reading, which 16 averages provides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup>
- **Low-level pickup.** Loosely coupled pickup loops and [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] probes give traces too noisy to interpret without averaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1329" title="EEVblog #1329 - Magnetic Field Shielding DEMONSTRATED">1329</a></sup>
- **Differential probe characterisation.** [[frequency-response|Frequency response]] plots taken at millivolt levels are dominated by noise and coupling; setups at this level are sensitive enough that a hand brought near a signal lead disturbs the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup>
- **Current probes.** Averaging allows measurement well below a probe's nominal specification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup>
- **Spectrum and [[fft|FFT]] work.** Increasing FFT point count lowers the displayed noise floor, and averaging on top of that brings signals further out of the noise; it also improves the usability of a scope FFT used as a substitute for a spectrum analyser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1188" title="EEVblog #1188 - $10 DIY EMC Probe using Scope FFT">1188</a></sup>
- **Noise density measurement.** Spectrum analysers accumulate a set number of averages before the reading settles — figures such as 22.2 and 24.56 nanovolts RMS per root hertz are quoted only after 100 averages have completed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-529" title="EEVblog #529 - HP 35660A DSA Upgrade Investigation">529</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup>
- **Photon counting.** Where individual counts are random, only the mean carries information; the baseline mean is taken first and subtracted, because the individual counts cannot distinguish signal from noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-869" title="EEVblog 869 - Counting LED Photons!">869</a></sup>
- **Power analysis.** Averaging repeated captures of a current-sense waveform is how data is recovered from the noise in powerline side-channel work, and dedicated tools exist for precisely this.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup>

## Averaging in firmware

The same principle applies below the instrument level. An ADC reading a [[potentiometer]] wiper shows visible noise on the last bits of a 12-bit result when no averaging routine has been implemented in [[firmware]], and adding one is the standard remedy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1463" title="EEVblog 1463 - Mailbag">1463</a></sup> Environmental monitoring instruments that must reject transient events such as doors slamming apply heavy averaging in software for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup>
