A soldering iron is a temperature-regulated heated tip used to melt [[solder]] into an electrical joint between a component lead and a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] pad. It is the single most-used tool on an electronics bench, appearing in every minimum lab specification alongside a [[multimeter]] and a pair of side cutters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ln_XJDPKJlc" title="eevBLAB 103 - Is an $800 Oscilloscope OVERKILL?">•</a></sup> Its defining performance parameter is not the temperature it displays but the rate at which it can put heat into a joint that is actively pulling heat away — a distinction that separates an iron that solders from one that merely gets hot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup>

## Temperature and thermal capacity

Standard 60/40 and 63/37 tin-lead solder melts at roughly 183 to 190 °C, yet almost no temperature-controlled iron will regulate that low; a typical unit bottoms out at 200 °C and ranges up to about 480 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> The reason is thermal load. A tip sitting in free air will hold 190 °C and will melt solder, but the moment it touches a joint the work draws the heat out and the tip is no longer at 190 °C, so the joint never reaches melt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1351" title="EEVblog #1351 - Mailbag">1351</a></sup> The headroom above the melting point exists to overcome that loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup>

A working bench setting of about 350 °C covers general through-hole and surface-mount work, with 370 °C used for kit assembly and heavier joints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup> Older fixed-temperature irons commonly ran near 700 °F, around 370 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> Lower settings suit specific tasks: 280 °C to keep a chip's pins molten while lifting it with a low-melting-point alloy, 250 °C for wicking the residue afterwards, and 260 °C with a bare tip for reheating a connection made with conductive [[adhesive]] rather than solder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-437" title="EEVblog #437 - Removing SMD Parts with ChipQuik">437</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1100" title="EEVblog #1100 - Dumpster Photocopier Repair">1100</a></sup> At the top of the range the physics becomes visible — heat a tip to around 500 °C and it begins to glow a dull red.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1187" title="EEVblog #1187 - Room Heater Technology Explained">1187</a></sup>

[[thermal-capacity|Thermal capacity]] is what actually gets measured when irons are compared. Bench stations in the common class run 65 to 70 W at the iron, while a high-end cartridge iron may be rated 130 W peak or more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup> Rated wattage is not the same as delivered heat: an older-style iron sucked hard enough will fail to keep power flowing continuously into the tip because of how the heater and tip are physically constructed, and portable irons advertised at 80 W and 100 W have been shown not to meet their claimed thermal capacity in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5vbg8QEZXfY" title="iFixit FixHub Soldering Iron Review EXPLAINED + More Testing">•</a></sup> The failure is unmistakable at the low end — an iron with no thermal capacity can be held on a single pad and pin for over 30 seconds without heating it enough to solder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup> Uncontrolled irons carry the opposite fault: one that free-runs toward 500 °C will lift pads off a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup>

The distinction between old and new iron technology lies mainly in the tip, not the station. Traditional irons place a ceramic heater element inside a sleeve with the tip slipped over it; modern cartridge designs drive and sense the tip directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> Old-style irons remain thoroughly useful tools.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup>

## Tips

Irons are usually supplied with a conical tip, which is of very little practical use and is best set aside; a chisel tip is the correct default for nearly all work, including [[surface-mount-technology|surface mount]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> The chisel earns its place by contacting the pin and the pad simultaneously, which is what actually transfers heat into the joint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> The most important single habit in [[hand-soldering|hand soldering]] is keeping the flat of the chisel flat against the board: if a joint refuses to heat, the cause is almost always that the tip is not making full flat contact, and twisting the iron until it seats will fix it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup>

Fine conical tips do have narrow justifications. [[soldering|Soldering]] a 0.4 mm pitch QFN into pre-tinned grooves requires a tip physically smaller than 0.4 mm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-408" title="EEVBlog #408 - Schmart Board 0.4mm QFN SMD Soldering">408</a></sup> At the other extreme, a very large tip lets leftover pins be wiped off an entire side of a footprint in one pass after a chip has been removed, though dragging across pads applies lateral force that can lift them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup> A tip with a slight bulge behind the working face can be the difference between reaching a joint and hitting the components standing up around it during rework.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> Tips do not need to be new to work — old, oxidised tools still produce good joints on ordinary work, though not to aerospace standards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> A clean tip is a precondition for a good connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1463" title="EEVblog 1463 - Mailbag">1463</a></sup>

## Technique

The order of operations is fixed: the iron goes onto the joint first, and only then is solder fed in. Kit instructions that direct the solder to be placed against the joint before the iron have it backwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Heat the pad and the pin together until both are up to temperature, then feed the solder in from the far side of the pad so it flows around both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1205" title="EEVblog #1205 - RC2014 Z80 Computer Kit Build">1205</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup> Firm pressure and a held-down board help; a light tug from the iron is enough to shift a small board across the bench, which is why weighted or clamped holders are useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-680" title="EEVblog #680 - Mailbag">680</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-906" title="EEVblog #906 - Mailbag">906</a></sup>

Copper mass fights the iron. A pad tied directly into a [[ground-plane|ground plane]] will pull heat away faster than the tip can supply it and can take a very long time to solder, which is why thermal reliefs — a pad connected to the pour by four spurs rather than a solid joint — belong in board layouts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Soldering a TO-220 tab down to a plane calls for a much larger chisel tip and 1 mm solder, and heavy power wiring into large pads needs a correspondingly high-capacity iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> Layout affects the iron in the other direction too: a minimal annular ring leaves too little exposed copper for the tip to contact before the solder is fed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

Surface-mount work is a matter of preparation rather than special equipment. [[flux|Flux]] is the key to good SMD soldering; the component is tacked at two opposite corners and the remaining pins reflowed afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> [[solder-mask|Solder mask]] carried between the individual pins keeps solder out of the gaps, because solder will not wet mask — it can be blackened but never tinned — and flows onto the pins instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Fine solder matters: 0.46 mm or smaller is appropriate, and 0.7 or 1 mm makes a poor job of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Drag soldering with a plain chisel tip handles fine-pitch packages in seconds, and dragging outward away from the pins rather than along them helps prevent bridges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-997" title="EEVblog #997 - How To Solder Surface Mount Components">997</a></sup> Excess solder bridging two pins is removed by laying the iron between them and dragging back out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EjpUYGRXzRQ" title="Sagan Soldering!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> Even a QFN with a bottom [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]] can be done with a conventional iron, flux and 0.5 mm solder if the pads run out far enough, with no paste or hot air required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup>

## Two irons and part removal

For rework, a second iron is more valuable than a [[desoldering]] station, because sucking solder from one side does nothing for a surface-mount part that must be released on both sides at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> Two irons with wedge tips will lift a tantalum or [[electrolytic-capacitor|electrolytic capacitor]] off a board immediately, and the same dual-wielding technique is the traditional way to remove small SMD components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1650" title="EEVblog 1650 - Sequre HT-140 SMD Desoldering Tweezers REVIEW">1650</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup> Where two irons are kept, identical models allow tips to be interchanged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-51" title="EEVblog #51 - A tour of the EEVblog Electronics Lab">51</a></sup> Larger packages can be released by flooding both rows of pins with solder and alternating the iron between them until everything is molten, or by cutting the pins away and cleaning the remnants with wick.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> A [[bga|BGA]] cannot be removed with a conventional iron at all; that is hot air work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup>

## Selection and quality

The floor for anyone serious about electronics is a temperature-controlled station rather than a fixed-temperature iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> Roughly $110 buys a genuine article, and spending less than that is a poor place to economise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup> Cheap clones in the $25 class do function if funds are genuinely short, and even a bottom-tier temperature-controlled station is preferable to a fixed-tip iron plugged straight into the wall.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1063" title="EEVblog #1063 - Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX888D Soldering Station">1063</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-596" title="EEVblog #596 - World&#x27;s Cheapest Soldering Station - Yihua 936">596</a></sup> The consistent engineering judgment is to buy a reputable brand for the iron specifically, since it is the tool in constant use and must be reliable, repairable and supported with available parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup> Combined desoldering-gun-and-iron units and [[battery]]-operated irons are not recommended.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup>

Portable irons are a separate category with their own limits. A [[usb|USB]]-powered unit of about 18 W is adequate for general field work and repairs but not for high-power tasks, and portable irons in general are compromises even when good.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1195" title="EEVblog #1195 - Sony 4K 60&quot; LCD TV Audio Repair">1195</a></sup> Modern portable stations pair a battery hub with a USB-C iron and expose settings and [[firmware]] updates over a serial connection to a host computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup>

Construction quality varies sharply at the low end: cheap mains-powered irons arrive with no cord strain relief and dangerously poor cable, and clone stations have been found with wobbling bases and handpieces whose plastic breaks before first use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-893" title="EEVblog #893 - Mailbag">893</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mV0-pV1C6Yk" title="Hakko FX-951 Ripoff First Look, Rant &amp; Use">•</a></sup> A more serious defect is the absence of a primary-side mains [[fuse]] in soldering stations, which caused a 120 V unit to release the magic smoke when plugged into 240 V; the same omission was found in an older analogue station from the same maker, with not even a thermal fuse in the [[transformer]] windings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1160" title="EEVblog #1160 - Weller Responds!">1160</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup>

## Bench environment and other uses

Rubber ESD matting resists heat and will not burn through under an iron; the cheaper PVC type melts on contact and should be avoided.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-585" title="EEVblog #585 - Lab Bench ESD Matting Upgrade + Tagarno HD Microscope">585</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-250" title="EEVblog #250 - Anti-Static Mat Myth">250</a></sup> Working under magnification imposes its own constraint — the microscope's [[working-distance|working distance]] determines whether the iron can come in at a usable angle, and a shallow approach angle is bad news for joint quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-282" title="EEVblog #282 - DIY Video Soldering Microscope">282</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-566" title="EEVblog #566 - Cheap USB Microscope Reviews">566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1640" title="EEVblog 1640 - Mailbag: 4k Microscope, Panaplex Displays, Piezo Singing, RF magic">1640</a></sup> At around 45° the geometry works; at 25 mm or so of clearance a slim microscope still leaves room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-282" title="EEVblog #282 - DIY Video Soldering Microscope">282</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-566" title="EEVblog #566 - Cheap USB Microscope Reviews">566</a></sup> Shooting or working at awkward angles degrades results, which is why a dedicated soldering position with proper access is worth arranging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-510" title="EEVblog #510 - Mailbag">510</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-679" title="EEVblog #679 - How To Build A Small Electronics Work Bench">679</a></sup>

Beyond joining, the iron serves as a general controlled heat source: burning enamel off fine magnet wire so solder will take, injecting heat into a thermistor or [[thermocouple]] junction to observe the response, burning away lamination over conductive tape, and welding a plastic case shut after it has been cut open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-539" title="EEVblog #539 - RFID Tag Card Repair">539</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-S-UqkM8iDKM" title="#1179 - PowerFilm Flexible Indoor Solar Cell">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup> It is also the tool for board modifications, which is one argument for leaving vias untented so a mod wire can be attached without scraping tinning off the pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Bridging two exposed pads with the iron is a documented way to change a boot sequence on some instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tM2j9zuBiRQ" title="Siglent Signal Generator Fix">•</a></sup> Hot air is preferable for heat shrink, where controlled temperature beats an iron or an open flame.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup> Industrially, the same principle scales up: a hot bar press is effectively a very large wedge tip that solders a row of fine contacts under pressure in one operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup>

One point of handling is worth stating plainly, since stock imagery routinely gets it wrong: you don't hold a soldering iron with the hot part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1136" title="EEVblog #1136 - Banksy Artwork Shredder - Part 2 (Still Trolling!)">1136</a></sup>
