Arduino is an open-source [[microcontroller]] development ecosystem built around the Atmel ATmega family, combining a standard board footprint, a bootloader, and a simplified C development environment into a single package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> The board itself is not the Arduino — it is the generic carrier for the ecosystem, and the same toolchain will program a bare ATmega soldered onto a board of one's own design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Its significance is that it removes the setup work that normally stands between a beginner and a working microcontroller program, without giving up a real compiled language.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup>

## Architecture of the platform

Underneath, the environment uses the AVR GCC compiler, but the compiler is hidden from the user.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Programs are essentially standard C, with floats, double-precision floating point, for loops, and the rest of the usual C constructs available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> What the environment adds is a level of abstraction above raw AVR GCC or a PIC C compiler: header files, register configuration, [[oscillator]] options, and input-pin setup are handled by the framework rather than by the programmer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Register-level initialisation is a substantial hurdle in conventional microcontroller development — a micro that has not had its peripherals configured simply does nothing — and eliminating it is the platform's main practical contribution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup>

Because the programs are ordinary C compiled by AVR GCC, they are portable, which makes the platform a professional development tool rather than only a hobbyist one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> The trade-offs are specific: there is no low-power solution, any peripheral without an existing library requires writing one, and a project large enough to max out a conventional micro is not a good fit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup>

The hardware side is a standard header layout that accepts stackable expansion boards, or shields.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Board designs, schematics included, are published, so derivatives and clones are common — Ethernet variants with power-over-Ethernet supply so that a single LAN cable both powers and connects the board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-203" title="Melbourne Hacker Space Visit - EEVblog #203">203</a></sup> kit-form versions intended to be assembled by hand,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-203" title="Melbourne Hacker Space Visit - EEVblog #203">203</a></sup> and boards with expanded peripheral counts such as two USARTs, four [[spi|SPI]], two I²C, nine [[pwm|PWM]] channels and additional GPIO.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup> Compatibility has also been claimed by hardware with entirely different silicon: PIC32-based boards running an 80 MHz MIPS core,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> and [[fpga|FPGA]] boards laid out to the R3 form factor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-636" title="EEVblog #636 - FPGA Demo Boards - DE0 Nano">636</a></sup>

Programming a board or a bare chip requires either an in-system Atmel programmer or a pre-programmed bootloader, in which case a cheap [[usb|USB]]-to-serial converter board is sufficient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> The FTDI USB-to-serial part is the standard choice for adding USB to a design whose microcontroller only offers a [[serial-port|serial port]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-676" title="EEVblog #676 - RANT: FTDI Bricking Counterfeit Chips!">676</a></sup> On the host side an attached board simply enumerates as a generic serial port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WXJP_CNYt3o" title="#1179 - Stempedia Evive Electronics Learning Kit">•</a></sup>

## Code size and execution timing

Program memory is rarely the binding constraint. A ten- to twelve-line sketch driving a character [[lcd|LCD]] occupied about 2.3 K of the 30 K available on the board, and that figure already includes the [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]], string handling, and other library overhead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Where a program does have to be squeezed byte by byte, the correct response is to choose a part with more memory rather than to optimise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> Wi-Fi-capable targets shift the baseline considerably: a plain [[led|LED]] flasher built for the ESP8266 consumes around 222 K, roughly 21 percent of program space, because the entire Wi-Fi stack is linked in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-998" title="EEVblog #998 - How To Program ESP8266 WiFi With Arduino">998</a></sup>

Execution timing matters where a sketch bit-bangs a protocol. The standard `digitalWrite` routine takes a couple of microseconds to execute, which is significant against a modulation bit time of 562 microseconds; the fix is to subtract the routine's overhead from the nominal delay, which in one infrared transmitter meant trimming a delay down to nine microseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup> Faster pin-access methods exist, but `digitalWrite` is the framework's basic idiom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup>

## Electrical interfacing

Outputs are 5 V TTL on the classic boards, which is a recurring source of interface problems. Driving logic that expects less than 2 V requires a [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] on each line — a 1 K and 680 ohm pair, for instance — when the target board has no series resistors of its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1531" title="EEVblog 1531 - Can ChatGPT generate all the code for my Macgyver Project? (Part 3)">1531</a></sup> A 3.3 V display module needs level converters in the other direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup> High-[[voltage]] display drivers can be worse still: some require a logic high of VDD minus two volts, meaning a 10 V input signal, and are therefore not compatible with 5 V TTL at all, so a level translator chip or [[transistor]] pull-up arrangement is mandatory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-948" title="EEVblog #948 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 1">948</a></sup> Mixed-supply projects that combine a 5 V board with 3.3 V radio hardware need a conversion board between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup>

An [[lcd-segment|LCD segment]] can be driven directly from board pins by generating in-phase and out-of-phase [[waveform|waveforms]], which produces twice the supply rail peak to peak across the segment from a single logic rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1045" title="EEVblog #1045 - How To Drive an LCD">1045</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1046" title="EEVblog #1046 - Mysterious Digital Voltage Doubling (LCD design)">1046</a></sup> Driving a segment with a static logic one instead, against a grounded common pin, applies a long-term DC bias to the panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZtOXfFkV2jw" title="LCD Bias Experiment!">•</a></sup>

Onboard regulation is stable over temperature. Measured at room temperature, one board's rails sat at 5.015 V and 3.407 V; over a 12 °C excursion the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] moved by 1 mV while the 3.3 V rail moved by 5 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup>

## Grounding and probing hazards

A board powered over USB from a desktop computer is not isolated. Its [[ground]] is connected to [[mains-earth|mains earth]] through the USB cable and the computer chassis, measuring around 9 ohms to earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> Clipping an [[oscilloscope]] ground lead to the board's ground point is safe, but clipping it anywhere else — a [[power-rail|power rail]], or an arbitrary node on an attached shield — shorts that node to earth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup> The board's own supply cannot deliver enough energy to be dangerous, so the result is a supply short and a shutdown rather than an explosion, but the circuit stops working and the fault is easy to misdiagnose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup>

Probing fine-pitch signal pins directly carries its own risk of shorting adjacent pins and destroying the board; [[soldering]] up a female-to-male extender to bring the signals out is the safer approach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> When [[troubleshooting]] a display that is receiving clock and latch signals but showing nothing, confirming that the board is actually outputting valid 5 V logic on each line narrows the fault to the data path or to the sketch itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

## Typical project patterns

The platform's characteristic use is a task that needs to work within minutes rather than hours — setting a few logic outputs, or putting text on an LCD — where wiring a bare PIC onto a [[breadboard]] would be disproportionate effort.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup> Beyond that it appears as the control element in a wide range of builds: shorting out the contacts of a remote control by wiring an output pin across the switch,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-505" title="EEVblog #505 - IR Learning Remote Control Hack">505</a></sup> transmitting a reverse-engineered infrared code directly from an LED on an output pin instead of hacking the remote at all,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-506" title="EEVblog #506 - IR Remote Control Arduino Protocol Tutorial">506</a></sup> persistence-of-vision displays,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EHLRQbCW5oM" title="Guest Video: Electronoobs - Building a POV Display">•</a></sup> a light-painting staff carrying a board plus an RF module,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-227" title="EEVblog #227 - Light Scythe">227</a></sup> motion control for milling machines and 3D printers [[via]] the RAMPS-style boards and G-code interpreters such as GRBL,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-843" title="EEVblog #843 - David&#x27;s rPrint 3D Printer Design">843</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-753" title="EEVblog #753 - Inventables X-Carve Milling Machine Unboxing">753</a></sup> and a four-axis robot arm whose inverse kinematics run on the board so the arm can be commanded in XY coordinates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-586" title="EEVblog #586 - Open Source Hardware uARM 4-Axis Desktop Robotic Arm Kickstarter">586</a></sup>

Where a bench project needs a serial-attached controller alongside a full PC, an Arduino-compatible section is sometimes integrated onto the same single-board computer, with the host application talking to it over a serial port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BoL8gD8IcXg" title="Mantis Elite Cam &amp; LattePanda Project FAIL">•</a></sup>

The instrument-design case is instructive. A lab [[power-supply|power supply]] built around an ATmega168 or ATmega328 was made Arduino-compatible specifically because the [[firmware]] would be written in the Arduino environment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> That decision propagated into the hardware: a ceramic resonator and an external AVR ISP header for in-circuit programming,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> an FTDI-based programming interface brought out to the rear panel so the case need not be opened to reflash the firmware,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> I²C pull-ups dropped to 2.2 K because five devices on the bus were slewing the edges badly enough to lose bits,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> and the constant-[[current]] setpoint driven from a hardware PWM output rather than a second DAC channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> The current-sense front end used a programmable-gain amplifier with a 320 mV full-scale input across the shunt — 0 to 320 mA at 1 ohm — and autoranged under firmware control to hold 12-bit resolution down to 10 microamp steps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> Debugging that firmware turned up faults on both sides of the boundary: an I²C LCD library that misbehaved even when substituted with the manufacturer's own code,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> and a DAC that produced nothing because only 14 of the required 16 bits were being clocked out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>

## Development-tool and ecosystem effects

The platform's hardware design was shaped by the free tools available to it — the board was laid out within the limitations of the free version of Eagle, and the wider open-source hardware movement grew up around those same free tools and their constraints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-527" title="EEVblog #527 - Altium Entry Level PCB Tool Rant">527</a></sup> Boards in the standard form factor are cheap to fabricate because they are small, and the classic board's edges are rough to the touch on all four sides, the telltale of a V-grooved panel snapped apart after assembly rather than individually routed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ct9K-EutiIQ" title="JLCPCB Prototype Inspection">•</a></sup>

Third-party silicon vendors have treated the platform as a target to be matched or joined: supporting standard platforms is a way for a semiconductor manufacturer to get its parts into the hands of people who will later specify them professionally,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-123" title="EEVblog #123 - Top 5 Tips for Semiconductor Manufacturers">123</a></sup> and a competing low-cost development board that stopped short of compatibility gave up part of its potential reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-96" title="EEVblog #96 - The TI LaunchPad MSP430 Development Board">96</a></sup> The ESP8266 in particular is programmed through the Arduino environment via an added board-support package, which installs support for the Adafruit Huzzah, NodeMCU and WeMos boards at once; the chip costs about $3 and includes Wi-Fi.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-998" title="EEVblog #998 - How To Program ESP8266 WiFi With Arduino">998</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yabl87SD-3M" title="Guest Video: Brian Lough - ESP8266 Libraries">•</a></sup> The WeMos D1 Mini's board package maps its [[silkscreen]] pin names into the framework, so an ordinary `digitalWrite` to D0 works as it would on any other board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VTAwzrKPjeE" title="Nixie Tube Display ESP8266 Wemos D1 Mini Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-952" title="EEVblog #952 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 3">952</a></sup> A large third-party library ecosystem covers web APIs, MQTT, addressable LED strings, and quadrature decoding, the last of which removes the need to write a direction-detecting algorithm by hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yabl87SD-3M" title="Guest Video: Brian Lough - ESP8266 Libraries">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1573" title="EEVblog 1573 - TEARDOWN: How a Rotary (Angle) Pulse Encoder Works">1573</a></sup>

The platform is not accepted everywhere. In industrial embedded computing, proposing an Arduino or a Raspberry Pi in place of an established standard such as PC/104 is not taken seriously, and PC/104 has outlasted three decades of contenders.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1028" title="EEVblog #1028 -  PC104 - The Full Version">1028</a></sup>

## Design philosophy and criticism

The platform was originally aimed at artists rather than engineers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> The criticism levelled at the maker movement it fed is that dropping in a board and blinking lights can stop a learner from digging down to the layer where the current direction through an LED and the registers behind a library call actually live; the value appears when the learner takes that next step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> The counter-position is that the platform expands embedded design rather than diluting it, since it brings more people in and remains one tool among many rather than a replacement for conventional microcontroller work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup>

Compared with the PICAXE, the Arduino is the less simple of the two — PICAXE programs can be entered as flowcharts with no programming language at all — but PICAXE is not a professional platform, whereas Arduino code is real C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup> That last point is the basis for preferring it over niche teaching languages: a beginner who learns C on this platform, library-dependent as the code may be, transitions seamlessly to industry-standard C afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> Programmers who insist that assembly is the only legitimate way to write embedded code, and that they can do it faster than any higher-level language, are simply wrong about the economics of the work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-45" title="EEVblog #45 - Arduino, PICAXE, and idiot assembler programmers">45</a></sup>

## Corporate ownership and licence terms

Arduino was acquired by Qualcomm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup> Prior to the acquisition the company had taken $53 million across two venture rounds from investors including Renesas, Bosch, CVP Capital and Andreessen Horowitz, with annual unit sales in the range of one to two million; the acquisition price was not disclosed, but a figure of at least $250 million is a reasonable estimate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup> Revenue came from hardware and from software services, with hardware the dominant share.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup>

The revised terms and conditions issued afterwards reserve the right to change, suspend or discontinue any service, with paid premium services honoured only for their specified term and 30 days' notice on shutdown — standard terms, but consequential for any product design that depends on the company's continuing services.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup> A newly added clause targets under-18 users, who must supply a date of birth at registration and receive a limited account, despite entry-level hobbyists being a large part of the platform's base.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup> Because the platform is open source, it can be forked regardless of what happens to the company.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup>
