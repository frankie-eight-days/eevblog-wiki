The output capacitor is the [[capacitance]] placed between a [[power-supply|power supply]]'s output terminal and its return, and it does two largely separate jobs: it stores and smooths energy so the rail holds up between switching or rectification events, and it forms part of the feedback loop that determines whether the regulator is stable at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup> In a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]] it is the reservoir the [[inductor]] dumps its stored energy into, and its value sets the [[output-ripple|output ripple]] directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> In a linear or low-dropout regulator it is a compensation component, and its value, its [[equivalent-series-resistance|equivalent series resistance]], and even its physical distance from the regulator can decide between a clean rail and an [[oscillator]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup>

## Role in switching and rectified supplies

A basic [[boost-converter|boost converter]] works by storing energy in an inductor through a switching FET and then letting the inductor dump that energy into the output capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The same [[capacitor]] is the filter element in a buck or flyback topology, and behind a mains [[transformer]] and [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] it is what turns rectified half-cycles into something resembling DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

For a design-from-scratch switching regulator the required [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] follows from the [[ripple]] specification: nine times the [[output-current|output current]], times the on-time, divided by the permitted ripple [[voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> For 100 mA output, a 7.3 µs on-time and a 100 mV ripple budget this gives 66 µF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Where that is not good enough, an optional second-stage LC output filter can be added after the main output capacitor — in one such design a 33 µH inductor followed by a further 100 µF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Behind a rectifier the same trade exists: ripple can be reduced by simply increasing capacitance, but post-regulation is often the better answer than an ever-larger capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

In a switched-capacitor voltage [[inverter]] such as the 7660 the output capacitor, conventionally labelled C2, is what determines the residual ripple, though the result depends jointly on the [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]], the internal [[resistance]] of the switches, and the ESR of the capacitor itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Because of that dependence, 7660 datasheets typically do not contain a ripple-versus-output-[[current]] graph at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

Output capacitance is also one of the parameters that sets converter [[efficiency]], alongside the [[mosfet|MOSFET]] and the switching frequency, when a DC-DC design is optimised for a particular output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

## The ESR floor

Ripple cannot be reduced indefinitely by adding capacitance, because past a certain point the ESR of the capacitance takes over and sets a floor on the achievable ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> The mechanism is a packaging constraint: for a given package size, the higher the capacitance the higher the ESR must go.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> The same limit shows up in practice at the other end of the [[frequency]] range — enlarging the capacitor across the output of a 2000 W supply reduced the low-frequency ripple noticeably, from roughly 200 mV peak to peak toward 70 mV, while leaving the higher-frequency switching content essentially untouched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

## Stability in linear and low-dropout regulators

Linear regulators are closed-loop systems, and the output capacitor sits inside that loop. Standard NPN-pass linear regulators traditionally require a minimum output capacitance to keep the loop stable; the LDO variant using a PNP pass [[transistor]] is more unstable still, requiring both a capacitance within a specified range and an ESR within a specified range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup> The underlying analysis is conventional control theory — Bode plots, phase diagrams, poles and zeros.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup> Getting it wrong produces [[oscillation]], and the error can be in the value, the type, or the placement distance from the regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

This is the principal hidden cost of the low-dropout family. The 1117 is widely treated as a jellybean part because it offers the advantages of the 78xx series and the [[lm317|LM317]] with better dropout, but its output capacitance is critical, and LDOs in general become unstable if the output capacitance or output [[impedance]] requirements are not met.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Where the divider resistors around a regulator are cheap, a 100 µF output capacitor that simultaneously satisfies the value and impedance requirements for stability often is not, and LDO datasheets frequently devote an entire section to output capacitor selection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Not every regulator needs one. Contrary to common belief, the fixed 78xx series does not require an output capacitor for stability and is stable with no output capacitance at all; the customary 0.1 µF is optional, as is the input capacitor unless the regulator sits a significant distance from the input filter capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The LM317 likewise does not strictly need an output capacitor for stability, though it is normally fitted as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> There is one interaction to respect: if the optional adjust-pin capacitor is used to improve ripple rejection, the output capacitor must be larger than it, or the regulator goes unstable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> On a working LM317 design the 1 µF recommended value proved somewhat generous — 100 nF on the output is perfectly stable in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Some regulators are remarkably tolerant: a one-cent part ran a 100 mA load with no input or output capacitance at all and no oscillation, and remained well behaved with 330 µF hung on the far end of the supply lead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

Precision voltage references occupy the opposite corner from LDOs: the better jellybean references need no output capacitor and tolerate a capacitive load, so existing bypass capacitors on the powered circuit are not a hazard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

The same stability requirement propagates into application circuits built around linear blocks. A lithium-ion charger IC has internal charging circuitry equivalent to a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]], and its loop is unstable unless the recommended output capacitance is fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> In a precision 1 A [[current-source|current source]] the loop stability is set by the phase margin of the reference's internal output amplifier, and the [[datasheet]] specifies an output capacitor — 10 µF in that case — with an ESR below 0.1 Ω; both the ESR and the capacitance matter, and oscillation in such a circuit usually means insufficient output capacitance for loop stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> Such circuits are worth building and verifying before committing to a [[pcb|PCB]]: if a [[breadboard]] prototype cannot be brought to even semi-stable behaviour while swapping input and output capacitor types, the design is in trouble regardless of how much tighter the PCB loops will be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup>

Conversely, a capacitor placed directly on the output of an op-amp is a liability rather than a benefit — it can affect stability, and a 100 nF part added on an op-amp output in the hope of reducing [[noise]] is better left off entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup>

Substituting a pin-compatible switching regulator for another carries the same caveat: even with identical output-voltage programming, the ESR of the output capacitors must still be correct, because some circuits misbehave without it, and the datasheet detail has to be checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> Loop-response measurement is the systematic way to settle these questions — injecting a signal into the feedback divider of a DC-DC converter and measuring magnitude and phase reveals the stability under various loads and allows the correct output capacitor and ESR to be chosen rather than guessed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup>

## Constant-current supplies and the case for minimum capacitance

In a bench supply with constant-current capability, output capacitance is actively harmful. The [[current-limit|current limit]] is enforced somewhere back on the board, but the output capacitor sits downstream of it and can deliver a spike of energy straight into the load, over and above the set current, during the changeover into constant-current mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> Charge stored on the output can be delivered before the control loop has time to respond at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> The design goal for any lab supply with constant-current mode is therefore to minimise the output capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup>

Real instruments frequently violate this. A 1,000 µF output capacitor is very high for a supply with constant-current capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> Another bench supply carried more output capacitance than it should, distributed across several 80 V parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> In a 360 W supply an additional capacitor was placed literally on the output terminals in parallel with the main output bank, adding to an already significant total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> In a 2000 W supply, large low-ESR output capacitors dumping current into the load on the transition into constant-current mode tripped the load's own [[input-protection|input protection]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

The problem is not confined to power supplies. A [[battery]]-profiling instrument with 1,000 µF on its output supplies short load pulses from that reservoir capacitor rather than through the current-sense resistors, corrupting the measurement of exactly the transients such an instrument exists to capture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

Removing output capacitance is not free either, and there is a genuine optimum. In a lab supply design, the transient response on the changeover into constant current was clean with 22 µF, noticeably worse with 100 nF, and worst of all with no capacitor at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> The complementary problem is discharging what remains: the stored charge is bled off deliberately, either by a [[bleeder-resistor|bleeder resistor]] permanently across the output rail discharging a couple of hundred microfarads on the output terminals, or by a transistor that dumps the capacitor through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] when constant-current mode is entered — the [[resistor]] value being a direct trade against turn-off speed, taking tens of milliseconds at 1 kΩ and approaching a direct short as the resistance falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

## Diagnosis, failure and repair

Output capacitors are a routine suspect in supply faults, and ESR is the usual measurand. High ESR in the output capacitors is the statistically likely cause of a burnt supply, since it leads to excess ripple on the output even under modest load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> In-circuit ESR probing distinguishes good parts from bad quickly: on one [[oscilloscope]]'s regulator board the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] output capacitor read as healthy while the pre-regulator capacitor measured almost 3 Ω and was the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> Capacitance is worth checking too — a failed negative rail from a 7660 inverter came down to an output capacitor measuring 600 nF where 22 µF was specified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup>

Output capacitors can equally be exonerated. Multiple capacitors in parallel on the secondary side, all visually sound with no bulging, argue against the output stage as the cause of a repeating hiccup, which is characteristically a primary-side start-up problem instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Parallel arrangements are also easy to misread on a board: capacitors that appear to be wired in series may in fact be in parallel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup>

Not every capacitor on an output is there for regulation. A 0.1 µF part across the output of a supply may exist purely for EMC and CE compliance, making no measurable difference to the output noise, so its omission is a compliance change rather than a performance one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup> Values in this role are small — a 0.047 µF part specified on a laboratory supply's output, with 0.1 µF an acceptable substitution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-667" title="EEVblog #667 - Agilent 6643A Power Supply Binding Post Hack">667</a></sup>

## Construction and layout practice

The output capacitors are physically the largest components on many secondary sides, and manufacturers commonly glue them down with silastic so they cannot flap around in service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> Their placement matters electrically: the return path from the output capacitors to [[ground]] must not be made through a single [[via]], which presents high [[inductance]] and constitutes poor board layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1475" title="EEVblog 1475 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1475</a></sup> Where capacitors are singular rather than doubled up, there is no redundancy against the short-circuit failure mode of ceramic capacitors, which can in turn lead to fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup>

In cost-reduced offline supplies the output capacitor's size directly determines a visible performance parameter. An [[led|LED]] driver using transformer feedback with no secondary-side regulator IC and no [[optocoupler]] will almost certainly flicker, and how much depends on the type and size of the output capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dLlhoUHlnjE" title="LED Flicker 3 - More Electric Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Adding a 470 µF, 50 V capacitor alongside an existing 330 µF one measurably reduced the 100 % ripple on the [[led-current|LED current]] without disturbing the [[power-factor|power factor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1253" title="EEVblog #1253 - LED Flicker 2: Electric Boogaloo">1253</a></sup>

Output capacitance appears in the smallest circuits too: a die with only a small amount of on-chip output capacitance will charge that capacitance up, briefly flash an LED, and drop back down, repeating — the flashing being purely an artefact of the charging cycle rather than of any signal being received.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup>
