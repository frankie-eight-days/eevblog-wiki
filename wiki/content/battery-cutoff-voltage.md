The battery cutoff voltage is the terminal [[voltage]] at which a [[battery]]-powered product stops functioning, or at which its functionality is no longer guaranteed — the point where a low-battery indicator lights, a shutdown circuit trips, or the device simply ceases to work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> It is defined at the battery terminals of the product under its own load, not at the cell's open-circuit terminals, and it is expressed per cell so that it applies regardless of how many cells are stacked in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> The figure matters because it sets the boundary of the usable region of the battery's discharge curve: everything below the cutoff is capacity paid for and never delivered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

## Typical values

For a modern active electronic product, the cutoff sits at roughly 1 V per cell, and measurements across about ten different products gave an average of about 1 V or just over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Battery manufacturers' characteristic discharge curves stop at 0.8 V, the industry-standard endpoint, on the understanding that there is "bugger-all energy left in these batteries once they hit 0.8 volts".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup> Below that point the curve collapses — anything under 0.8 volts, "it just basically drops off like a rock" — making 0.8 V per cell effectively the lowest cutoff worth designing to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

Any product intended to run on both nickel-metal-hydride rechargeables and primary alkaline cells must cut off at 1.1 V per cell at the absolute most, because a NiMH cell spends its working life near that level; a cutoff set at 1.2 V would strand most of the rechargeable cell's capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> In practice almost any decently designed product on the market that accepts both chemistries has a dropout between 1.1 and 1.0 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Claims that consumer devices stop working at 1.35 V or 1.4 V per cell do not survive bench testing — a survey of battery-powered equipment in a working lab turned up no product that dropped out at those levels, and even an unregulated thermometer with no low-battery indicator still ran at 1.1–1.2 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

Well-designed converters can go far lower. A Fenix LD01 keychain flashlight running from a single cell holds its regulated brightness with no perceptible change as the supply falls, drawing about 100 mA at 1 V, and only cuts out around 0.5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-78" title="EEVblog #78 - Fenix LD01 LED Keychain Flashlight And Victorinox Minichamp Review">78</a></sup> At the other extreme, a Fluke 37 [[multimeter]] powered from a 9 V battery drops out somewhere between 6.4 and 6.5 V; its low-battery symbol is latched at power-on rather than monitored continuously, so the symbol will not appear mid-session as the battery sags.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WWMXJLhPVdA" title="34,000 Hour Battery Life Fluke 37 Multimeter">•</a></sup>

## Measuring it

The standard method is to replace the battery with a bench [[power-supply|power supply]], wind the voltage down until the product cuts out, and read off the value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> For a high-[[current]] product the reading is taken with a meter connected as close as possible to the battery terminals rather than from the supply's own display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Because a power supply presents essentially zero source [[impedance]], it forces the chosen voltage directly onto the terminals, which is exactly the quantity the product's internal [[comparator]] and [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] respond to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> The alternative — connecting a real cell and waiting for it to discharge — can take days or months to reach the same point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

The objection that this ignores the battery's internal [[resistance]] inverts the physics. A real cell's internal resistance manifests only as a drop between its open-circuit voltage and its terminal voltage; the product sees the terminal voltage and nothing else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> Setting a supply to 1.0 V therefore reproduces the loaded condition of a cell whose terminal voltage has fallen to 1.0 V, internal resistance included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup> The genuine error is the opposite one: measuring a cell's unloaded open-circuit voltage and treating that as a point on a discharge curve is meaningless, which is why manufacturers publish only loaded curves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

## Cutoff and wasted capacity

Energy in a cell is the area under its discharge curve, measured in watt-hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Drawing a horizontal line at the product's [[cutoff-voltage|cutoff voltage]] and dropping a vertical from where it intersects the curve separates the delivered energy from the stranded remainder.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> For an alkaline AA, moving the cutoff from 1.0 V down to 0.8 V recovers only about 4–5% more energy at a given discharge current, and the exact figure shifts with load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> That margin rarely justifies the extra bill-of-materials cost or the technical difficulty of operating a circuit down to 0.8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> The same arithmetic destroys the premise behind boost-converter sleeves marketed as [[battery-life|battery life]] extenders, whose business case assumed products quit at 1.35–1.4 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup>

Lowering the cutoff is nevertheless a legitimate design lever wherever it is cheap. A multimeter powered by four AA cells has a 6 V nominal supply, and honouring a chip's 4.8 V minimum specification would set the cutoff at 1.2 V per cell — before the regulator's dropout is even counted — discarding perhaps half the pack's capacity, whereas running the chip outside its specified range directly off the cells recovers it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup> More generally, the design choices that follow from a target cutoff include swapping a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] for a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]] to obtain a lower [[dropout-voltage|dropout voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

Practical measurement of the cutoff is slow enough that dedicated instruments address it. A battery-profiling tool that substitutes for the cell lets a designer ask what-if questions — a different chemistry, a different cutoff — and re-optimise the design accordingly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> The natural extension is a mode that replays a chosen cell's discharge profile, which would allow the cutoff behaviour of a product to be exercised without stocking real cells or manually tweaking a supply down to the threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Capacity measurement practice

When capacity is measured by discharging a cell to a chosen cutoff, the cutoff is the stopping condition and must be caught precisely; overshooting it forces the endpoint to be interpolated from the logged curve instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup> A quality alkaline AA — a Duracell Procell with four years of stamped shelf life remaining, fresh from the box — delivered just over 2 Wh at a continuous 250 mA discharge, reaching 0.8 V at roughly the 7.5 hour mark.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup>
