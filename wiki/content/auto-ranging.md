Auto ranging is the ability of a measuring instrument — most commonly a [[digital-multimeter|digital multimeter]] — to select its own measurement range automatically, choosing the attenuation or gain setting that gives maximum resolution for the applied signal. It matters because manual ranging, in which the user pre-selects a range on the dial, is slower to use, and manual-ranging meters carry no price advantage over auto-ranging equivalents, so there is no reason to buy one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Nearly all modern digital multimeters are auto-ranging, whereas analog meters are inherently manual-ranging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1067" title="EEVblog #1067 - Analog vs Digital Multimeters!">1067</a></sup>

## Implementation

Auto ranging is implemented at several levels of an instrument's architecture. At the chip level, dedicated [[multimeter]] chipsets incorporate the range-switching logic directly: the Fluke 27's proprietary AP25 chip combines the ADC, active filter, buffer integrator, and input network selection that performs the auto ranging<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup>, the Hycon HY3131 chipset is shared by the [[121gw|121GW]], the Keysight U1282A, and the Gossen Metrahit Ultra<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uUCQuIp_hzU" title="121GW Ohms Autoranging Speed Update">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup>, and parts such as the SDIC SD7500 are sold as complete auto-ranging multimeter systems-on-chip including [[lcd|LCD]] drivers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup>

Range switching is done either with relays or with analog switches. Meters that have no manual [[range-switch|range switch]] at all must contain relays, which are audible as a clunk when the range changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1457" title="EEVblog1457 - Old School Mailbag - ESC Burnout">1457</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> [[oscilloscope|Oscilloscope]] front ends similarly use per-channel relays that click on power-up and on every auto-range change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup> Hardware auto ranging can also be built from comparators that set the thresholds at which range changes occur.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> A cheaper alternative avoids switching altogether: the Voltech PM300 power analyser runs several op-amp gain stages in parallel, lets the higher-gain stages overload and saturate, and simply selects the appropriate output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup>

## Ranging speed

Ranging speed is a primary differentiator between meters and a standard evaluation test. The quick in-shop check is to select ohms and short the probes: if the meter takes much more than about a second to cycle down through its ranges to zero, it is slow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Measured examples span roughly an order of magnitude. The Brymen [[bm235|BM235]] ranges in under a second, close to human reaction time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-izCDWjK_pV4" title="121GW Autoranging Speed">•</a></sup> The 121GW takes around three seconds, with the exact time varying depending on where the continuously running measurement cycle happens to be when the input is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-izCDWjK_pV4" title="121GW Autoranging Speed">•</a></sup> At the slow end, meters in budget shootouts have been described as "slow as a wet week".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 2of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1734" title="EEVblog 1734 - Uni-T UT892 2000V High Voltage Multimeter TESTED">1734</a></sup>

Speed is largely a property of how the manufacturer implements ranging on a given chipset rather than of the chipset itself: the 121GW, Keysight U1282A, and Gossen Metrahit Ultra share the HY3131 yet range at different speeds, while Brymen's fast meters use a custom chipset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uUCQuIp_hzU" title="121GW Ohms Autoranging Speed Update">•</a></sup> [[firmware|Firmware]] also matters — 121GW ohms auto-ranging was improved in firmware 1.04, again in 1.05, and again in 2.00, and a community-produced hacked firmware targeted the same slow ranging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uUCQuIp_hzU" title="121GW Ohms Autoranging Speed Update">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-q4gXnpFPFzQ" title="121GW Multimeter 2.00 Firmware">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3MD88EYLdQQ" title="121GW Hacked firmware test">•</a></sup> Update rate and ranging speed are independent: the Agilent U1253A updates its ohms display 14 times per second yet has notably slow auto ranging on that same range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup>

In automated test systems, avoiding range changes entirely is a throughput advantage: an 8.5-digit voltmeter can be locked on its 100 V range and still read any incoming signal accurately, whereas a 6.5-digit meter may have to change ranges, and each change takes time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 3 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Why ranging is needed: dynamic range

Ranging exists because no single shunt and converter can cover the full span of real signals. Measuring microamp-level sleep [[current]] in the presence of amp-level bursts with one shunt would require something like a 32-bit ADC, which is impractical, so current-measurement instruments switch shunts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> A device drawing tens of microamps in sleep cannot be resolved on a 180 mA range with a 14-bit converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

Auto ranging is not a complete solution to this problem. A true auto-ranging current instrument needs hysteresis so the ranging does not oscillate back and forth on small dips in current, and switching the shunt fast enough not to brown out the device under test is difficult; for some products the only workable approach is a fixed manual range with the device forced into one mode at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

## Failure modes

- **Inductive inputs.** Measuring the primary of a [[transformer]] confuses auto ranging — the large [[inductance]] makes the meter skip erratically between ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup>
- **Mains-[[frequency]] [[noise]].** With too short an integration time (a low number of power line cycles), picked-up 50/60 Hz noise makes meters range chaotically between, for example, the 1 V and 10 V ranges, with relays flicking continuously; a smoothing filter does not fix it, but setting the integration to one or more power line cycles does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup>
- **Stuck ranges.** A faulty BM235 measured correctly on a manual range but its auto ranging stuck at a wrong reading (e.g. showing 16 MΩ for a 100 Ω input) — a fault that manifests only in the auto-ranging path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0z5dGXlRibA" title="A Very Strange BM235 Fault">•</a></sup>
- **[[overshoot|Overshoot]].** Some meters overshoot badly while ranging up; in one test a 10 V step produced an 18 V reading on an Extech meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup>
- **Mode interactions.** On some meters, engaging min-max or record mode forces the instrument onto a fixed manual range, so an overrange during recording is possible; others stay in auto range during recording.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup>
- **Auto-scan hazards.** Auto function-selection modes that must identify the input before measuring can mishandle high [[voltage]]; one meter in auto-scan mode failed destructively when connected to 240 V mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup>

## When to force a manual range

As bench practice, auto ranging is frequently overridden to save time or to get a valid measurement at all. Fixing the range manually avoids waiting for the ranging cycle when probing around a circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup> Low-level noise measurements on a dynamic signal analyser require a fixed input range rather than auto ranging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> In frequency-response measurements, letting the input auto range can clip the channel and corrupt the coherence trace, producing a spurious response that disappears once the range is set correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> When measuring capacitors in circuit, the function should be set manually so the auto-selection algorithm is not confused by parallel active elements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Some meters do not auto range down to the millivolt range on AC volts, so low-level AC signals require manual ranging regardless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> Conversely, auto mode can be faster than manual: the Agilent 34461A produces an intermediate unsettled reading on a fixed range that it suppresses in auto mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

## Related automatic functions

Auto ranging is distinct from, but often bundled with, *auto function selection*, in which the meter identifies what it is connected to — DC volts, AC volts, [[resistance]], or continuity — and switches function as well as range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1706" title="EEVblog 1706 - K8 Wrist Worn Multimeter REVIEW">1706</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup> Such auto-detection requires firmware thresholds, which introduce arbitrary cut-outs: the Brymen BM2257's auto-sensing LowZ mode stops detecting below about 0.93 V even though there is no electrical reason it could not read to zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> Auto modes may also search only a subset of functions — volts, ohms, and continuity but not [[capacitance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1420" title="EEVblog 1420 - Mailbag">1420</a></sup> Auto ranging interacts with other features in meter-specific ways: some relative/null modes continue to auto range after the offset is taken, which is the more useful behaviour, while others freeze onto one range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-137" title="EEVblog #137 - BK Precision 879B Handheld LCR Meter Review">137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-216" title="EEVblog #216 - Gaussian Resistor Redux">216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup>

Beyond multimeters, the same concept appears as auto set or auto scale on oscilloscopes — where implementations can fail even on a basic 1 kHz [[sine-wave|sine wave]]<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup> — as per-sample auto ranging in audio and [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] measurement routines that maximise [[dynamic-range|dynamic range]] on each acquisition<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>, and in LCR meters, which scan ranges to decide whether a device under test is dominantly capacitive, inductive, or resistive, a decision that depends on the chosen test frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1473" title="EEVblog 1473 - How Your LCR Meter Works">1473</a></sup>
