The operational amplifier (op amp) is a DC-coupled differential amplifier with extremely high open-loop gain, designed to be operated inside an external negative-feedback network that defines the actual closed-loop behaviour of the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> The name reflects the original application in electronic analog computers, where such amplifiers performed mathematical operations — addition, subtraction, multiplication, and the like.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-854" title="EEVblog #854 - B52 Bomber Automatic Astro Compass Teardown">854</a></sup> Because the bare gain is enormous, poorly controlled, and temperature-dependent, it is unusable on its own; negative feedback converts that unwieldy gain into accurate, stable amplification set by a few passive components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

## Idealised behaviour

Two rules of thumb make closed-loop op amp circuits analysable by inspection. With negative feedback present, the op amp drives its output to whatever [[voltage]] is required to force its two inputs to the same potential, and its high-[[impedance]] inputs draw negligible [[current]] from the source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> The output is low-impedance and can source a reasonable current — milliamps to tens of milliamps for ordinary parts, and a couple of hundred milliamps for power op amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

A consequence that confuses beginners is the virtual ground: in the inverting configuration the summing node is held at the non-inverting input's potential, so a signal probed on one side of the input [[resistor]] appears to vanish at the node and reappear at the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

## Standard configurations

- **Buffer / voltage follower:** output tied directly to the inverting input, giving unity gain with high [[input-impedance]] and a low-impedance output capable of driving loads from a sensitive source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>
- **Non-inverting amplifier:** a feedback divider from output to inverting input to ground sets a precise, stable gain from the raw open-loop gain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>
- **Integrator:** a [[capacitor]] in the feedback path with the input referenced to ground produces an integrator, the core of dual-slope ADCs used in digital multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup>
- **Difference amplifier:** a single op amp with a four-resistor network forms the standard differential stage, used in everything from current-shunt measurement to high-voltage differential probes; a three-op-amp instrumentation version appears in [[thermocouple]] cold-junction compensation ICs with a trimmed internal [[temperature-sensor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-419" title="EEVblog #419 - Thermocouple Tutorial">419</a></sup>
- **[[comparator]]:** an open-loop op amp works as a comparator — e.g. as a zero-crossing detector or a supply current-limit latch — although a purpose-built comparator such as the LM311 is usually the better choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup>
- **Howland current pump:** five resistors and a [[voltage-reference]] around one op amp generate a constant current in the few-to-tens-of-milliamps range for driving LEDs or sensors where [[efficiency]] is unimportant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUKf-4rv_sQ" title="EEVacademy #8 - Howland Current Pump">•</a></sup>

Unused sections of dual and quad packages should be properly terminated rather than left floating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> Switching gain by breaking the feedback path turns a stage into a unity-gain follower, but inserting switches into the feedback path carries traps for the unwary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

## Key parameters

### Input offset voltage

[[input-offset-voltage]] (V<sub>OS</sub>) is an internally generated error inherent in all op amps, including ultra-low-offset parts; it cannot be measured by simply placing even a 6½-digit [[multimeter]] across the input pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-476" title="EEVblog #476 - Opamp Offset Voltage Measurement">476</a></sup> A general-purpose part exhibits millivolts — the LM358 runs to several millivolts — while a precision bipolar part such as the OPA227 holds 10 µV worst case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup> Chopper (auto-zero) amplifiers reach about 1 µV, and the best parts on the market approach 0.1 µV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-476" title="EEVblog #476 - Opamp Offset Voltage Measurement">476</a></sup> Offset is multiplied by the closed-loop gain: 1 mV of offset in a ×100 amplifier produces 100 mV of output error, which is why a ×100 current-shunt amplifier resolving microvolt-level signals essentially forces the choice of a chopper part such as the MAX4238/4239.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup> Even a 5 µV-offset part becomes significant when trip thresholds sit below a millivolt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> Where residual offset must be eliminated entirely, a [[chopper-amplifier]] is the standard remedy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup>

### Input bias current

Small currents flow into or out of the input pins. They generate offset across source resistances, set the droop rate of peak-hold capacitors once [[diode]] leakage is eliminated, and can reverse direction over the supply-voltage range, making compensation impractical — the realistic options are to accept the error, fix the operating point, or choose another part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> Femtoamp-input parts exist, and picoamp-input precision parts such as the LT1012 are used where leakage dominates the error budget.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup> Bias current also drifts with temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup>

### Noise

Input noise is specified as a voltage density in nV/√Hz and splits into two regimes: inescapable flicker (1/f) noise dominating below roughly 10–100 Hz, and flat Gaussian white noise above it — so op amps are noisier toward DC, not quieter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> Datasheets quote spot noise at 10 Hz, 100 Hz, and 1 kHz precisely to allow comparison across the 1/f corner, but headline banner specs are chosen to flatter and the graphs must be compared directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> Representative figures span from about 40 nV/√Hz for a TL072, through 4 nV/√Hz for an NE5534, down to 0.88 nV/√Hz for an LME49990.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-541" title="EEVblog #541 - Mailbag">541</a></sup> Verifying state-of-the-art low-noise parts requires a measurement preamplifier quieter than the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup>

### Gain-bandwidth and cascading

Gain-[[bandwidth]] product varies between individual units of the same part, with supply voltage, and with test [[frequency]], so a design needs margin — pushed to the limit, the op amp begins to distort.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-572" title="EEVblog #572 - Cascading Opamps For Increased Bandwidth">572</a></sup> Cascading two lower-gain stages yields more total bandwidth than a single high-gain stage; total noise is dominated by the first stage, so maximum gain belongs at the front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-572" title="EEVblog #572 - Cascading Opamps For Increased Bandwidth">572</a></sup> This is the technique used to extract bandwidth from cascaded chopper amplifiers in precision [[current-measurement]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup>

### Common-mode rejection

An op amp's own [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] commonly exceeds 100 dB and is rarely the limiting factor: in practical differential amplifiers and probes, resistor-divider matching dominates and can drag the system CMRR down to 40 dB or less.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

### Supply rails and output swing

±15 V is about the upper supply limit for ordinary op amps; rails of ±21 V or more exceed what jellybean parts tolerate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Classic parts such as the LM741 cannot swing near either rail, so outputs clip well short of the supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> Single-supply parts accept inputs at — and slightly below — the negative rail and drive the output to ground, which is essential when the signal source is a 0–2 V DAC referenced to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> Rail-to-rail CMOS parts handle low-voltage designs but many are limited to 5.5–6 V total supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dv0B_WqL7w4" title="UniT 210E Current Clamp Teardown + Brymen Comparison">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-661" title="EEVblog #661 - Mailbag">661</a></sup> Where the output must swing both polarities about ground, a split supply is required; a part run from ±1.5 V simply perceives a 3 V single supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

## Stability and capacitive loads

Op amps generally do not tolerate large [[capacitance]] on their outputs, which can push the feedback loop into oscillation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> A [[series-resistor]] between the output and a capacitive load such as a [[mosfet]] gate is the standard isolation measure, but it does not magically decouple the load — the capacitance still shifts a loop pole.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup> Substituting a different manufacturer's LMV321 into an existing design has caused outright oscillation, cured more cleanly by bypassing the virtual-ground divider on the input side than by hanging capacitors on the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup> Loops that enclose pass transistors — current sources, regulator error amplifiers — likewise oscillate if not stabilised, particularly at high load current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Applying an input voltage beyond the supply rails destroys the input stage: feeding 10 V into a part powered from a 6 V rail kills the input op amp, and unruggedised inputs are the usual enabler.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-727" title="EEVblog #727 - How To Kill An Opamp">727</a></sup> Failed op amps typically go short or low-impedance internally, draw excess current, and run hot, making a thermal camera — or a finger — a first-line diagnostic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup> Because everything shares the rail, one supply overvoltage event can kill dozens of op amps spread across a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-540" title="EEVblog #540 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 3">540</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>

## Applications

- **Regulator error amplifier:** the op amp drives a series-pass [[transistor]] so the divided-down output equals the reference, which is the regulation mechanism of linear supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>
- **Constant-current sources and loads:** op amp, pass transistor, and shunt give I = V<sub>REF</sub>/R<sub>SHUNT</sub>; a bench dummy load needs little more than an op amp, a MOSFET, and a few resistors, with op-amp output offset setting the minimum achievable current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> A precision reference such as the REF102 plus a precision resistor and an OPA227 yields a microamp-class current source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup>
- **Precision rectifier:** placing the diode inside the feedback loop makes the op amp absorb the diode drop in real time, compensating for temperature and diode quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>
- **Peak detector:** a precision rectifier charges a storage capacitor; a smaller capacitor tracks faster but droops more, the op amp spends the inter-peak time slammed against the negative rail and must recover, and a following buffer presents a low-impedance source to an ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>
- **Negative-rail generation:** a diode charge pump can produce a low-current negative supply for op amps from a single rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup>
- **Audio:** the JRC 4580 is the classic dual audio op amp at 0.05 % typical distortion; the OP275 uses a Butler front end combining JFETs and bipolars with 9 MHz bandwidth; in a large mixing console, thousands of op amps at 5–10 mA each make [[quiescent-current]] a supply-design issue in its own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ag-MjKAfATw" title="Rode AI-1 USB Audio Interface Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-840" title="EEVblog #840 - Yamaha M3000 Mixing Console Teardown">840</a></sup>
- **High-speed and high-current output:** current-feedback parts such as the THS3095 (210 MHz, high-voltage, high-current) serve as function-generator output stages, and some current-feedback amplifiers have remained available only in DIP packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1005" title="EEVblog #1005 - Keithley 2302 Battery Simulator Teardown">1005</a></sup>
- **Composite amplifiers:** enclosing one amplifier inside another's feedback loop combines their strengths — a chopper front end for microvolt offset wrapped around a high-current output buffer, or a power audio amplifier enclosed in an op-amp loop for extremely low distortion — a technique largely absent from textbooks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1609" title="EEVblog 1609 - Composite Amplifier Tutorial + Practical Demo">1609</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vwAhHz7Zpzk" title="Guest Video - Karl Adams - Audio Distortion Measurement">•</a></sup>
- **Capacitance multiplier:** an op-amp variant of the transistor capacitance multiplier works, but is constrained by the op amp's limited output current and gain-bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

## Choosing parts

If any specification genuinely matters to a design, the correct approach is a purpose-selected part rather than a jellybean; thousands of op-amp types exist precisely because requirements differ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> High-end instruments reflect this, using many different op amps each chosen for a specific role rather than one type throughout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup> Jellybean parts — the LM358 with its ground-sensing inputs, or the TL071/072/074 JFET family common in [[oscilloscope]] front ends — are acceptable where millivolts of offset are irrelevant, but even a jellybean-adjacent part like the LMV321 has caused failure when swapped across vendors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> There is no single go-to op amp in professional practice, because each new design optimises around different constraints.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup>
