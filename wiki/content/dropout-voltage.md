Dropout voltage is the minimum differential a [[voltage-regulator|voltage regulator]] requires between its input and its output before it can no longer hold its output constant. Every [[linear-regulator|linear voltage regulator]] carries a dropout voltage specification, and the input must sit at least that far above the output or the part falls out of regulation entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> The figure is not a footnote in a [[datasheet]]: it sets the minimum supply rail a design can run from, and in [[battery]]-powered equipment it directly determines how much of the cell's stored energy is reachable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup>

## Definition and measurement

The specification is the input-to-output differential [[voltage]], so a regulator producing 5 V from a part quoted at 2 V minimum drop needs at least 7 V at its input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Below that differential the internal control loop no longer has the headroom to function — the circuitry that drives the pass element must itself be biased above the regulated output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> The consequence is exactly what the name says: it drops out of regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

Measured in practice, dropout is defined at the point where the output falls by 100 mV from its regulated value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The bench procedure follows from that definition: hold the load [[current]] fixed, wind the input voltage down while watching the output, and record the input-output difference at the moment the output starts to sag.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> A 3.3 V rail from an [[lm317|LM317]] at 20 mA load and 25 °C measured 4.803 V in against 3.26 V out, giving 1.54 V of dropout and matching the datasheet curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Watched on an [[oscilloscope]], the approach to dropout is visible as more than a DC sag — the output stays roughly present but becomes unstable as the loop loses control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

## Dependence on current and temperature

Dropout is not a single number. It rises with load current and varies with temperature, which is why datasheets present it as a family of load lines on a differential-voltage-versus-temperature graph rather than a single entry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> An LM317 characterised this way shows separate curves for 20 mA, 200 mA, 500 mA, 1 A and the maximum 1.5 A output, with the highest-current curve sitting well above the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> At 25 °C the 20 mA line gives about 1.5 V while the 500 mA line gives roughly 1.75 to 1.8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> The temperature dependence matters mainly at industrial extremes; for room-temperature bench work the middle of the curve is representative.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

The current dependence is what turns a comfortable margin into a marginal one. A 3.3 V output at 1.5 V dropout needs 4.8 V in, which [[usb|USB]] nominally supplies, but the same regulator at 500 mA needs 5.1 V — technically above what a 5 V USB rail provides, and USB is specified at ±5 %, so the supply could legitimately be as low as 4.75 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> An LDO with 0.2 V dropout in that same application would have left ample margin and delivered the full 500 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

Failing to meet a part's minimum load current specification can also degrade dropout, among other unpredictable behaviours; a part quoting 500 µA minimum load must be given that load across the entire [[output-voltage|output voltage]] range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

## Standard linear regulators versus LDOs

A conventional linear regulator uses an NPN Darlington pass element, so the input-output drop must cover two base-emitter drops plus the pass [[transistor]]'s saturation voltage — which is why a 7805 typically shows around 1.5 to 2 V, and sometimes more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> The precise figure depends on the specific variant and the load current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> The LM317 is likewise not a low-dropout part; its datasheet quotes a 3 V minimum input-to-output differential typically, more than the 7805, though a 2 V minimum drop at full current is the usual working rule of thumb.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> Several volts of mandatory headroom makes such parts unsuitable wherever the available input sits close to the desired output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

A [[low-dropout-regulator|low dropout regulator]] is simply one with a smaller tolerated input-output difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Special low dropout types reach several hundred millivolts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup> and modern jellybean parts do better still: a SOT-23 regulator rated 250 mA specifies 0.2 V dropout at 90 mA rising to 0.4 V at 150 mA, and measured 300 mV at its maximum recommended 250 mA — a figure its datasheet omits entirely, along with any performance graphs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> For a 3.3 V output, an LDO of this class needs only about 3.4 V in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Sub-100 mV dropout is available where it is needed; 50 mV parts inform where a low-battery detector threshold can be set relative to the highest-voltage part in a design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup>

The low differential is bought at a cost. LDOs use a PNP or P-channel pass element that is inherently less stable, so the loop is more sensitive to the value and ESR of the [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]], and their [[ground]] current can scale with [[output-current|output current]] and waste power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Standard linear regulators are simpler and more stable but demand the larger [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup>

Dropout also interacts with [[ripple]] rejection. The attenuation an LDO provides from input to output depends on the actual input-output differential, and gets worse as that differential falls, so a regulator running with very little headroom passes most of the input [[noise]] straight through.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Higher output current degrades the rejection further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> This makes an LDO a poor choice for cleaning up tens or hundreds of millivolts of ripple unless it is given real headroom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

## Parts with a separate control pin

Some regulators specify dropout in two ways, giving distinct figures for the control pin and for the input pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> On the LT3080 these pins are normally tied together, in which case the worst-case combined figure applies — 1.2 V at 100 mA, and as bad as 1.6 V at full current, so a 5 V output needs at least 6.6 V in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup> Measured on the bench with the pins tied, the part showed about 1.25 V at no load and 1.35 to 1.4 V at 1 A, agreeing with the datasheet curve once [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]] above 25 °C is allowed for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

Where a higher voltage already exists elsewhere in the circuit, powering the control pin from it — for example from the far side of a [[current-shunt-resistor|current shunt resistor]] — yields a substantially lower input-to-output dropout than tying the two pins together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup> The technique is not universally available: some smaller pin-count packages bond the two pins internally, leaving no separate control connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup> The part's headline 350 mV dropout at full load applies only under the favourable pin arrangement, not the tied-together case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

## Switching converters and buck regulators

Switching converters have a dropout condition too. A buck topology cannot produce an output above its input, so the input must exceed the desired output by whatever the converter's dropout amounts to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> A module rated 6 to 40 V in and 0 to 32 V out therefore requires the input range to sit at least one dropout above the intended output range; a 0 to 12 V bench supply built from it could be fed from 13 to 15 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup> Measured with no load, one such module fed 32 V produced a maximum of 31 V out, giving 1 V of dropout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> Dropout can be far larger in high-power designs: a 2000 W buck supply specified for a 96 V output accepts 20 V minimum and 110 V maximum input, implying at least 14 V of dropout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

A switching design can also enter dropout through component sizing rather than input voltage. A converter designed for 100 mA held regulation to about 170 mA before the output began to fall, the limit set by the chosen component values; substituting a 47 µH [[inductor]] for a 33 µH one moved the dropout point down to about 120 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

Because switchers tolerate a much smaller input-output difference, they are the standard remedy where a linear regulator wastes usable range: a 5 V regulator running from a six-cell alkaline 9 V battery drops out around 6 V — roughly 1.15 V per cell — while at least 1 V per cell and ideally 0.8 V per cell is needed to extract most of the battery's capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> Any competent switch-mode converter reaches that easily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> The same reasoning appears in battery-life optimisation generally: moving from a linear regulator to a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]] to obtain lower dropout is one of the standard what-if changes when a design's runtime is being re-optimised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Consequences for battery-powered design

Dropout sets the effective [[cutoff-voltage|cutoff voltage]] of a battery-powered product, and that cutoff determines how much capacity is usable. A single-cell lithium polymer battery discharging to a 3 V cutoff feeding a 3.3 V LDO with 50 mV of dropout cannot run below about 3.35 V, which moves the cutoff point far up the discharge curve and wastes something like 45 to 50 percent of a 1000 mAh cell's capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> Powering a 3.3 V rail directly from a single lithium polymer cell through a linear regulator is therefore poor practice unless a switching converter is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> The same arithmetic applies to alkaline cells: a 4.8 V minimum supply implies a 1.2 V per cell cutoff before the regulator's own dropout is even counted, discarding roughly half the batteries' capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup> Where a chip's true operating limit is well below its specified minimum — one meter's highest-voltage internal part functioning down to 3.6 V against a datasheet figure of 4.8 V — the low-battery threshold can be set only slightly above that limit, provided the regulator's dropout is small.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup>

Equipment that fails this test fails visibly. A [[multimeter]] whose display brightness collapsed as its supply was wound down, going dark by about 2.5 V, cannot work across the 1.25 V per cell range at which a meter should still function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

Claims of extracting extra energy from cells by boosting their voltage have to be evaluated against this backdrop: whatever gain a booster offers by working below a product's own dropout voltage is offset by the converter's [[efficiency]], which falls off sharply at higher currents, and the product's current draw is unknown to a general-purpose part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

## Dropout in service and diagnosis

Dropout voltage is a diagnostic tool during repair. If a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] is derived from a pass transistor with an assumed 2 V maximum dropout, its raw input must sit at least 7 V above ground — so a rail measuring near 9 V is healthy, and one significantly lower points to the primary side failing to deliver enough power on that winding, and probably on the others too.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The same check applied to a repaired instrument means verifying that the ripple troughs on the raw rails stay well above the regulators' minimum dropout requirement, which some designs cut uncomfortably fine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup>

Dropout can be inferred in reverse from a known regulator. A board using 7815 and 7805 parts to generate ±15 V and ±5 V rails must have at least 2 V of dropout on the 15 V regulators, implying an unregulated supply of roughly ±18 V rather than ±12 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup> Keeping that raw rail no higher than necessary limits dissipation in the regulators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup>

The requirement persists in instruments with linear output stages. A bench source running its outputs in linear mode cannot swing to the maximum of its own input supply because some dropout is always consumed by the pass element — and at low output voltages and high current the resulting drop across that element becomes the dominant dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup>

Because dropout behaviour is a function of the specific silicon, a regulator carrying a new suffix on its part number is not interchangeable with the old one on faith: it may drop out differently or oscillate with a different combination of load and bypass capacitors, and requires re-qualification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup>

## Terminology boundaries

The term belongs to voltage regulators and does not transfer cleanly. Voltage references have an equivalent requirement — typically 2 V above the output for all variants, so 5.3 V in for a 3.3 V reference, 6 V for a 4.096 V part, and 7 V for a 5 V part, below which output regulation is lost — but reference datasheets generally do not use the phrase "dropout voltage", which is a trap when searching for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

The phrase also appears with a different meaning on DC electronic loads, where a settable dropout voltage is the cutoff at which the load stops discharging the cell under test — 2.6 V, for instance, on a coin cell discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup>
