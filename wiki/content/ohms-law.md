Ohm's law is the relationship between [[voltage]], [[current]], and [[resistance]] in a conductor: voltage equals current multiplied by resistance, and the three quantities have a direct influence on each other so that changing any one changes the others.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> It is named after George Ohm, who discovered it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> It is the single most-used piece of arithmetic in practical electronics — the tool for sizing a dropper [[resistor]], predicting a [[voltage-drop]] along a [[pcb-trace]], working out how much power a pass [[transistor]] must dissipate, and explaining why a measurement instrument disturbs the circuit it is measuring.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> Along with Kirchhoff's laws it is the foundation on which every other DC and AC analysis technique is built, and it holds in every practical circuit: "Ohm's law always must be obeyed."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup>

## Statement and forms

The three algebraic forms — V = I × R, I = V / R, and R = V / I — are rearrangements of one equation, and are conventionally memorised through the Ohm's law triangle, a visual device in which covering the quantity to be solved for leaves the correct expression of the other two.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> Given current and resistance, the voltage across a component is the product of the two; given resistance and voltage, the current through it is the voltage divided by the resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> A higher voltage across a given resistor forces more current through it than a lower voltage does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup> Ohm's law together with the power equations belongs to a small set of formulas that has to be committed to memory before any circuit work is possible at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>

Power follows immediately from it: P = V × I, and by substitution P = I² × R and P = V² / R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1009" title="EEVblog #1009 - DC Fundamentals Part 1: Voltage vs Power vs Energy">1009</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup> The squared forms are what make Ohm's law a thermal tool as much as an electrical one — a 1.6 V drop at 250 mA across a 6.4 Ω [[led]] dropper resistor works out to a quarter of a watt by I²R, and 3.66 V across a 5 Ω [[multimeter]] [[current-shunt]] is 2.5 W by V²/R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup>

## Resistance is everywhere

The resistance in the equation need not be a resistor. It can be the internal resistance of a [[battery]] or [[power-supply]], a PCB trace, a length of wiring, [[transformer]] winding resistance, or the effective output resistance of a logic gate's output [[mosfet]]; whenever current passes through any of them a voltage drop appears and power is dissipated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Copper on a [[pcb]] is a conductor with a non-zero resistance, so a trace carrying current drops voltage, which is one of the constraints on trace current rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1559" title="EEVblog 1559 - PCB Design: Trace Current Rating">1559</a></sup> Even a large [[ground-plane]] is not zero ohms: at eight amps flowing across a board, a measurable drop develops over the distance travelled, which is why lowering trace resistance is the standard remedy for excessive DC voltage drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Splitting a supply's bulk [[capacitance]] into three parallel electrolytics divides the current between three traces, and each carries a third of the current and therefore develops a smaller drop — a real consideration in high-power systems even with 1 oz copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

The same logic sets the scale of what can be ignored. Test leads of a milliohm or so drop essentially nothing into a 10 MΩ multimeter load, so remote sensing is unnecessary there while a heavy load would demand it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZYC763Vx9O8" title="eevBLAB #6 - Don&#x27;t Assume It&#x27;s Faulty">•</a></sup> A resistor that runs hot cannot be a kilohm-range part, because kilohms would require thousands of volts to dissipate appreciable power — visible overheating therefore identifies a low-value resistor before it is even measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> A transformer primary reading 0.2 Ω rather than the expected tens or hundreds of ohms implies a catastrophic power figure across [[mains-voltage]], and hence shorted turns with the enamel insulation burnt off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup>

A battery's internal resistance produces the difference between its loaded and unloaded terminal voltage, which is why an open-circuit measurement is meaningless for characterising discharge curves or cut-off voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-779" title="EEVblog #779 - Batteriser: How To Measure Battery Cutoff Voltage">779</a></sup>

## Consequence: zero volts means zero current

Because current through a resistance is proportional to the voltage across it, forcing the two ends of a resistor to the same potential guarantees no current, whatever the resistance value is. This is exploited directly in guard-ring design: a buffer amplifier actively drives the guard trace to the same voltage as the high-[[impedance]] input node, so no voltage difference exists across the board's surface leakage paths and no leakage current flows, regardless of contamination, fingerprint oils, or moisture on the PCB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> The same reasoning explains why an op-amp input bias current cannot flow through a resistor that has zero volts across it and must instead come entirely through the feedback resistor,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup> and why a current-limit control node with the same 10 V on both sides of its resistor passes zero current no matter how hard the load demands more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup>

## Design calculations

Series dropper resistors are the canonical application. For an LED string, the resistor value is the supply voltage minus the LED forward voltage, divided by the desired current: 1.6 V at 250 mA gives 6.4 Ω, rounded to the nearest E12 value of 6.8 Ω;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup> 3.3 V minus a 1.8 V LED at 20 mA gives 75 Ω;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> 4 V minus a 2 V LED across 100 Ω gives 20 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> Where the LED drop is an order of magnitude smaller than the supply it can simply be dropped from the calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> The extreme case is a 1 MΩ LED dropper in an electric fence controller, where ignoring the [[diode]] drop against 1,000 V yields a convenient rule of thumb of 1 mA per kilovolt, so a 20 mA LED maximum corresponds to 20 kV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup> The position of the [[series-resistor]] within a string is irrelevant to the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup>

Zener shunt regulator design is the same arithmetic twice over. The dropper value is the input-to-Zener voltage difference divided by the test current — 12 V in, 5.1 V out, 50 mA, giving 138 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Regulation performance then falls out of the Zener's dynamic resistance: raising the input to 15 V pushes the current to 71.7 mA, and the resulting current change of 21.7 mA across 5 Ω of dynamic resistance shifts the output by about 0.11 V, from 5.1 V to 5.21 V, roughly 2%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

Precision current sources are built by imposing a known voltage across a known resistor. A 1.25 V bandgap across 10 kΩ sets 125 µA;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-329" title="EEVblog #329 - Tracking Pre-Regulator LTspice Simulation Part 2">329</a></sup> a 1.25 V reference across a 1.25 Ω four-terminal precision resistor sets exactly 1 A;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> a 10 V reference lifted by an [[op-amp]] across a precision resistor gives a calculable load current, measured at 1.008 µA against prediction;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup> and 1 V across 1 Ω in an op-amp dummy load gives 1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> For an [[lm317]]-style regulator, the adjust-pin current flowing through the lower divider resistor adds a voltage-drop term to the output by Ohm's law, which is the origin of the I_adj × R2 term in the [[datasheet]] equation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

Small currents through large resistances are the dominant error source in precision analog design. A 100 pA input bias current through a 10 kΩ source resistance produces a 1 µV offset error;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup> 30 pA through a 100 kΩ resistor produces 3 µV, which the op amp's gain of 100 multiplies to 300 µV at the output;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-479" title="EEVblog #479 - Opamp Input Bias Current">479</a></sup> and 10 µA through a 2 kΩ divider resistor produces 20 mV, a 2% error when the intended output is 1 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> In each case the remedy begins with recognising which resistance the stray current is developing a voltage across.

Ohm's law also underpins the conversions between equivalent circuits: the Norton current of a Thevenin source is simply V_TH divided by R_TH, and the reverse conversion is V_TH = I_N × R_N.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Shorting a 10 V source through 10 kΩ gives 1 mA, and dividing the 5 V Thevenin voltage by that short-circuit current gives a 5 kΩ Thevenin resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup> Nodal analysis solves for node voltages, after which the branch currents are recovered by a single division — V_A divided by R2.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> Maximum power transfer is derived the same way, expressing load power as I²R with I calculated from the source voltage and the total series resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup>

## Instrumentation and measurement

Every ammeter inserts a shunt resistance into the circuit under test, and Ohm's law governs the resulting error. A 1 V source across 100 Ω should read 10 mA, but with a meter in series the total resistance becomes 100 Ω plus the shunt resistance R_S, so the reading is necessarily lower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> The voltage the shunt develops is the [[burden-voltage]]: 500 µA full scale through a 100 Ω shunt is 50 mV, the figure datasheets typically quote as maximum burden voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Shunt values are chosen to hold that figure constant across ranges — 10 mΩ at 5 A and 100 mΩ at 500 mA both give 50 mV, an order of magnitude less current against an order of magnitude more resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> The same relation sets the lower limit of usefulness: 5 mA through a 10 mΩ shunt is only 50 µV full scale, which is not enough signal to resolve, so a low current range cannot reuse the high-current shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> More generally, 1 mV per amp requires 1 mΩ, 1 mV per milliamp requires 1 Ω, 1 mV per microamp requires 1 kΩ, and 1 mV per nanoamp requires 1 MΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> Burden voltage is the reason a battery-life measurement setup cannot simply trust the supply voltage: the external meter's shunt drops voltage that must be accounted for at the device under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup>

A voltmeter's [[input-impedance]] is a resistor too, and Ohm's law converts it between the voltage and current domains. A 10 MΩ input in series with a reverse-biased Schottky diode carrying 473 nA — calculated as 4.73 V divided by 10 MΩ — is enough to invalidate a reverse-leakage measurement; at 2.2 µA the resistor would need 22 V across it, so a 5 V supply simply clamps the reading at 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Run in reverse, the same calculation converts a measured leakage current into an equivalent resistance: 10 µA at 100 V is 10 MΩ, and a worst-case 5 µA at 1000 V corresponds to 200 MΩ of MOV leakage, high enough not to disturb a 10 MΩ input divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup> A femtoammeter's minimum full-scale range follows the same product: a 10 GΩ feedback resistor at 20 pA full scale gives 0.2 V, a 200 mV full-scale front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

Where a four-terminal ohmmeter is unavailable, the measurement can be reconstructed from Ohm's law using two multimeters, one reading voltage and one reading current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> [[input-protection]] design uses it in the other direction: clamping diodes limit the voltage across the current shunt, and the maximum power in that shunt during the time the [[fuse]] takes to blow is then calculable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The output resistance of a logic output is obtained the same way, from a 0.2 V drop at 5.2 mA of output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

## AC form

Ohm's law applies to AC exactly as it does to DC, with the substitution of impedance Z for resistance R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> Impedance is measured in ohms just as DC resistance is, and the AC equation states that impedance equals AC voltage divided by AC current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Strictly the DC form applies at a single instant in time, since both voltage and current are changing; in practice the instantaneous formulation can be set aside and the law applied directly to maxima or to RMS values, since I_max = V_max / R and I_RMS = V_RMS / R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> Resistors are linear and introduce no phase shift, so their current-voltage relationship is unaffected by the change to AC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup>

For reactive components the resistance term becomes the reactance: inductive reactance X_L is ωL, or 2πfL, and an [[inductor]] can be treated exactly like a resistor if the phase relationship is not of interest, giving I_max = V_max / ωL.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> Capacitive reactance X_C is 1/ωC, carrying a −90° angle against the inductor's +90°, for pure components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1470" title="EEVblog 1470 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 3 - Complex Numbers are EASY!">1470</a></sup> Once phase is retained, voltages, currents, and impedances all acquire real and imaginary parts, expressed in rectangular or polar form, and the law itself is unchanged — only the arithmetic becomes complex rather than real.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1470" title="EEVblog 1470 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 3 - Complex Numbers are EASY!">1470</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup> Solving an AC circuit is therefore the same procedure as solving a DC one: total the impedances and divide the source voltage by the result, as with a total impedance of 10 − j75 Ω where the current is the voltage divided by that impedance, most easily handled in polar form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1661" title="EEVblog 1661 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 5: Time Domain vs Frequency Domain">1661</a></sup> Every DC technique — Kirchhoff's laws, nodal and mesh analysis, superposition — carries over intact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1729" title="EEVblog 1729 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 7: AC Ohms Law">1729</a></sup> For a purely resistive AC load the power equations also survive unchanged as P = I²R and P = V²/R.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1730" title="EEVblog 1730 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 8: Apparent, Reactive &amp; Real Power">1730</a></sup>

## Thermal analogy

Thermal design is mathematically identical to DC circuit theory, with power playing the role of current, [[thermal-resistance]] the role of resistance, and temperature the role of voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> The case temperature rise of a transistor is therefore the dissipated power multiplied by the case thermal resistance, and temperature accumulates stage by stage from ambient up to the junction, which might be rated for an absolute maximum of around 120 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Understanding Ohm's law and series circuit theory is essentially sufficient for the whole of practical [[heat-sink|heatsink]] design, which in most cases needs no more than back-of-the-envelope calculation followed by real measurements and a safety factor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

## Limits and misapplications

The law describes linear resistances, and several common situations are not linear. A [[short-circuit]] measured at 0.11 Ω with a milliamp-level ohmmeter should not be expected to match Ohm's law at 10 or 11 amps, because the short is non-linear, has a [[temperature-coefficient]], and heats up under load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> A PTC thermistor is deliberately non-linear for the same reason: a 2.25 kΩ device across 240 V mains would dissipate an unacceptable amount of power if its resistance stayed put, so the resistance must rise with temperature while remaining at its nominal value at low voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> Battery discharge cannot be modelled by Ohm's law either, because the terminal voltage falls throughout the discharge; a product containing a DC-DC converter behaves as a constant-power load and must be assessed against constant-power discharge curves rather than constant-current ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> Where a supply's [[current-limit]] interacts with an over-voltage condition, the interaction is again a simple division: 3.3 V into a 2 Ω load exceeds a 1 A limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup>

Because the law is inescapable, it also functions as a falsification tool. Free-energy and over-unity claims collapse under a couple of simple calculations against datasheet values and nothing more complicated than Ohm's law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> A wireless power demonstration showing 0.52 V across 100 Ω delivers about 5.2 mA, not the 52 mA claimed;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1408" title="EEVblog 1408 - $5M WiGL Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1408</a></sup> and a stated smartphone charging requirement of 4.2 V at 62 mA is 260 mW, against the 26 mW the same document admits to receiving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MCyLO-1grEk" title="WiGL Wireless Power BUSTED AGAIN!">•</a></sup> Practical engineering has built up Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, maximum power transfer, transmission line theory, and signal theory precisely to give usable insight above the level of the underlying physics, and none of it can be evaded — if energy did not flow through the conductor, megawatts could be carried on 30 gauge wire, which they cannot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup>

## Place in practice

Ohm's law is the entry point of the discipline and, in Dave Jones's assessment, close to its practical ceiling as well: everyday engineering work rarely requires mathematics much above Ohm's law, with the calculus taught at university going largely unused outside specialised roles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> Troubleshooting in particular depends on it, alongside Kirchhoff's current law, basic building-block circuits, and concepts such as power supply decoupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zyuRcsM0gjI" title="eevBLAB #10 - Why Learn Basic Electronics?">•</a></sup> The scope is wide enough that even a mousetrap trigger reduces to a resistor and a [[voltage-source]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> a heating element that measures good will heat up when voltage is switched across it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> a resistor cube can be attacked with Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law before symmetry simplifies it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup> and a [[microcontroller]] doing Ohm's law arithmetic on the fly can turn an [[electronic-load]] into a constant-resistance or constant-power load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup>
