Startup funding is the capital a hardware company raises to carry it from an idea to a shippable product, and in deep tech it is a structural constraint on engineering rather than a background financial detail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> Because a raise buys a fixed span of time rather than a fixed quantity of work, the funding cycle sets the schedule that the technical program must fit inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> The amounts involved range from trivial to enormous: a two-way radio repair business was started with a founder's savings of a few hundred dollars,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fK2KBDo7ISY" title="Dick Smith - Amateur Radio &amp; Adventure">•</a></sup> while an ultrasonic wireless charging venture consumed roughly $30 million over five years without arriving at a product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rLly4WdRAjY" title="More uBeam Ultrasonic Wireless Charging LOL&#x27;s!">•</a></sup>

## The eighteen-month clock

The standard shape of a hardware raise is a fixed runway: "You get cash in the bank and you have 18 months before you're out of money."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> Raising the following round is not instantaneous — it typically absorbs three to six months of that window — so the effective period of undisturbed development is materially shorter than the runway itself, and the founders must begin the next raise while the [[current]] work is still in progress.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> This is the ordinary case, not a failure case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup>

The same eighteen-month figure appears on the spending side of offering documents. In one wireless power offering, "60% of the maximum offering, $5 million is earmarked to go to salary for the next 18 months."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1408" title="EEVblog 1408 - $5M WiGL Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1408</a></sup> A majority of raised capital going to salaries over a runway-length period says that the money is buying staff time rather than tooling, certification, or production, which is a legible fact about what the company expects to have at the end of the period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1408" title="EEVblog 1408 - $5M WiGL Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1408</a></sup>

## What investors are buying

At the pre-product stage there is no product to evaluate, so the assessment falls on the team and its grasp of the problem. One founder who raised a total of $3 million attributed the outcome to having the right people around the company, an understanding of the problem space, and the aptitude and keenness to execute — an aptitude judgment more than a credential one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> The years of prior research in the area functioned as evidence of that understanding rather than as a deliverable in itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup>

Funding also comes in stages matched to how much of the technical risk has been retired. A working minimum viable product with no manufacturing path behind it is a pre-seed proposition, sought specifically to get units made at volume, and may be pursued in parallel with a licensing route to industry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> A company that already ships one product may go looking for investors separately for an adjacent industrial variant rather than returning to the same crowd-funding audience.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-657" title="EEVblog #657 - Maker Faire 2014 Interviews">657</a></sup>

## Funding as a diagnostic

Because raising money forces a company to make claims in public, the funding activity itself carries information about the underlying technology. A venture soliciting funding for an energy technology while presenting no prototype photographs and no data at all is describing an idea, not a result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup> The inference runs the other way as well: technology of the claimed significance would attract capital on the strength of published data, so the absence of data alongside an active raise is evidence against the claim rather than merely a gap in disclosure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup> The characteristic substitute is marketing framed in multiples — some large factor more energy dense than the incumbent — in place of measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup>

Sustained spending without convergence is a related signal. A company still recruiting for foundational technical roles after five years and $30 million has not passed the stage its funding history implies it should have.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rLly4WdRAjY" title="More uBeam Ultrasonic Wireless Charging LOL&#x27;s!">•</a></sup>

## Money does not remove the schedule risk

Funding buys time but does not buy delivery. A 360-degree camera project that raised $1.5 million still ran two to three months behind schedule and shipped developer prototypes in rough form, with [[firmware]] watermarking the output and messaging that the quality was not final.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MtRU8WPGlss" title="Giroptic 360cam Kickstarter Prototype Test Footage">•</a></sup> Late and imperfect early hardware is the normal condition of a funded hardware program, distinct from the pattern of a company that has funding and nothing to show.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MtRU8WPGlss" title="Giroptic 360cam Kickstarter Prototype Test Footage">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup>

## The bootstrapped alternative

Not all hardware companies begin with an external raise. Dick Smith Electronics was founded on personal savings accumulated over four or five months: "we started Dick Smith Electronics with $610", contributed $600 and $10 by the two founders.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fK2KBDo7ISY" title="Dick Smith - Amateur Radio &amp; Adventure">•</a></sup> The initial business was servicing two-way radios in taxis — a service operation generating revenue from the first job, which is a fundamentally different capital profile from a deep-tech program that must fund years of development before any revenue exists.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fK2KBDo7ISY" title="Dick Smith - Amateur Radio &amp; Adventure">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup>
