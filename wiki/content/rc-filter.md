An RC filter is a [[resistor]] and a [[capacitor]] arranged to attenuate signals above (or below) a chosen [[frequency]], and in its low-pass form it is the simplest way to strip [[ripple]] or [[switching-noise|switching noise]] off a node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> It is a first-order, single-pole filter: one resistor, one capacitor, and a cutoff frequency given by 1/2πRC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Its ubiquity comes from that simplicity — it is a building block that appears on its own, in cascades, and embedded inside larger topologies such as the [[capacitance]] multiplier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

## Cutoff frequency and roll-off

The −3 dB point of an RC filter is 1/2πRC, and the same formula holds whether the filter stands alone or is buried inside a more elaborate circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> A 10 kΩ resistor with a 100 nF capacitor gives a nominal cutoff of 159 Hz; 1 kΩ with 100 nF gives 1.59 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Component tolerance sets how closely a measured response matches the calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

The cutoff is not a brick wall.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Above it, attenuation increases gradually, which is why a single pole placed at 159 Hz does a poor job of removing a 10 kHz interferer — the separation looks large but the first-order roll-off is too shallow to be effective.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Response is conventionally examined on a logarithmic frequency axis in decades rather than a linear one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Adding poles steepens the roll-off: beyond the single-pole RC there are two-, three-, and four-pole configurations, and active arrangements such as Sallen-Key when an op-amp is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

## Converting PWM to a DC voltage

A pulse-width modulated output from a [[microcontroller]] carries no useful DC information on its own — it is a digital signal swinging between the rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Passed through a low-pass RC filter, it averages to a DC [[voltage]] linearly proportional to the [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]]: a 5 V microcontroller at 10% duty produces 0.5 V, and the full 0–100% duty range maps to 0–5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> The same principle scales to other rails; a 10% duty cycle on a 1 V peak-to-peak signal settles at exactly 100 mV average.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> This makes an RC filter a substitute for a DAC in circuits that need only a slowly varying setpoint, feeding an op-amp wired as a voltage follower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup>

The averaged output is never pure DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Residual ripple depends on the filter values relative to the [[pwm|PWM]] frequency, and a useful design target is to keep it below one LSB of the controlling resolution — for a 12-bit system, under 1.22 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A buffer is required between the filter and the load: an RC-filtered PWM output is not adequate to drive a control input directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Where the filtered PWM sets a supply's output, any [[noise]] not removed by the filter passes through the control loop and appears on the output, so filtering of the modulated signal becomes the dominant noise limit — more so than driving the same pin from a quiet [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] and a pot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup>

## Cascading stages and the response-time trade-off

Increasing R and C to buy more attenuation lengthens the settling time, since the [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]] also governs how fast the output tracks a change in duty cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A first-order 10 kΩ / 100 nF filter fed with a 10 kHz PWM signal leaves roughly 100 mV of ripple on the setpoint and about 10 mV on the supply output — inadequate for a supply asked to deliver 100 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

Cascading two identical RC sections in series is the cheap way out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> A second 10 kΩ / 100 nF stage drops the ripple to the order of 0.1–0.2 mV — hundreds of microvolts — while keeping the response time near 10 ms, which raising the values of a single stage could not do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup> Cascading is preferable to simply enlarging one stage precisely because it improves attenuation without the corresponding sluggishness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

## Ripple rejection on power rails

For removing supply ripple, the larger the capacitance for a given resistor, the greater the attenuation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The limit is [[current]]: a resistor large enough to filter well drops too much voltage, and a plain RC network is only workable at very low currents — on the order of 10 mA — after which the required capacitance climbs to absurd values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Adding a second RC stage improves rejection but doubles the [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] for the same load current, so a multi-stage RC is still ineffective for anything but small currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The case where it works cleanly is precisely the low-current one, such as smoothing a PWM-derived control voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup>

The capacitance multiplier addresses the [[current-limit|current limit]] by combining an RC filter with a series pass emitter follower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The [[transistor]]'s current gain means the resistor and capacitor handle roughly 1/β of the load current — a couple of hundred microamps instead of tens of milliamps for a β of 100 — giving an effective capacitance of C times beta.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> The name is contested because the cutoff frequency still follows 1/2πRC exactly as for a bare RC filter; what is multiplied is not the filtering capacitance but the current the stage can pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> Post-regulation of a noisy converter output can likewise be done with RC or LC filtering as an alternative to a low-dropout regulator or a Zener.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

## Other roles

An RC network on a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] gate slows the edge enough to swamp mechanical switch bounce, which is a practical fix in power-supply sequencing when a physical switch would otherwise make the FET turn on and off rapidly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-995" title="EEVblog #995 - Power Supply Sequencing">995</a></sup> Series [[resistance]] ahead of a filter capacitor also limits input current into a chip, so an RC at an input serves as filtering and protection at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> An RC network lets a microcontroller's on-chip ADC sample a slowly varying quantity such as [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] during charging, even where that converter is only 4 bits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-284" title="EEVblog #284 - Braun Toothbrush Teardown">284</a></sup> Simple tone controls have been implemented as nothing more than an RC filter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup> Fed a [[square-wave|square wave]], an RC filter produces an approximation of a [[sine-wave|sine wave]], with fidelity improving as the filter improves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> At cryogenic temperatures, boxes of RC and LC filters both thermalise the wiring and strip high-frequency radiation, whose photons carry energy proportional to frequency [[via]] the Planck-Einstein relation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup>

Because the topology is so recognisable, an unidentified part sitting behind a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] and a capacitor can be read as part of an RC filter configuration during board [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]], narrowing the candidates to something on a filtered rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1541" title="EEVblog1541 - What is this Blown SMD Component? Redux">1541</a></sup> Conversely, RC snubbing networks omitted from a low-cost design are a visible sign of cost-cutting on drive circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup>

## Measurement

An RC filter is the standard first test case for frequency-response instrumentation, being trivial to build and having a response that can be predicted exactly and cross-checked against a simulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> A 2.2 nF capacitor with a decade resistance box set to 10 kΩ is enough to characterise a Bode plotting setup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> Gain-phase analysis on a network analyser treats such a filter as the device under test, measuring input on one channel and output on another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup> On instruments whose own response is flat to within thousandths of a [[decibel]], inserting an RC filter is what makes any response visible at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup>
