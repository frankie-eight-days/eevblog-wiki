The term *thermal pad* covers two distinct things that both exist to move heat out of a component. The first is a feature of the semiconductor package itself: an exposed metal pad on the underside (occasionally the top) of a surface-mount chip, soldered to copper on the board so the die's heat escapes downward rather than through the leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> The second is a compliant sheet of thermally conductive material — commonly known by the trademarked name Sil-Pad — placed between a hot device and a [[heat-sink|heatsink]] or metal chassis to bridge the microscopic air gaps between two imperfect surfaces while keeping them electrically isolated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Whether a package carries a thermal pad at all is one of the fastest ways to judge how much power a circuit was designed to dissipate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1263" title="EEVblog #1263 - Mailbag">1263</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>

## The package thermal pad

Modern QFN, MLF, MSOP and power SO packages route heat out of the die through a large metal slug on the bottom of the body. A 12-pin MLF measuring only 3 mm by 3 mm carries such a pad, alongside a DC-to-DC converter die that would otherwise have nowhere to send its dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> The same construction appears on an MSOP-8 buck converter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup> on automotive DC-to-DC controllers in packages with power tabs,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-958" title="EEVblog #958 - Mailbag">958</a></sup> and on custom ASICs where the bottom pad is the only meaningful heat path off the die.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup>

The pad is useless on its own — it has to be coupled through the board. The standard arrangement is an array of thermal vias directly under the pad, tying it to copper flood on the opposite side: nine vias under a stepper driver on a 3D printer control board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup> "dozens and dozens" scale arrays on [[oscilloscope]] acquisition chips,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup> and exposed copper with heavy [[via-stitching|via stitching]] visible on the underside of a [[function-generator|function generator]]'s output amplifiers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> Where the exposed copper and [[via]] field are visible on the back of a board, the chip on the other side almost certainly has a thermal pad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup> Semiconductor datasheets normally supply the recommended footprint, via pattern and the calculations behind them, and following that example layout is the intended design route.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-805" title="EEVBlog #805 - Siglent SDG2122X Arb Generator Teardown">805</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-407" title="EEVblog #407 - Mailbag">407</a></sup>

Because the pad is usually tied to [[ground]], it becomes a large ground connection as well as a thermal one, and pins can short to it during assembly — a continuity check from every pin to the pad is a sensible post-reflow step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> Some parts ship in variants that differ only in which face the pad is on: the STA350BW carries its pad on the bottom, while a sibling part places it on top, and the choice depends entirely on which heatsinking scheme the board uses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> An SO-8 with no pad at the bottom is, by construction, not intended to dissipate meaningful power; a package nominally rated for several watts will run extremely hot if actually asked to do so.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> The absence of pads on the underside of a board is therefore evidence that the design is low-power,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1263" title="EEVblog #1263 - Mailbag">1263</a></sup> and a board whose devices lack back-side pads can only shed heat through the pins and the copper traces attached to them — which fails badly when those traces are thin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup>

## Soldering and desoldering pad packages

The thermal pad complicates both assembly and rework. [[solder-paste|Solder paste]] is the practical route, because getting [[solder]] underneath the pad with an iron first leaves a lumpy layer that has to melt into place afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> With drag [[soldering]] and hot air, solder reflowing visibly on the pins is the indicator that heat has already travelled through the bottom pad and the part is attached — there is no need to wait for anything further.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup> Under hot air the pad reflows along with the pins, and packages are built to survive oven profiles of a couple of hundred degrees for an extended period, so overheating during hand reflow is not the main risk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup>

Misalignment is. Surface tension at the large pad can overwhelm the self-alignment that the fine pins would otherwise provide, pulling the chip visibly off-centre or lifting it, and a badly proportioned pad on the layout makes this worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Removal is correspondingly difficult: a QFN dissipating only about a watt still needs a large tip, higher temperature and more [[airflow]] to release, and tilting the board on its side can be what finally frees it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-E1IqcGcZKHE" title="RPi CM4 Compute Module FAILURE Part 2: Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Done properly, the whole package lifts with minimal heat applied to the surrounding pads, leaving neighbouring components essentially untouched — often a better technique than heating the entire area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup>

## The interface pad

Two nominally flat mating surfaces — the back of a power [[transistor]] and a heatsink face — contact only at microscopic high points, and the trapped air between them adds [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] to the path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> The two remedies are thermal compound, which fills the gaps as a paste, and a silicone rubber interface pad, which does the same job as a solid sheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Its defining advantage is that "you don't have to add heat sink compound" — the pad alone is the interface, and adding grease on top of one is redundant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> The pad also provides [[electrical-isolation|electrical isolation]], which is why heatsinks carrying multiple devices at different potentials use one: it transfers the heat while keeping the metalwork off the live tabs, and it removes the need for insulating bushes around the mounting screws.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-644" title="EEVblog #644 - How To Design Front Panels On Extruded Enclosures - µSupply Part 14">644</a></sup>

That isolation is not free. The pad is one more thermal resistance in a chain that already includes junction-to-case, the vias, and any heat transfer bar, and the design goal is to keep as few elements in series as possible — going straight from device case to heatsink, when the topology allows it, gives a substantially lower [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> In a marginal design the stack of junction-to-case plus vias plus the insulating pad can push a [[diode]] close to its 150 °C maximum junction temperature with no forced airflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

Pad quality matters as much as its presence. Bubbles and voids under a pad leave air gaps that defeat the point; the material conforms to surface irregularities but not completely, so a flat mating surface is still worth having.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> [[adhesive|Adhesive]]-backed pads are the usual answer for small [[pcb|PCB]]-mount heatsinks that have no mechanical attachment provision at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-940" title="EEVblog #940 - Mailbag">940</a></sup> Clamping is preferred where possible — a clip or secondary plate pressing the device against the pad is common in high-quality equipment, and some designs dispense with per-device mounting hardware entirely in favour of pads plus a pressing plate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix MDO4000 Oscilloscope Teardown - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup>

## System-level use

Interface pads are the mechanism by which a chassis becomes a heatsink. Instrument and appliance designs commonly mould or machine the metalwork so that raised bosses come down onto each heat-generating package, with pads engineered to the right thickness to close the remaining gap — a spectrum analyser's aluminium plate is shaped so every major dissipating package lines up with a pad,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> and the same approach appears in an early HD DVD player where the metal is depressed specifically to reach down onto the chips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1011" title="EEVblog #1011 - Retro Teardown - World&#x27;s First HD DVD Player">1011</a></sup> A fanless rugged laptop uses its magnesium alloy shell the same way,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-925" title="EEVblog #925 - Panasonic CF-U1 ToughBook Teardown">925</a></sup> and a TO-220 device can simply be wedged between the board and the case bottom with a pad for insulation, making the extrusion itself the heatsink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-644" title="EEVblog #644 - How To Design Front Panels On Extruded Enclosures - µSupply Part 14">644</a></sup> Where a package is on top of the board, a pad and a copper plate can still route heat down to the base of the enclosure; placing the [[processor]] on the underside of the board with a pad to the base is the more direct arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-734" title="EEVblog #734 - Giroptic 360cam Kickstarter Prototype">734</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-914" title="EEVblog #914 - Sony VAIO UX Micro PC Teardown">914</a></sup>

Scale ranges from a single chip to an entire assembly: one isolated oscilloscope design uses a single large pad covering the whole board, drawing heat out of a 12-layer stack with heavy copper that spreads dissipation from an ADC running at about 1.5 W and a 500 MHz clock generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1753" title="EEVblog 1753 - Designing a 2000V Isolated Oscilloscope (Cleverscope)">1753</a></sup> At the other end, M.2 SSDs and memory modules routinely ship with a small pad under a thin heatsink,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zNG_oNhKCBg" title="Lenovo Legion 5 Laptop M.2 SSD Memory">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MiqxUaWkGYY" title="AMD Ryzen 5900X Upgrade S**T SHOW">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup> and Raspberry Pi cases include pads to couple the processor and regulator to the shell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup>

The pad only works if the far side of it is actually a heatsink. In a plastic-cased [[power-supply|power supply]], pads over the switching MOSFETs help marginally but cannot function as a dissipation path with no metal backing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> Mechanical arrangement constrains the approach as well: a slide-on enclosure makes it hard to establish good contact between an internal heatsink block and the case, which is achievable with a screw-down case but not a sliding one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup>

## Failure and service considerations

Pads are a recurring nuisance during repair. They stick to whichever surface they were pressed against, so disassembly frequently leaves the pad adhered to the metal plate rather than the chip, and a pad that has come loose from a device suspected of overheating is itself a possible cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1154" title="EEVblog #1154 - Surprising 4K Dumpster TV Fault">1154</a></sup> Pads tear when a heatsink is lifted,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> go crusty with age and heat,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ij6r6uXr2Mo" title="Flashing the eMMC on a Raspberry Pi CM4 Compute Module">•</a></sup> and in dense camera assemblies must be peeled back to reach the components beneath and then pressed carefully back down onto the die during reassembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1433" title="EEVblog 1433 - More Adventures in Sony RX100 Repair (Part 3)">1433</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1428" title="EEVblog 1428 - Sony RX100 IV Camera Repair and then WTF!">1428</a></sup> Their one service virtue is cleanliness: a heatsink mounted on pads rather than compound comes apart without any goop to clean up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup>
