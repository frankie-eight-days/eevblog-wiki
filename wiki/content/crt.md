A cathode ray tube (CRT) is an evacuated glass tube in which a beam of electrons is accelerated toward a phosphor screen and steered across it to draw an image.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> For most of the twentieth century it was the only practical display for televisions, oscilloscopes, computer terminals and instrument front panels, and it remains the defining component of vintage test gear: the part that sets the depth of the enclosure, carries the lethal voltages, and is usually the first thing to fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WHwf26JCUec" title="Tektronix TDS540D Repair Part 1">•</a></sup> The underlying insight — that a picture can be built from rows of varying brightness rather than from independently addressed dots, with the beam steered by electromagnets and its intensity set by a [[voltage]] — is what turned the tube into television.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1577" title="EEVblog 1577 - History of Television &amp; Cathode Ray Tubes with Kathy Loves Physics">1577</a></sup>

## Construction and operation

The tube is evacuated, with a screen at the front and a narrow neck at the rear carrying the electron gun and the deflection assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup> Early [[oscilloscope]] tubes used a round faceplate; later ones adopted a rectangular front that still tapers back into a round neck.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup> Drive electronics sit on a small neck board pushed onto the base pins, with the filament driver and associated circuitry mounted directly on the back of the tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-839" title="EEVblog #839 - Mailbag">839</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup> In a television the beam is steered by a deflection yoke and driven from a line output [[transformer]], with the video path handled by a single integrated jungle chip feeding a three-[[transistor]] driver on the neck board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1246" title="EEVblog #1246 - Dumpster TV to Retro RGB Monitor Conversion">1246</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup>

The screen face carries a positive charge in order to attract the electrons bombarding it from the rear, which is also why the front of a CRT collects dust.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Uk5F3rQNUkU" title="ESD Explained &amp; Demonstrated + History of the Pink Polyethylene ESD Bag">•</a></sup>

Deflection comes in two forms, and the choice is fundamental rather than cosmetic. Electromagnetic deflection uses coils around the neck; electrostatic deflection uses plates inside the envelope. The Sony WatchCam pocket monitor uses a full electromagnetic deflection tube, identifiable by its coil windings, in contrast to the electrostatic Sinclair flat tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1033" title="EEVblog #1033 - Sony WatchCam Pocket Flat CRT Monitor Teardown">1033</a></sup> Small portable televisions and the flat Sinclair design differ not only in deflection but in acceleration technology as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup>

## High voltage

The anode supply is the dangerous part of any CRT-based instrument. A Tektronix 2225 oscilloscope runs 7 kV on the anode, generated by a voltage multiplier fed from the mains board and covered by a plastic shield and warning labels; the anode lead is routed across the chassis under a plastic retaining clip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> A Fluke PM3370B combiscope uses an EHT driver transformer feeding a tripler that takes roughly 5 kV to 14 kV for the tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup> In televisions the high voltage comes off the line output transformer and reaches the tube through the anode cap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup> Sinclair's flat pocket television is at the other extreme, with an EHT of 2.4 kV produced by a ZTX655 and a multiplier chain, and 1,440 V on the repeller electrode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-553" title="EEVblog #553 - Mailbag">553</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-554" title="EEVblog #554 - Sinclair FTV1 TV80 Flat Screen Pocket TV Teardown">554</a></sup>

These supplies remain charged after power is removed, so instruments must be treated as live even when switched off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup> The rule applies to miniature tubes too: a camcorder viewfinder CRT does not store the energy a full-size monitor or television does, but the same high-voltage precautions still apply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-380" title="EEVblog #380 - Sony CCD Sensor Teardown">380</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope tubes

In an [[analog-oscilloscope|analog oscilloscope]] the beam sweeps across the screen from left to right while the vertical amplifier deflects it in Y, so the trace is drawn continuously rather than stored.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup> A [[digital-oscilloscope|digital oscilloscope]] performs the same function with stored samples; in manual operation the two behave identically, and the claim that analog instruments are harder to use does not survive comparison with a well-laid-out analog front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup> An old second-hand analog CRO remains the recommended first oscilloscope for a beginner, with a 20 MHz dual-channel instrument sufficient for hobby work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-86" title="EEVblog #86 - Buy a real Analog Oscilloscope PLEASE!">86</a></sup>

CRTs could also render text. The Tektronix 213 drives its readout through a custom CRT character generator [[rom|ROM]] whose X, Y and Z axis outputs are switched to the tube only in [[multimeter]] mode and disabled in scope mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup> Later Tektronix instruments print power-on self-test results as text on the CRT, which makes diagnosis awkward when the tube itself is the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> High-end analog scopes provide separate intensity controls for the [[waveform]] and the readout, along with waveform focus, beam find and scale illumination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup> Some tubes carry a removable contrast filter on the front; a 2465B missing that filter still works normally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup> Vectorscopes of the period exposed astigmatism and geometry adjustments on the rear panel as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-756" title="EEVblog #756 - Mailbag">756</a></sup>

The tube also sets the size floor for a portable instrument. In the Tektronix 213 the surrounding circuitry could be shrunk with modern parts but the CRT could not, so a redesign would not be substantially smaller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-628" title="EEVblog #628 - Tektronix 213 Vintage Portable Oscilloscope Teardown">628</a></sup>

## Geometry and image faults

Dark rounding in the corners of a CRT image — vignetting — is not caused by a circular mask over a rectangular screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup> It occurs when the deflection coil sits too far back along the neck, so the beam at extreme deflection angles clips the inside of the tube; sliding the coil forward to the base of the neck restores a full test pattern, though the coil's rotation must then be reset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup> Apparent pulsing of screen brightness is an artifact of the scan update rate interacting with the camera, and shutter-priority exposure avoids it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup> The mesh over the front panel of such instruments is a screening mesh that stops CRT scanning frequencies escaping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-524" title="EEVblog #524 - Vignetting on a Cathode Ray Tube">524</a></sup>

Geometry can also be trimmed magnetically. A small magnet fitted at the factory on the WatchCam board adjusts the image, but the effect is slight — it expands the right-hand side a little — which argues against the tubes being individually matched to their boards on the production line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-q1CaC_Ezt-c" title="Sony WatchCam CRT Geometry Adjustment">•</a></sup> External neodymium magnets warp the raster dramatically enough to animate the image.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Z6yLBqc5SCc" title="Sony WatchCam CRT Fun With Neodymium Magnets">•</a></sup>

CRTs need a warm-up period before reaching full brightness, so a vintage machine that appears dead on power-up may simply not have come up yet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup>

## Miniature and flat tubes

Very small CRTs were produced in quantity for camcorder viewfinders and pocket televisions. A Sony electronic viewfinder tube is a conventional CRT in miniature, its assembly lifting straight out of the housing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-380" title="EEVblog #380 - Sony CCD Sensor Teardown">380</a></sup> Broadcast-style shoulder cameras used a CRT viewfinder rather than an [[lcd|LCD]], with a tally light on the front to show the subject that recording is under way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1391" title="EEVblog 1391 - Mailbag">1391</a></sup> Portable televisions of the mid-1980s used some of the smallest monochrome tubes available, packaged with a UHF/VHF tuner and [[battery]] operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-830" title="EEVblog #830 - Portable CRT TV Retro Teardown">830</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup> Small battery-powered oscilloscopes were built around miniature tubes as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-416" title="EEVblog #416 - 35 Years Of Test Equipment">416</a></sup>

The Sinclair FTV1 flat-screen pocket television is the notable exception to the conventional geometry: a flat CRT rather than an LCD, and by most accounts the only flat-screen television built around a cathode ray tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-554" title="EEVblog #554 - Sinclair FTV1 TV80 Flat Screen Pocket TV Teardown">554</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-553" title="EEVblog #553 - Mailbag">553</a></sup> Its deflection plates are labelled F and L for frame and line rather than X and Y, and it carries three control grids fed from an on-board video amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-554" title="EEVblog #554 - Sinclair FTV1 TV80 Flat Screen Pocket TV Teardown">554</a></sup> Because the beam strikes the screen obliquely, the tube cannot produce as sharp a spot as a conventional CRT, and a Fresnel lens on the front corrects the viewing geometry; the widescreen format reduces how far the beam must be deflected, trading physical size against drive power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-554" title="EEVblog #554 - Sinclair FTV1 TV80 Flat Screen Pocket TV Teardown">554</a></sup> In some units the envelope is transparent, exposing the plates directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-553" title="EEVblog #553 - Mailbag">553</a></sup>

The same beam-steering principle scales in the other direction: an electron beam lithography writer is functionally a CRT with a spot size of 2 nanometres, rastered across a chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup>

## Service and repair

CRTs dominate the mechanical design of the instruments that contain them. In a Tektronix TDS540D the tube must come at least half way out before the main board can be lifted from the chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WHwf26JCUec" title="Tektronix TDS540D Repair Part 1">•</a></sup> In a Tektronix 2225 the vertical gain adjustment pots — R145 and its channel-two counterpart — are located on the main board underneath the CRT.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> In an HP 9845B a board extends the full height of the machine to reach the tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1404" title="EEVblog 1404 - The Amazing HP 9845B Computer">1404</a></sup> A Macintosh SE's tube is attached to the front bezel, so the rear case lifts away from it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-414" title="EEVblog #414 - Apple Macintosh SE Teardown">414</a></sup>

Mounting arrangements vary in sophistication. The Osborne 1 rigidly mounts its 5-inch NEC tube to the inner plastic, leaving the outer shell to provide the shock absorption, which is cheap and effective without a dedicated spring mount.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup> An HP 85 uses a welded aluminium bracket running the full depth of the case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-903" title="EEVblog #903 - HP85 Vintage Computer Teardown">903</a></sup> Placing a tube very close to a board below it, as in the Stanford Research SR430, is poor practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup>

Faults cluster in a few places. A neck board can work loose from the tube base entirely, which is enough to kill the display and is repaired by pushing it back on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup> CRT driver boards fail, sometimes with visible fire damage, while the [[power-supply|power supply]] and digital sections continue to work normally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WHwf26JCUec" title="Tektronix TDS540D Repair Part 1">•</a></sup> A dead CRT can also be secondary: on a Compaq Portable the negative rails feeding the display do nothing until the power supply is repaired, so the tube cannot be tested at all beforehand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1354" title="EEVblog #1354 - Compaq Portable Repair - Part 2">1354</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> A raster that is present but extremely faint and compressed indicates a tube or drive problem rather than a supply failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9viKKRFhQog" title="OLD VIDEO: EXCLUSIVE: Scope Assembly &amp; Lab Move Update">•</a></sup> Residual dark fringing at the corners can persist after other faults are cleared and is not affected by the rotational position of the deflection components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup>

## Replacement and obsolescence

Where an instrument provides a video output, replacing a failed tube with a small [[lcd-display|LCD panel]] is a practical alternative to repairing the CRT drive. A TDS540D with a dead CRT still works fully through its VGA output, making a 6- or 7-inch VGA-compatible panel a viable substitute that is lighter, lower power and more reliable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1420" title="EEVblog 1420 - Mailbag">1420</a></sup> Working purely from an external monitor is possible but awkward, because the softkey labels no longer line up with the front-panel buttons.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1354" title="EEVblog #1354 - Compaq Portable Repair - Part 2">1354</a></sup> Video hardware of the period frequently supported both paths from one chipset, as with the Chips and Technologies 65545, which could drive either a CRT output or an LCD.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1028" title="EEVblog #1028 -  PC104 - The Full Version">1028</a></sup>

CRT televisions are no longer manufactured, and they make up a large share of [[current]] electronic waste.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qDWLTSfrq_E" title="2021 Australia Right to Repair Summit - Session 2 of 4">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xiXucGYuQ6M" title="Australian Right to Repair Inquiry with Productivity Commissioner Paul Lindwall">•</a></sup> Instruments built to service them have been obsoleted alongside them; a Philips PM5639 colour analyser, which uses red, green and blue filter lenses to take a spot measurement of a single point on the screen, has no remaining market even though the underlying technique still applies to calibrating modern LCD monitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-967" title="EEVblog #967 - Mystery Teardown">967</a></sup>
