Compliance voltage is the maximum [[voltage]] a [[current]] source can develop across its load while still delivering its programmed current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> It is the ceiling imposed by the [[voltage-source]] that sits behind every constant current circuit: a [[constant-current-source]] is a voltage source with constant current circuitry wrapped around it, and it cannot exist without that voltage source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The term matters because it sets the hard boundary of a current source's usefulness — below the compliance limit the circuit forces its current and the voltage falls out of Ohm's law; at the limit the current source simply stops being a current source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The concept follows directly from the elementary theory of voltage and current sources and their Thevenin and Norton equivalents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1397" title="EEVblog 1397 - DC Fundamentals Part 2: DC Voltage &amp; Current Sources (Thevenin &amp; Norton Theorems)">1397</a></sup>

## Behaviour at the limit

A constant current circuit adjusts its output voltage to whatever the load demands in order to sustain the set current, following V = IR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Drive a low-value load and the output voltage winds down to match; short the output entirely and the current source is untroubled, delivering its full current at a few tens of millivolts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The failure is at the other end. A constant current circuit is not a voltage multiplier: with a 5 V compliance limit it cannot produce more than 5 V across the load no matter what the load asks for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Past that point the output voltage pins at 5 V and stays there whether the load is 7, 8 or 9 ohms, and the programmed current is no longer delivered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Into an open circuit the output sits at the compliance voltage with zero current flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup>

The limit is often discovered rather than designed for. A [[fuse]] endurance test drifted off its set current because the rising [[resistance]] of the heating fuse element pushed the required voltage past the 6 V compliance of the supply, which topped out at 6.17 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1377" title="EEVblog 1377 - The Amazing UNPREDICTABILITY of Fuses!">1377</a></sup> A [[diode]] ramp test aborted at 5 V compliance and had to be repeated with the limit raised to 10 V before the curve could be traced to 1.5 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup>

## Bench supplies and source measure units

Any laboratory supply operating in current-limiting mode has a compliance voltage: it is simply the voltage setting, renamed for the mode it is in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Set a supply to 10 V and current-limit it, and 10 V is the compliance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> The term is not normally used while the supply is in voltage mode, but it is the correct industry parlance once the supply is regulating current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup> Dedicated current sources make the naming explicit — a Keithley 225 has a knob labelled voltage compliance, adjustable from 10 V to 100 V, making it effectively a 100 V supply with the current dialled in instead, restricted to a maximum of 10 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Source measure units go further: on a Keithley 2400 the compliance can be set to 150 V at 1 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup>

Choosing the compliance setting is itself a measurement decision. When an SMU is used to sink a small current from a converter output for an [[efficiency]] measurement, the compliance is set just above the expected output voltage — 13 V for a nominally lower output — because too low a setting clamps and loads the circuit under test rather than measuring it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Working the other way, the compliance reading reveals the load's own voltage: a bar graph tube driven at 1.05 mA from a 150 V compliance setting was found to be sustaining 105 V, its own sustaining voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-729" title="EEVblog #729 - Mailbag">729</a></sup> Charging a [[battery]] pack in [[constant-current-mode]] shows the same thing — with an 8.4 V maximum compliance the terminal voltage read 4.27 V and climbed as the pack recovered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XpZVIWdXliY" title="Fluke OneTouch Network Tester Battery Restoration Attempt">•</a></sup> A 500 mA constant current charge reading 28.98 V and rising was taken as evidence the pack was accepting charge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1760" title="EEVblog 1760 - Today in the Dumpster (July 12th, 2026)">1760</a></sup>

Compliance voltage cannot substitute for current. A [[zener-diode]] refuses to operate at 1 µA regardless of whether it is fed from a 10 V or a 100 V compliance source; the limitation is the current, not the headroom above it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

## LED strings

Series [[led]] strings are the archetypal [[constant-current-load]], and the string's total forward voltage sets the compliance a driver must have.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> Twenty white LEDs at a nominal 3 V each require around 60 V, and individual parts can run as high as 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup> Because the string is driven at constant current, the driver's output voltage is not constant — it rises to whatever the string needs, up to the compliance limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GbADsyp9wM8" title="Failed Lucci Constant Current LED Driver Teardown">•</a></sup>

The threshold behaviour is sharp. A string dialled up from a 9 V compliance setting passes no current at all, then at roughly 43 V current begins to flow and the LEDs light, with the output settling at 45 V, the string's total forward drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1688" title="EEVblog 1688 - Constant Current Sources EXPLAINED + DEMO">1688</a></sup> A 1 V, 100 A supply lights nothing: without the minimum compliance voltage, no current flows through the diode at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> Recovered television backlight strips illustrate the range — one set of LEDs dropping 5.1 V each at 1 mA and 5.8 V at 100 mA allowed only 18 in series from a 100 V compliance source,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-916" title="EEVblog #916 - Work Bench Lighting From A Dumpster LCD TV">916</a></sup> while another strip was run at 20 mA from a 48 V compliance supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup> A half-amp string in a photography light box required about 36 V, measuring 35.7 V against a 40 V maximum setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1372" title="EEVblog #1372 - DIY PCB Photography Light Box">1372</a></sup> LEDs mounted in series around the perimeter of a light box likewise call for a relatively high compliance voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6ZH2KeplSrs" title="DIY Product/PCB Photography Light Box - Part 3">•</a></sup>

Commercial constant current LED drivers are specified by current and compliance together. Architectural fittings used 500 mA drivers rated to 47 V and 45 V compliance,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GbADsyp9wM8" title="Failed Lucci Constant Current LED Driver Teardown">•</a></sup> a mains LED floodlight ran 4 A at a nominal 37 V, likely extending to around 40 V,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup> and a deck lighting installation of eight series LEDs used a 700 mA driver with roughly 40 V of compliance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup> Finding a replacement with the right current, compliance voltage, form factor and dimming capability proved difficult through local trade suppliers and had to be sourced from overseas.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GbADsyp9wM8" title="Failed Lucci Constant Current LED Driver Teardown">•</a></sup>

Compliance also bounds dimming. Where a constant voltage strip is dimmed by lowering the rail, the usable window runs from the nominal rail down only as far as the string's compliance requirement — one strip drew current down to a measured 13.7 V minimum and extinguished around 15 to 16 V on the way down from 24 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> Below roughly 14 V there is nothing left to work with — "No more compliance left for you." — and any dimming scheme has to take that floor into account.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> A strip using a simple dropper [[resistor]] instead of active electronics behaved the same way, dropping out at about 15 V for six LEDs in series, over 2 V per LED.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup>

The same relationship makes compliance a diagnostic. On a backlight driver suspected of failure, measuring zero across the output ceramic capacitors showed the boost stage was not generating the compliance voltage needed to drive the LED string.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup> Non-linear loads make the point more generally: charge extracted from a ceramic [[capacitor]] during reflow could push 30 µA, but without the compliance voltage the LEDs would not light regardless of the available current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering! WTF!">1455</a></sup> Plotting voltage alongside current would show both the compliance reached and the power actually delivered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering! WTF!">1455</a></sup>

## Compliance in test instruments

The voltage a meter presents to the circuit under test is a compliance voltage, and its magnitude determines what the measurement disturbs. Some 1980s digital multimeters carried a low ohms function that reduced the open circuit voltage of the resistance ranges to under 0.6 V, below the silicon junction turn-on threshold, so that in-circuit resistance could be measured without switching on transistors or diodes in parallel with the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup> Implementations set the maximum compliance of the resistance range to under about half a volt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup> The function is rare on modern meters,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup> and the accompanying habit — reversing the probes on an in-circuit [[resistance-measurement]] to check whether the reading is genuine resistance or a semiconductor turning on — remains the practical substitute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup> One modern meter advertising the feature was found still to exceed 0.6 V, and so not to be a true low ohms mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup>

Diode test ranges run the opposite way: the compliance must exceed the forward drop of the part under test. A meter powered from two AAA cells managed 2.43 V, enough to light a white LED,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup> while another delivered 2.1 V from three AA cells against 2.3 V from a two-cell competitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> A meter whose diode range tops out at 1.5 V cannot light a white LED or a red one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1084" title="EEVblog #1084 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout Part 2">1084</a></sup> Meters with elevated diode compliance extend what can be characterised — a 15 V diode test compliance read 10.7 V on a part that a lower-voltage tester showed simply as open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup> An [[lcr-meter]]'s component test compliance was measured at 0.6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

Compliance is also a repair symptom. A meter fed 30 mA from a programmable source reading zero, with a compliance voltage present at its terminals, indicates a fault in the meter rather than the source,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1424" title="EEVblog 1424 - Fluke 23 Multimeter Repair">1424</a></sup> and a reading of 13.4 V on a current input is the compliance voltage of the driving source appearing because the instrument is on the wrong range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2QZ8GWwzXXY" title="Tektronix Multimeter Repair-O-Thon">•</a></sup>

## Other current-driven loads

Piezoelectric accelerometers require a constant current supply, typically a few milliamps to 10 mA, together with a compliance voltage in a specified window — one part calling for anywhere from 18 V to 30 V, set to 20 V in practice with the resulting bias landing mid-range once the sensor is connected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Industrial 4-20 mA loop sources specify the same parameter under the name loop voltage, commonly 12 V to 24 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f_SdM6sXHD4" title="FNIRSI SG-003A Process Meter + DSO-TC2 Oscilloscope &amp; Transistor Tester">•</a></sup> Nixie tube drive raises the question of whether transistors must withstand the full 170 V compliance, or a nominal 200 V, or something lower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> A small auxiliary output rated 5 V at 20 mA for driving an external LED is likely a constant current output with 5 V of compliance, which is the sort of thing that ought to be marked on the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup>

Compliance voltage also explains an otherwise puzzling measurement in fuse testing. A fuse carrying 400 mA drops only around 0.2 V of [[burden-voltage]], or 0.3 V measured two-wire including the lead drop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup> When the fuse blows, the voltage across it jumps to the compliance voltage of the constant current supply — 12 V in one such arrangement — and that jump is what a detector circuit triggers on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1676" title="EEVblog 1676 - Lab Timer HACK">1676</a></sup> Even a deliberately low compliance setting is not always low enough: an LED matching exercise at 20 mA with the compliance reduced to 10 V still destroyed a part connected the wrong way round.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>
