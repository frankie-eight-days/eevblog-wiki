A [[lcd|liquid crystal display]] modulates light that is already present rather than emitting its own, which is why it dominates every application where power budget, cost or daylight readability matters: wristwatches, calculators, handheld multimeters, panel meters, and flat-panel televisions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> The technology spans an enormous range, from a single-common static segment display costing a dollar or two to a 4K television panel driven by chip-on-flex drivers across its entire edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup> LCDs displaced plasma in the television market outright, and plasma's poor reliability record helped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup>

## Operating principle

The basic stack is a front polarizer, a back plane electrode acting as the common terminal, and the liquid crystal fluid itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Drive is by [[electric-field|electric field]] between the positive and negative plates, not by [[current]], and that field changes the orientation of the crystals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> With no field applied to a twisted nematic cell, light passes through, reflects off the rear reflector and comes straight back out, so the segment is invisible against the silver background; applying a field lines the crystals up and blocks the light, turning the segment on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> Microchip application note AN658 covers the chemistry and physics in depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup>

Because the cell is capacitive rather than resistive, its current draw is minute, and a monochrome reflective display needs no [[backlight]] at all in ordinary room light.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup>

## Static and segment displays

A traditional low-cost reflective static LCD is the simplest form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1044" title="EEVblog #1044 - LCD Technology Tutorial">1044</a></sup> A typical three-digit part is a 24-pin device where pin 1 is the common and every remaining pin is a single segment, so one common terminal serves all segments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> Not all displays are wired this way — many carry two, three or four commons, and the number of commons is the critical parameter when selecting a driver chip or a [[microcontroller]] with a built-in LCD module.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> Segment LCDs also arrive in odd configurations: three-and-a-half digits with a plus/minus indicator and an arrow annunciator, or alphanumeric variants that can display letters and symbols as well as numbers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup>

Driving these segments requires dedicated LCD driving circuitry; they cannot simply be strobed like an [[led|LED]] display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> A design needing one common and 23 segments narrows the microcontroller field considerably, and an ATtiny48-AU meets that requirement at 86 cents in hundreds — cheap enough that once the display and the DC-to-DC converter are excluded, the microcontroller becomes the most expensive part on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> Some microcontrollers add core-independent LCD animation, so blinking and alternating displays continue autonomously while the core stays in low-power mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> At the opposite extreme, a level meter built entirely from 74- and 4000-series CMOS logic can drive an LCD with no [[processor]] at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1258" title="EEVblog #1258 - MORE Dumpster Wandel And Goltermann!">1258</a></sup>

## Voltage doubling across an inverter

A five-volt logic supply can produce ten volts peak to peak across an [[lcd-segment|LCD segment]] without any charge pump. Connecting the display across the input and the output of an inverting gate — an XOR gate and a buffer fed from ordinary 5 V logic, or even an [[arduino|Arduino]] pin — presents the panel with two square waves in opposite phase, and the difference between them, A minus B, swings 10 V peak to peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1046" title="EEVblog #1046 - Mysterious Digital Voltage Doubling (LCD design)">1046</a></sup> This is not a theoretical artefact: measured differentially on an [[oscilloscope]] at 5 [[volts-per-division|volts per division]], the result matches the mathematical A-minus-B function exactly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1046" title="EEVblog #1046 - Mysterious Digital Voltage Doubling (LCD design)">1046</a></sup> The effect is simply a change of reference: nothing is stepped up, but the segment sees double the supply [[voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1046" title="EEVblog #1046 - Mysterious Digital Voltage Doubling (LCD design)">1046</a></sup>

## Character modules

Character modules with a standard chipset and a standard footprint are among the most reusable parts in hobby and small-volume design. A 53 by 20 mm two-line by 16-character module is an industry standard size available from four, five or six manufacturers with identical mounting holes and dimensions, and it uses a standard LCD chipset so existing code works unchanged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4" title="EEVblog #4 - Calculator Design, Mr Bean, &amp; FPGA&#x27;s">4</a></sup> An 8 by 2 module of roughly 40 by 35 mm with the standard Hitachi pinout is similarly generic, and the non-backlit version is very thin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-313" title="EEVblog #313 - Bus Pirate LCD Debugging">313</a></sup>

Such a part can function as the seed of an entire product. The MicroWatch calculator watch was built around that 53 by 20 mm module; without it, the project would have been a non-starter, because a custom LCD, custom keypad, custom case and custom band attachment all cost serious money to develop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11" title="EEVblog #11 Part 1 of 2 - Talk about DIY product design">11</a></sup> Hooking an LCD to a microcontroller and a keypad is a couple of hours' work — the hard problem was making the result fit on a wrist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11" title="EEVblog #11 Part 1 of 2 - Talk about DIY product design">11</a></sup>

Interface choice matters at the pin-count level. An [[i2c|I2C]] module needs only SCL, SDA and a reset line, freeing microcontroller pins that a parallel interface would consume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Graphical modules often offer both: a Newhaven 128 by 64 pixel display supports [[spi|SPI]] and a traditional 8-bit parallel interface, the parallel path chosen where transfer speed matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8" title="EEVblog #8 Part 1 of 2 - Graphical LCD Displays &amp; PIC Micro Demo Boards">8</a></sup> A 20 by 2 line module was specified over the more common 16 by 2 because the extra characters were needed for status display, and at eight or ten dollars it was the most expensive component in an entire bench [[power-supply|power supply]] — a cost accepted on the principle that a decent display is not optional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup> Where a module carries an RGB backlight, the LEDs should be driven from the microcontroller's own [[pwm|PWM]] peripheral rather than through the I2C port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

## Power consumption

The power case against LED seven-segment displays is decisive. LED segments need at least a couple of milliamps each for usable brightness in a lab or office, and a good grunty 5 mA per segment across a multi-digit display reaches roughly 40 mA, a substantial share of a 3.3 V power budget.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> A Sharp memory LCD updating at 1 Hz draws about 6 microwatts, roughly 2 microamps at 3 V, and in one [[battery]]-powered design the display rather than the Giant Gecko processor dominated total consumption at that level.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup>

[[datasheet|Datasheet]] figures should be verified on the bench: a display specified at 180 microamps measured 600 microamps because the contrast setting left many pixels partially on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup> Where a reflective display is specified, a backlight is worth designing in but not worth using in normal operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1699" title="EEVblog 1699 - µTimer PROJECT Part 1 - The LCD">1699</a></sup> LCD current also matters at the layout level, since the display's own draw adds [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] along shared supply traces and eats into an already narrow margin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

## Selecting and sourcing

[[parametric-search|Parametric search]] by digit count and price is the practical selection method, filtering distributor catalogues down from thousands of parts — one search returned 38 candidates in the three to four-and-a-half digit range, sorted by price, with the cheapest a Lumex part at $1.61 each in hundreds against roughly $2 for the next tier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-298" title="EEVblog #298 - Dave&#x27;s Decade Digit Display - USB Supply Part 3">298</a></sup> Datasheet configuration, particularly the pin table and the common arrangement, must then be checked individually.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup>

For a project centred on its display, the display is chosen first and the product envelope, battery and feature set designed around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1703" title="EEVblog 1703 - µTimer Project Part 2: An E-Paper Like LCD">1703</a></sup> E-paper is a tempting substitute but is specified for update cycles from tens of seconds to minutes, which rules it out for a timer or stopwatch updating once a second or faster.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1703" title="EEVblog 1703 - µTimer Project Part 2: An E-Paper Like LCD">1703</a></sup> The related memory LCD is not e-ink at all: it is essentially an LCD with an individual memory per pixel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-761" title="EEVblog #761 - Pebble Time Smartwatch Teardown">761</a></sup>

Second sourcing is worth designing for. One eBook reader carried two alternative [[connector]] footprints on the same board so that whichever TFT panel was available could be fitted by loading the appropriate connector and moving the [[ribbon-cable|ribbon cable]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup> At the panel level, several sizes have become de facto standards — the 070-family 7-inch 800 by 480 panel with a 50-pin flat flex, used widely in Raspberry Pi conversions, appears in low-cost instruments and can be replaced with a generic equivalent right down to the asymmetric bezel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-g2kCBfUa9H0" title="Will this $5 Oscilloscope Work?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1492" title="EEVblog 1492 - $5 Oscilloscope Repaired! + Oz GIVEAWAY">1492</a></sup>

## Panels, backlights and diffusion

A modern flat-panel television contains no high-voltage drive circuitry: the panel with its chip-on-flex drivers, a single-sided power supply, and an LED backlight driver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup> Power draw is correspondingly modest — around 100 W for a 50-inch LCD against roughly 500 W for a plasma of similar size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup>

The backlight assembly is where much of the technology sits. An LED-backlit panel replaces the cold [[cathode]] fluorescent lamp with LED strips plus a stack of diffusion and prism layers that improve brightness, contrast, uniformity and energy [[efficiency]] by redirecting off-axis light back into the stack rather than losing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> Earlier notebook screens exhibited visible hot spots and uneven illumination that these layers eliminate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup> The LCD panel itself forms the entire front surface of a modern set, with no separate protective film over it — a fact that becomes relevant the moment the set is dropped, and one that packaging designers address with foam lattice on the front face only.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-465" title="EEVblog #465 - LED LCD Panel Teardown">465</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-FUjRmecLGzw" title="eevBLAB #77 - TV Packaging Technology is INTERESTING">•</a></sup>

Above the panel sits the timing control board, which splits and times the video signal coming from the main processor board and feeds the panel over multiway flat flex.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup> Some designs place an additional buffer board in the ribbon run to the panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup>

## Failure modes and diagnosis

A blank display is a power problem until proven otherwise; the first rule is "thou shalt measure voltages" before suspecting the screen or the microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> LCD bias supplies are frequently negative or unusual — an Amstrad notepad generates minus 15 V purely for its display — and a failed [[electrolytic-capacitor|electrolytic capacitor]] in that bias rail is a common and easily repaired cause of a dead screen, far more likely from the outset than a failed [[lcd-driver|LCD driver]] or a failed panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup>

Symptoms distinguish the display from what drives it. A meter showing all segments lit indicates the processor never booted far enough to send the clear command, not a display fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup> A wrong reading that persists across voltage, millivolt, milliamp and amp ranges is a processor problem rather than an LCD problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9s9LXOBknck" title="Unusual Brymen BM235 Fault">•</a></sup>

Panel faults are usually terminal. Persistent vertical or horizontal lines that survive a change of input, appear on both halves of a split drive arrangement, and remain after both the T-con and processor boards have been reflowed and every rail measured clean, point to the panel or to its chip-on-flex drivers, and repairing those gives Buckley's chance of success.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-794" title="EEVblog #794 - Samsung Dumpster LCD Repair Connector Followup">794</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> A replacement T-con board runs 80 to 100 dollars before shipping, and a replacement panel is generally beyond economical repair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-794" title="EEVblog #794 - Samsung Dumpster LCD Repair Connector Followup">794</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup>

Physical damage is the dominant reason large panels reach the dumpster, and a crack often shows only as a faint impact mark until the set is powered up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fxEFnCpq_m4" title="Dumpster Diving 50 inch LG LCD TV">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ia4xB9wk80o" title="Dumpster Diving 86&quot; LG 4K LCD TV">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1261" title="EEVblog #1261 - Dumpster Diving 55&quot; LCD TV">1261</a></sup> Blunt force with no visible external mark can still destroy a panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ia4xB9wk80o" title="Dumpster Diving 86&quot; LG 4K LCD TV">•</a></sup> Connector damage is another route: on one laptop design, liquid ingress carbonized the LCD connector between the pins, with the largest pin carrying about 50 volts of backlight supply — the highest voltage in the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup>

Old displays fail in ways unrelated to their electronics. A 33-year-old colour laptop panel still boots with every pixel working while the image is ruined by degradation of the polarizing filters, the vinegar syndrome familiar to Macintosh collectors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W91qP8GpgjM" title="Apple Powerbook 165C Laptop">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-afwqRu7W8V0" title="Apple Powerbook 165C Vinegar Syndrome Repair Attempt">•</a></sup> The original Sharp LM64C06P panel, a 640 by 400 passive matrix part, remains available new-old-stock or refurbished at around 220 Australian dollars, with no modern replacement made.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-afwqRu7W8V0" title="Apple Powerbook 165C Vinegar Syndrome Repair Attempt">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-W91qP8GpgjM" title="Apple Powerbook 165C Laptop">•</a></sup>

## Retrofit and salvage

Replacing a failed [[crt|CRT]] with an LCD is a routine upgrade for older test equipment. A commercial retrofit kit for a Tektronix TDS-series scope costs around 350 dollars, against 20 or 30 dollars for a generic 640 by 480 VGA colour LCD complete with driver board — leaving only the mechanical problem of mounting it behind the original bezel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WHwf26JCUec" title="Tektronix TDS540D Repair Part 1">•</a></sup> The payoff is lighter weight, lower power and better reliability, at the cost of brightness: a retrofitted panel washes out as the brightness control is advanced and is noticeably dimmer than the original tube.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1515" title="EEVblog 1515 - Dumpster Tektronix TDS540D 500MHz Oscilloscope LCD Upgrade">1515</a></sup>

Salvaged panels are of limited value on their own, since a project will generally call for a specific display rather than whatever came out of the last teardown.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1302" title="EEVblog #1302 - Scrapping a Dumpster Laser Printer For Parts">1302</a></sup> A cracked television is more useful for its LED backlight assembly, which can be stripped of the broken glass and repurposed as a large diffuse light panel — useful, among other things, as a light box for viewing through printed circuit boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup>

## Displays in test equipment

Handheld meters are built around their displays: four digits at the low end, five at the higher end, quoted as counts, where the count is the maximum value the reading can reach before the meter must change range and lose a digit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Ruggedness follows from the panel. A meter rated for a 3 metre drop kept its LCD intact through a 25 metre drop; the failures came from corner-first landings that put the impact directly beside the screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-868" title="EEVblog #868 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Torture Test">868</a></sup>

OLED alternatives read beautifully indoors but are power hungry and close to useless outdoors, which is why manufacturers continue to offer LCD versions of the same meters — sometimes with reduced functionality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WDYOSddZwto" title="Mystery Keysight Unboxing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup>

Oscilloscope screens are typically 320 by 240 quarter-VGA in the budget bracket with an LED backlight, and 800 by 600 in larger instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kdjpbWLi7UI" title="Full Review of the Rigol DS1052E  Oscilloscope">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Vertical viewing angle is a common weakness: graticule visibility can be lost at around 45 degrees from below, a limitation shared across Rigol, Agilent X-series and Tektronix 3000-series instruments alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Panel quality is sometimes explicitly derated in the datasheet — one bench scope's display is specified to permit up to five dead or stuck pixels without being considered a failure, a consumer-grade clause absent from the same manufacturer's higher series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup>

Safety architecture can also be dictated by the display. In an isolated instrument, isolating a flat panel display is harder than isolating a vacuum fluorescent one, because the LCD must connect directly to the processor over a bus of some 24 lines; moving that processing to [[ground]] potential required a new, much narrower protocol to the measurement engine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 5 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>
