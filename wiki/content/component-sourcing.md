Component sourcing is the process of finding, evaluating, selecting, and purchasing the electronic parts that go into a design. It consumes the majority of the effort in product development: a designer will typically spend more time searching for suitable parts that meet electrical, mechanical, visual, and cost criteria than on the circuit design and [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]] themselves, with each of the hundreds of individual part decisions in a product potentially involving hours of searching.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-88" title="EEVblog #88 - There&#x27;s More To Electronics Than Just Circuit Design">88</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> For kits and small production runs, the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] and component availability — not the circuit — is usually the hard part, sometimes requiring volume commitments of several thousand pieces on a single part to guarantee supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## The parametric search workflow

The standard workflow begins with [[parametric-search|parametric search]] on catalog distributor sites — Digi-Key, Mouser, Farnell, Newark, RS Components — which allow filtering across many manufacturers at once by parameters such as accuracy, on-[[resistance]], package, price, and stock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> Aggregator services such as Octopart and FindChips search across distributors simultaneously to compare price and availability, and are used routinely to verify that a candidate part is actually obtainable before it is designed in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-754" title="EEVblog #754 - Altium Circuit Maker First Impressions">754</a></sup> Distributor search engines are not exhaustive, however; parts frequently appear only on a manufacturer's own website, so a thorough search cross-checks manufacturer parametric tools as well, since a missed part can mean a missed optimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> Search results must then be confirmed against stock: filtering to in-stock items only can collapse hundreds of candidates down to a manageable few dozen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Obscure or unbranded parts — for instance an unidentified Sino Power [[mosfet|MOSFET]] — may require searching the manufacturer's own site directly when the part number yields nothing on the major catalogs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup>

## Economics of search time

Time spent searching is justified by production arithmetic. Saving 10¢ on a single part across a 10,000-unit run returns $1,000, so at a nominal engineering rate of $100 per hour, five to ten hours spent finding the right part still comes out ahead; genuinely critical parts can merit days of searching.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup> Selection of apparently mundane components can be protracted in professional contexts — selecting and qualifying a suitable switch for a navy simulator took three months.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1686" title="EEVblog 1686 - Electronex 2025 Melbourne - Walkaround">1686</a></sup> For cost-sensitive designs, BOM costing is conventionally done at 1,000-off distributor pricing as a realistic benchmark, since that is the quantity break distributors publish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Very large buyers obtain pricing far below catalog figures through direct deals, which is why BOM estimates for products from companies with the buying power of Amazon or Apple tend to overstate their true component cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-220" title="Amazon Kindle Fire Tablet Review &amp; Unboxing - EEVblog #220">220</a></sup>

## Availability-driven design

Availability is a first-order design constraint, not a purchasing afterthought. A part with a 9-week lead time or thin stock is a design-in risk, and where stock differs between variants of the same part, the well-stocked variant wins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> The consistent practical rule is to order the parts before designing the [[pcb|PCB]]: "Buy the parts before you design the PCB."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> Parts that go out of stock between [[schematic]] capture and layout can leave a board with an unpopulated footprint, as happened with an FT230XS [[usb|USB]]-UART that became unavailable everywhere after design-in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup> Distributor stock shown online may be physically offshore — a part listed by Farnell or RS in Australia may actually sit in a UK warehouse with the attendant delay.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1387" title="EEVblog 1387 - MOSFET Repair Replacement Search">1387</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GbADsyp9wM8" title="Failed Lucci Constant Current LED Driver Teardown">•</a></sup> Lead times of 18 weeks or more effectively disqualify a part regardless of price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> During shortages the problem compounds: high-end [[fpga|FPGAs]] have shown zero stock and 52-week lead times at catalog distributors, and products designed around a single constrained [[microcontroller]] have forced small businesses to close entirely when multi-year lead times made the part unobtainable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1396" title="EEVblog 1396 - BlackMagic ATEM Mini Extreme ISO TEARDOWN">1396</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> Shortage conditions also attract brokers — described as "component vultures" — charging around $15 for a chip normally worth $2–$3, which alone can blow out a product's cost target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>

## Jellybean parts and second sourcing

A jellybean part is defined by four properties: it has been manufactured for a long time, it is available from dozens of manufacturers both Western and Asian, it is extremely cheap, and it is stocked in depth — millions of pieces across distributors even during component shortages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1436" title="EEVBlog 1436 - The TOP 5 Jellybean OPAMP&#x27;s">1436</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1599" title="EEVblog 1599 - TOP 5 Jellybean Bipolar Transistors">1599</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup> The engineering value is substitutability: if the specified manufacturer cannot supply, any equivalent with the same part number will do the job, and jellybean MOSFETs can be found at around 1.3 cents each with hundreds of thousands in stock across multiple vendors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1436" title="EEVBlog 1436 - The TOP 5 Jellybean OPAMP&#x27;s">1436</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> The "holy grail" of design is multiple qualified suppliers for every part in the bill of materials, though most real products contain at least one single-source part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> When relying on an obscure ultra-cheap source, equivalent name-brand alternates should be listed on the BOM as insurance against that source drying up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1461" title="EEVblog 1461 - The MOSFET Search CHALLENGE">1461</a></sup> Within a product line, reusing parts already in the company's catalog and already loaded on its pick-and-place machines is standard practice, because "the last thing you want to do is use yet another component that you've got to stock."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup> Consolidating the number of distinct line items matters for the same reason: a board using 160 different component types costs more to assemble, and any single missing part out of those 160 can halt manufacture entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>

## Bill of materials discipline

The BOM is the boundary between engineering and purchasing. A competent design engineer specifies exact part numbers down to package and grade suffixes, plus a list of qualified alternates, and competent purchasing staff buy only what is listed from the specified suppliers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup> Ordering suffix letters carry real meaning — package, supply [[voltage]] range, temperature grade — and getting one character wrong yields the wrong part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1074" title="EEVblog #1074  - Custom LCD Design - Part 2 - (µSupply Part 17)">1074</a></sup> Specifying a generic part ("buy an [[lm317|LM317]]") is cited as the worst practice, because characteristics then vary by manufacturer and batch across the production run, with no consistency between units.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup> Even legitimately purchased parts can change under a fixed part number: a manufacturer die revision that alters a specification the design happens to rely on will flow automatically into production and fail in the field at scale, which is why end-of-life and change notifications matter at high volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup> Conversely, if the BOM permits any qualified supplier, the assembler will load whichever reel is on hand, and batch-to-batch or brand-to-brand variation — for example in the piezoelectric behavior of a generic 10 µF ceramic [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] — becomes an uncontrolled variable in the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1697" title="EEVblog 1697 - What&#x27;s Up With Different Brand Op-Amps?">1697</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> Connectors nominally rated for reflow have arrived in batches that melt, apparently due to changed plastic formulation or substituted clone parts, one batch working and the next failing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-782" title="EEVblog #782 - The Dangers Of Reflow Soldering">782</a></sup>

## Distributors, the gray market, and counterfeits

Authorized distributors — Digi-Key, Mouser, Farnell, and similar catalog suppliers — are the standard defense against counterfeit parts; fake components from these channels are described as virtually unheard of, whereas gray-market purchases regularly deliver fakes or wrong-tolerance parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-197" title="RAW! Testing µCurrent&#x27;s, Keithley Current/Voltage Sources, DIY Pick And Place - EEVblog #197">197</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-134" title="EEVblog #134 - The Maxim Manipulation">134</a></sup> Re-reeled cut tape from a distributor carries less traceability than an original manufacturer reel, since the original label is lost when the distributor applies its own sticker.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup> Supply chains are porous enough that even large, reputable companies get stung by counterfeit parts, which is why driver-level retaliation against end products (as in the FTDI bricking episode) punishes buyers rather than counterfeiters, who have already been paid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-676" title="EEVblog #676 - RANT: FTDI Bricking Counterfeit Chips!">676</a></sup> Historical teardown evidence shows the consequences of price-only purchasing: the Osborne 1's memory was bought as the cheapest chips available from whatever source was at hand, mixing brands and factories on one board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup> Cheap consumer products similarly use "whatever grade component they can find," including potentially fake capacitors, which precludes any reliability expectation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6" title="EEVblog #6 - Part 2 of 2 - Why cheap multimeters suck">6</a></sup> Partial reels and cut tape also create practical assembly problems, feeding poorly in pick-and-place machines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup>

## Chinese supply channels

A large parallel ecosystem of parts exists that is invisible to Western catalog channels: Chinese-market microcontrollers at 3 cents, linear regulators at 1 cent, and 10-cent RISC-V parts are stocked in tens of thousands by suppliers such as LCSC but do not appear on Digi-Key, Mouser, FindChips, or Octopart.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1524" title="EEVblog 1524 - The 10 CENT RISC V Processor! CH32V003">1524</a></sup> Accessing this channel means going direct to Chinese suppliers or manufacturers, [[via]] LCSC, Alibaba, or AliExpress; AliExpress is preferable to Alibaba for small buys because Alibaba listings frequently carry placeholder prices and require quote negotiation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Direct dealing with larger Chinese vendors is workable if exact part numbers are specified precisely, and Mandarin language ability is a significant asset.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> Hobbyist-scale purchasing exploits the same channel: [[resistor]] kits and assorted parts ship from China for one or two dollars delivered, making a $300 lab setup feasible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> The trade-offs are quality opacity — no-name film and ceramic capacitors carry elevated failure risk in stress applications such as capacitive droppers — and distribution gaps, since even legitimate Chinese manufacturers' parts may be unobtainable from any catalog stockist.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1486" title="EEVblog 1486 - What you DIDN&#x27;T KNOW About Film Capacitor FAILURES!">1486</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1037" title="EEVblog #1037 - Solving Ceramic Capacitor Cracking">1037</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1275" title="EEVblog #1275 - Uniden UHF Walkie Talkie Teardowns">1275</a></sup>

## Production purchasing

Production quantities change the purchasing rules. Assemblers require parts on reels rather than loose cut tape; supplying 100 loose resistors for 100 boards forces manual re-reeling and extra charges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Minimum order quantities bite hard: a part available only as a 3,000-piece reel at 40 cents forces a $1,209 outlay to obtain 100 needed parts, and parts that come only in 2,500-piece reels must simply be designed out for small runs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Distributor re-reeling services (around $7) supply exact quantities, though buying the full reel is often simpler and provides stock for future runs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup> Orders must include attrition overage — roughly 5% extra on passives, or 10% / 50 extra pieces as alternative rules of thumb — because pick-and-place machines drop parts and reel leaders waste the first components fed through the machine; parts worth fractions of a cent are never recovered, so a 1,000-board run cannot be kitted with exactly 1,000 of each part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup> A turnkey assembly service removes all of this — the assembler sources everything from the Gerbers and BOM alone — at a substantial price premium and loss of control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup> Contract assemblers typically supply 80–90% of components themselves, asking the customer to buy in only product-specific parts, a division that reflects who bears the sourcing risk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup> For one-time-programmable parts, factory programming happens before delivery, with parts shipped pre-programmed on tubes or reels straight to the line.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1141" title="EEVblog #1141 - Padauk 3 CENT Micro - Programmer">1141</a></sup> Historically none of this existed: components were expensive, lead times ran to 40 weeks, and salvaging parts from discarded equipment was standard practice, particularly outside the US.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-54" title="EEVblog #54 - Electronics - When I was a boy...">54</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup>

## Lab stockpiling

Maintaining a comprehensive personal component stock is a losing game — "you're never going to have the part you want in your lab" — because modern designs need specific devices for specific reasons. Next-day or same-day delivery from catalog distributors has largely eliminated the case for deep personal stock beyond jellybean staples.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup>
