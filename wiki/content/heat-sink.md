A heat sink is a piece of metal attached to a dissipating component to disperse waste heat into the surrounding air, so that the component's junction stays at a reasonable operating temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Virtually every electronic component turns some of the power passing through it into heat, and the heat sink is the element that decides whether that heat leaves the die fast enough to keep the part alive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Its effect is quantified by a single number — [[thermal-resistance|thermal resistance]] in degrees Celsius per watt — which makes heat sink selection an arithmetic exercise rather than a matter of taste.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup>

## Thermal resistance and the design calculation

Semiconductor data sheets give thermal resistance from junction to case and from junction to ambient. A typical power [[transistor]] might specify 3.13 °C/W junction to case and 62.5 °C/W junction to ambient; the latter figure means that if the device is mounted free-standing on the board with no heat sink at all, dissipating one watt raises its junction 62.5 °C above ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Heat sinks are specified the same way: a commercial extrusion rated at 4.5 °C/W will rise 4.5 °C above ambient for every watt put into it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> The elements stack in series exactly as resistances do in an electrical circuit, and the chain can be walked through with back-of-the-envelope arithmetic to arrive at a [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]], with [[airflow]] and geometry then adjusted until that number is acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

The method is accurate enough to be trusted. A dummy load dissipating 11 W into a 4.5 °C/W heat sink was predicted to sit about 50 °C above a 20 °C ambient, giving roughly 70 °C; the measured heat sink temperature was a couple of degrees over 70.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> [[datasheet|Data sheet]] figures are not always representative, however — one small regulator's heat sink measured about 4 °C/W where the nearest catalogue part was quoted at 15 °C/W, the difference attributable to larger fins and therefore greater surface area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Because so many terms in the chain are estimates, sound practice is to add margin deliberately: take some real measurements, apply a fudge factor, and choose a heat sink twice as big as the calculation says is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup>

Two traps recur. Data sheet dissipation ratings are usually quoted at 25 °C ambient, but the temperature that matters is the local one — if the heat sink has climbed to 60 °C, the device is derating against 60 °C, not against the 25 °C of the room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> And the thermal resistance figure for a bare heat sink assumes it is radiating into free air; forced air changes it completely, so the specification must match the actual installed condition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup>

## Airflow

Heat sinks are not very efficient without air flow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup> The same 10 W into the same heat sink produced 76.3 °C in still air and 35.7 °C once air was blown across it, with the device case dropping from about 87 °C to about 47 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> A small regulator that survived comfortably with airflow across it cut out entirely when run as a free-air heat sink at the same dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

Fin orientation follows directly: fins must run parallel to the airflow, and heat sinks are routinely found with their fins deliberately aligned to the intake direction even where the fins are short and the available surface area is therefore limited.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1396" title="EEVblog 1396 - BlackMagic ATEM Mini Extreme ISO TEARDOWN">1396</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> Fins turned across the flow simply block it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1396" title="EEVblog 1396 - BlackMagic ATEM Mini Extreme ISO TEARDOWN">1396</a></sup> The same logic governs the whole enclosure. Intake vents placed low draw air across the board and up through the heat sinks before exhausting, which is more efficient than venting at the top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> Air can be channelled through an extrusion so that the whole flow is forced through the fin gaps rather than around them,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> and formed aluminium ducting can be used to take air in from the sides, through the fans, over the main processor heat sink and out the back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1280" title="EEVblog #1280 - Dumpster Lab PC Upgrade">1280</a></sup> Obstructions matter — an L bracket placed in the stream breaks up the flow,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> boards stacked across a duct block it,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-946" title="EEVblog #946 - Apple (Raspberry) Pi Cluster - PART 2">946</a></sup> and blowing the exhaust of one CPU heat sink into a second one is poor practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-726" title="EEVblog #726 - Dual Xeon Video Editing Machine Build">726</a></sup>

Fan-cooled equipment often runs its fan under [[temperature-control|temperature control]], so a heat sink can counter-intuitively run cooler under load than at idle, because loading the outputs spins the fan up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

## The chassis as a heat sink

Using existing metalwork saves a component. A power device can be bolted or clamped to the side of a case, sometimes through a custom folded aluminium bracket with individual clamps and sil pad isolation,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> and the practice of using the rear panel or an internal backing plate as the sink is long established.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup> Variations include a bracket angled to push a switching transistor against the back panel,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-788" title="EEVblog #788 - Apple IIC Teardown">788</a></sup> a 7805 tied down to the chassis for the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-840" title="EEVblog #840 - Yamaha M3000 Mixing Console Teardown">840</a></sup> a series pass transistor mounted directly to the chassis,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1153" title="EEVblog #1153 - 1970&#x27;s Programmable Calculator Teardown">1153</a></sup> a [[soldering]] station's bottom case,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> a die-cast speaker enclosure,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1156" title="EEVblog #1156 - Focal Professional CMS40 Studio Monitor Teardown">1156</a></sup> and a brass centre block connecting to both the upper and lower cases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-236" title="EEVblog #236 - FE-5680A Rubidium Standard Teardown">236</a></sup> A folded sheet heat sink that mates with and screws into the aluminium back panel shares its load with the chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup> Aluminium plates and thermal pads can be moulded to contact every major heat-generating package on a board at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> The approach has a hard limit: a plastic-backed case cannot be used as a heat sink at all, and an alloy-backed case would have allowed a larger passive solution in its place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

The [[pcb|PCB]] itself is a heat sink. Copper flood fill thermally relieved to a device's [[ground]] pin serves as one,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup> and placing a [[resistor]]'s leads close to the board lets the copper pull heat out of the resistor body — though lead thermal resistance limits how much, and the relationship tapers off with lead length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup> Relying on the bare board alone can fail, requiring a bodged-on heat sink at the production stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-872" title="EEVblog #872 - Mailbag">872</a></sup>

For surface-mount parts, heat can be taken through thermal vias and a transfer bar to the case, which then acts as the heat sink; this is a professional solution for getting rid of a couple of watts, not for a hundred-watt amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Since every element in the chain adds thermal resistance, going straight from the case to the heat sink and eliminating the vias, sil pads and transfer bar gives a lower junction temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> The competing pressure is production cost: bolts, washers, heat bars and screwed-on heat sinks are all extra assembly operations, which is why surface-mount heat sinks exist, kept small by the limited suction force of pick-and-place heads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> Where no catalogue part fits the volumetric constraints, a custom heat sink is neither difficult nor expensive at production runs of a couple of thousand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup>

## Mounting and interface

The joint between device and heat sink is part of the thermal path. Thermal pads and sil pads provide [[electrical-isolation|electrical isolation]] while maintaining coupling, and once devices are isolated at the board no further pad is needed between the transfer bar and the case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> A heat sink carrying isolated devices may be left at no potential at all, floating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Clip-on heat sinks that slot into the board without any compound, stick-on heat sinks fixed with thermal [[adhesive]], and thermal adhesive bonding LEDs or Peltier plates to aluminium are all in common use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup>

Thermal compound rewards attention. The single central blob of paste relies on the mating surface being flat; on a base with machining ridges, paste is caught in the grooves and can travel along them instead of spreading across the die.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MiqxUaWkGYY" title="AMD Ryzen 5900X Upgrade S**T SHOW">•</a></sup> A mounting that does not sit flush needs a great deal of paste to compensate and performs poorly for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-648" title="EEVblog #648 - Mailbag">648</a></sup> Retention hardware is typically spring loaded and cannot easily be over-tightened, with the corners done up diagonally,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-462" title="EEVblog #462 - Video Editing PC Assembly">462</a></sup> but paste can dry out and mountings can loosen with age, producing over-temperature shutdowns years later.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yKflC-fmSX8" title="CPU Thermal Ghost">•</a></sup> Chips of differing height under one common heat sink are accommodated by machining raised lands into the sink to meet the shorter packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup>

A heat sink can be given secondary duties: carrying the main [[current]] in a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] bridge,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup> pressing shield cans down onto the board to eliminate a hand-[[solder]] step,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> or acting simultaneously as the display mounting bracket.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> Temperature sensing is usually a single TO-92 device clamped to one side of the extrusion, since a large aluminium heat sink has negligible internal thermal resistance and the other side will read the same.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup>

## Typical loads

Linear regulation is the classic case: a linear supply needs a big heat sink on the back and is wasteful for a given output power, the price paid for its [[noise]] and [[ripple]] performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup> Where two regulators share one heat sink, the dissipation shifts between them depending on whether the supply is in constant-current or constant-[[voltage]] mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> Multiple channels sharing an extrusion interact — the shared channel gets hotter, and worst-case thermal testing means loading every channel on that sink at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Electronic loads put essentially all of their rated power into the sinks: 300 W into two large finned extrusions with fans drawing air through them,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup> or 250 W into a single heat sink carrying ten power transistors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> In an instrument combining several functions, the purely linear [[electronic-load|electronic load]] section carries the largest heat sinking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> Switching topologies remove most of this: once the design goes switching, massive heat sinks are no longer required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1513" title="EEVblog 1513 - Dumpster Tossers Have No Shame!">1513</a></sup>

LEDs are mounted on heat sinks because they are not efficient and dissipate the majority of their input power as heat in the die.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> A COB module's data sheet may cap the aluminium PCB temperature at 85 °C, which becomes the design target for the heat sink behind it, and the extrusion is anodised for improved dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup> Home-built arrays follow the same arithmetic: eight LEDs putting about 12 W into an aluminium strip produced a 13 °C rise, implying roughly 1 °C/W for the strip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-50" title="EEVblog #50 - Solid State Cree LED Lighting, and How Thermal Design Sucks.">50</a></sup> Die temperature can be inferred from a measured heat sink temperature by adding the data sheet junction-to-solder-point [[resistance]] and the measured resistance from that point to the fins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Where a panel has no aluminium backing and no thermally conductive adhesive, the only path out is the board copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-916" title="EEVblog #916 - Work Bench Lighting From A Dumpster LCD TV">916</a></sup>

Digital parts are increasingly in the same territory. A single-board computer drawing about 3.7 W reached 90 °C on the [[processor]], close to thermal shutdown, making a stick-on heat sink mandatory rather than optional.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-883" title="EEVblog #883 - Orange Pi One vs Raspberry Pi 2">883</a></sup> Cluster builds glue a small heat sink to each board with thermal adhesive and rely on passive convection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-934" title="EEVblog #934 - Raspberry Pi Supercomputer Cluster PART 1">934</a></sup> Elsewhere a heat sink built into the lid presses down onto the processor beneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #858 - Red Pitaya Extended Cut">2</a></sup>

Thermoelectric generators invert the usual purpose: the heat sink is there to hold one plate anchored at ambient so that a temperature differential exists at all. Without an adequate sink on the cold side, both plates equalise and the generator produces nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> The [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] is bonded with thermal adhesive for good contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> The same mass effect appears at the bench, where a large copper plane or a power transistor tab acts as a heat sink that pulls heat out of the [[soldering-tip|soldering iron tip]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup> clip-on heat sinks are placed on component legs to protect sensitive parts during soldering,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup> and a heat sink bonded with thermal adhesive to a chip presents such a thermal mass that removing the chip requires hot air with a proper nozzle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Undersizing shows up immediately in temperature. A 5 W dissipation in one undersized regulator heat sink ran off the end of the manufacturer's own curve, producing roughly a 100 °C rise above ambient — over 90 °C measured through the case vents and well over 110 °C with the case off and no airflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> The fix was a larger heat sink matched to the same board footprint, so no layout change was needed beyond moving the capacitors clear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> Deleting a heat sink entirely is worse than the margin might suggest: a 7805 found with its sil pad still fitted but its heat sink missing overheated within seconds of power-up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup> A tiny surface-mount power brick with no heat sink at all reached die temperatures over 100 °C and tripped its own protection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup>

The neighbours suffer too. Electrolytic capacitors sandwiched between two heat sinks are heated by them, and heat is what shortens electrolytic life,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-52" title="EEVblog #52 - Panasonic Plasma TV&#x27;s Suck (and a Teardown)">52</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> which makes the [[capacitor]] nearest a heat sink the first suspect in a failure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> Orientation matters for the same reason — a board mounted so that heat rises past a capacitor is a bad design where the inverted arrangement would have been acceptable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup> And when reading thermal images of an assembly, the hottest pixel is not necessarily on the heat sink at all; a cut-out in the extrusion can expose something else behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pwS6HnM9PDo" title="The Rigol DHO800 Fan(boys) Go Wild">•</a></sup>
