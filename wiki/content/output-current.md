Output current is the [[current]] a supply, regulator, converter, or logic output can deliver into its load. It is the headline number on almost every power-related [[datasheet]] and the front-panel readout on almost every bench supply, and it is also one of the most conditional specifications in electronics: the same part quoted at 800 mA may manage only a fraction of that at the input [[voltage]] the design actually runs on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Almost every other parameter of a power device — [[efficiency]], [[ripple]], dropout, dissipation — is a function of output current rather than a fixed figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

## The headline rating is conditional

A single maximum output current on the front page of a datasheet is close to meaningless for a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]], because these parts are designed to work over a wide input voltage range and the current capability collapses at the extremes of that range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> A part may be able to deliver 2.5 A at a comfortable input voltage and nothing like it at a low one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The governing relationship is the differential between VIN and VOUT: as that gap widens, the current capability drops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> A [[boost-converter|boost converter]] whose input falls to 0.9 V while driving a large step-up ratio may be limited to 150 mA, and one advertised at 800 mA can fail to reach 500 mA at the required input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> At 0.9 V in for 5 V out, another candidate manages only about 100 to 150 mA, rising to perhaps 200 mA at 1 V in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> A wimpier device gives only 25 mA at 0.7 V in and under 100 mA even at 1.2 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Another does 50 mA at 5 V out from a 1 V input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

The practical consequence is that [[parametric-search|parametric search]] on output current is a trap. Filtering a distributor catalogue at the current the load needs excludes suitable parts, because the internal switch current in a boost topology must be considerably larger than the output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The usable approach is to search on output power and then read the maximum-output-current-versus-input-voltage and efficiency curves in each datasheet to see what the part really does at the operating point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Those curves carry their own trap: a family of efficiency-versus-output-current graphs may be plotted only for one [[output-voltage|output voltage]], such as 1.8 V, and says nothing about the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] actually being designed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Graph conventions also vary between manufacturers — some plot against output current, others against VIN — and interpolating between the 300 mA and 600 mA traces to guess 500 mA is often the only option available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

## Everything else is a function of it

Efficiency varies strongly with output current. A boost converter delivering only tens or hundreds of microamps — the regime of a remote control or similar low-drain product — may be running at around 50% efficiency, with some converters considerably worse, even though the same part achieves 85% at 1 mA to 100 mA under favourable conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Smaller magnetics both reduce efficiency and limit output current, which is the fundamental constraint on how much current can be squeezed out of a physically tiny converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Where a vendor declines to publish a maximum output current at all, the omission is itself informative.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

[[output-ripple|Output ripple]] is likewise a function of load. A charge-pump voltage [[inverter]] may show 25 mV of output ripple at 100 mA and several times that — 80 to 100 mV peak-to-peak against 20 mV — at a lower current or a different temperature, so a circuit with two distinct current modes can see its ripple change by a factor of four or five between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> [[power-supply|Power supply]] ripple and [[noise]] specifications rarely state the output current they apply at, and where no current is given the figure is conventionally taken to mean maximum output current or maximum output power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

[[dropout-voltage|Dropout voltage]] is a function of output current for every [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]], and datasheets normally tabulate it at several currents; a common jellybean part specifies up to 1.2 V of dropout at 800 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Dissipation tracks output current directly: a small buck module running 3 A out dissipates about 4.5 W at its worst point, with efficiency falling under 80% for the 5 V output from a 30 V input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> In converter design, the peak current through the [[inductor]] and [[diode]] is derived from the maximum output current — for a 100 mA output with the relevant on/off ratio, the peak works out at 0.74 A, which sets the diode rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

## Logic outputs: per-pin versus package limits

Digital logic datasheets specify output current in two places that must both be respected. The per-output absolute maximum may be ±25 mA, but the continuous current through VCC or [[ground]] for the whole package may be only ±50 mA, so a quad gate cannot source 4 × 25 mA — the total across all four outputs must not exceed 50 mA or something internal fails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Loading below the [[resistance]] implied by the continuous output current rating exceeds the absolute maximum ratings outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> Output high voltage is specified against output current, with separate VOH figures for sourcing 4 mA, 5.2 mA, and a very low 20 µA as when driving other gates, each in turn given at several supply voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

The same arithmetic governs [[microcontroller]] pins. Treating 20 mA as the per-pin limit is a working simplification rather than a hard threshold, but a design that turns all segments of a large display on at once must share that budget across the pins involved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> [[comparator|Comparator]] outputs are similarly modest: a typical jellybean part gives around 16 mA typical with no maximum stated, which is enough to drive a [[relay]] or a decent [[led|LED]] but no more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1464" title="EEVblog 1464 - TOP 5 Jellybean Comparators">1464</a></sup>

## Measured against claimed

Manufacturer claims for output current frequently fail under load. A model-aircraft regulator rated for 8 A to 15 A continuous depending on input voltage delivered 10 A of output current — 65 W output power — but did not meet its 15 A continuous claim at 16 V input, and could not reach it even momentarily, while performing acceptably at higher input voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Its wiring reflected the same confusion: heavy 16-gauge wire on the input, which carries lower current than the output in a step-down converter, feeding output connectors too small for the rated current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> A boost module claiming 350 mA managed that on its 3.3 V setting but not on 5 V, where the output sagged to 4 V from a 3 V input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup> An [[lm317|LM317]]-based supply built into a programmer is limited to about 150 mA of output current, matching the datasheet figures closely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

Current capability can also be limited by something other than the regulator. In a [[battery]]-leakage test rig, the controller's output current of a couple of hundred milliamps was not the constraint; the internal discharge [[transistor]] was, with no maximum current for it given anywhere in the datasheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup> A current-source application note built around a 2N2222 quotes a 35 mA maximum output that makes it unusable for a 1 A design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup>

## Devices and readouts

Ranges and applications span several orders of magnitude. A charge-pump inverter driven from a microcontroller pin, followed by a low-dropout regulator at 3.3 V, yields a clean supply good for a couple of hundred microamps of output current from a 3.64 V unregulated rail into a 10 K load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> An energy-harvesting chip with a 950 nA [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]] and an integrated full-wave bridge is rated up to 100 mA of output current, far more than a piezoelectric element will generate in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup> Thermoelectric generators are characterised by output current from the VOUT pin against temperature differential for each module size and [[transformer]] ratio; a 40 mm module at a 1:100 ratio should give on the order of 400 µA at a 10 °C differential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> Charge-pump voltage inverters top out around 200 mA in the high-current types, with most applications needing only milliamps or microamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> A linear regulator chosen for a bench supply design delivers up to 1.1 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> [[analog-multimeter|Analogue multimeter]] ohms ranges use two different battery voltages, the lower one supplying the higher output current needed for the low ohms range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup>

On bench supplies, output current is one of the primary displayed quantities alongside output voltage and computed output power, typically with three decimal places on current and milliwatt resolution on power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup> Good practice is for the display to show measured output current when the output is on and switch to the adjustable current setting when it is off, avoiding the need to short the terminals to set the limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup> Instruments should hold their output voltage and output current within specification after extended load testing rather than drifting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> At the high end, a laboratory supply may offer 20 A per channel and 800 W total across independent channels with remote sensing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> For a battery-powered instrument, the maximum output current capability — an amp, say — combined with the regulator topology determines how long the batteries last.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>
