USB Power Delivery ([[usb|USB]] PD) is the negotiation standard that lets a USB-C source and its load agree on a [[voltage]] and [[current]] far beyond the original 5 V bus, turning a single [[connector]] into a general-purpose DC supply for laptops, test instruments, and [[soldering]] irons.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> The negotiation happens over the CC1 and CC2 configuration pins of the USB-C connector, which run to a dedicated PD controller that settles how much power the host can deliver and how much the load needs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> It is not a set of fixed voltages in the way earlier charging schemes were: a compliant source can be programmed to essentially any voltage up to the compliant limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup>

## The negotiated envelope

The specification's ceiling is 48 V, and a well-behaved programmable supply will accept a request for 48 but reject 49.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup> At the bottom end it reaches down to 3.3 V, with 3.2 V refused and 2.5 V out of range, and the voltage increment is roughly 20 mV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup> The 3.1 revision added the high-voltage tiers — figures in the region of 24, 36 and 48 V — on top of the older fixed steps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1558" title="EEVblog 1558 - Mailbag">1558</a></sup>

Current is negotiable too. As well as adjustable voltage, the standard allows the [[current-limit|current limit]] to be set, in what appear to be 50 mA steps, although in practice power banks and bricks that actually expose that capability are essentially absent from the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup> The top of the range is a 240 W cable and supply combination at 5 A and 48 V, carried alongside 80 Gbit/s data on the same cable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1725" title="EEVblog 1725 - Mailbag: with special guest Sagan!">1725</a></sup>

Below that, the familiar fixed modes dominate real hardware. 100 W is reached as 20 V at 5 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> A 30 W [[soldering-iron|soldering iron]] gets there on the 12 V mode at 2.5 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup> Devices routinely negotiate 9 V, 12 V, 15 V, 20 V and 21 V depending on what the sink asks for and what the brick can supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> An [[oscilloscope]] powered over USB-C would light its front-panel [[led|LED]] on 5 V but could not actually run until it had negotiated 15 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup>

## Sink and source silicon

A PD sink is normally built around a dedicated negotiation chip. The RT1716 is a Richtek PD controller in a small [[bga|BGA]] whose CC1 and CC2 lines run to the USB-C connector; it does the hardware negotiation, is programmable, and presents an I²C interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> The CH224 is a USB power delivery fast charging protocol sink controller supporting PD 3.0 and 2.0 plus BC 1.2 negotiation, and can negotiate up to the full 100 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> Increasingly the function is absorbed into general-purpose parts: the STM32G0 value-line [[microcontroller]] integrates USB PD, which removes not just the separate controller but its pull-up resistors and decoupling caps from the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup>

Negotiation only sets the rails; the downstream converter still constrains what the product can output. A 100 W USB-C bench supply built on a plain buck converter can only produce about 400 mV below whatever PD has negotiated, and cannot exceed the input at all — reaching 30 V out from a 12 V input would require a SEPIC or another buck-boost topology.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup>

## Implementing it is hard

The engineering judgment that emerges consistently from designing with PD is that the standard is far more work than vendor marketing suggests. A promotional claim that a PD sink can be created in less than ten minutes does not survive contact with the actual silicon.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> Searching a microcontroller [[datasheet|data sheet]] for "USB" can return no mention of it at all despite USB PD being a headline feature, and the correct data sheet's dead [[battery]] section reads "The content of this section will be provided later."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> The reference libraries may have to be requested from the vendor directly, possibly for money.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> Manufacturer PD stacks are also large — a 32 KB flash part chosen as ample for PD configuration plus serial and HID comms proved marginal once the vendor library was included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup> Vendor libraries can carry undocumented registers, requiring separate spreadsheets from the manufacturer just to document them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>

Dave Jones's summary of the µSupply experience is that PD "implementing it correctly and thoroughly is pretty horrific experience", and that the negotiation swallowed an outsized share of that project's development effort alongside the [[processor]] isolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> The µSupply ended up with two microcontrollers: one on the isolated USB side handling PD negotiation, USB HID and serial comms, and a second running the [[lcd|LCD]], keypad and DAC-based supply functions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1264" title="EEVblog #1264 - uSupply Software Development Setup">1264</a></sup> Part of the PD code was bit-banged because the controller's I²C pins were shared with the programming interface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1264" title="EEVblog #1264 - uSupply Software Development Setup">1264</a></sup> Jones's blunt verdict on the whole family of fast-charge schemes is "Bloody temperamental charge and voltage standards."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup> He has also noted that PD negotiation is considerably easier now than when that design was started, with better solutions available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup>

## Interoperability failures

PD in the field is less uniform than the specification implies. Sources may be rated well below the standard's maximum, so a PD port is no guarantee of high power, and implementations are often buggy and not fully compatible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1375" title="EEVblog #1375 - Mailbag: IEEE">1375</a></sup> Sinks fail in the other direction: an 18 W-only power bank will not deliver more, and there are a lot of 18 W-only packs in circulation, so an iron capable of 30 W on PD silently drops to 18 W and loses thermal recovery performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup> A device may fall back to Quick Charge 2.0 instead of Type-C PD, and refuse to negotiate the higher voltage from a given pack at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup>

A sink that omits the CC pull-down resistors advertises nothing about its capability and will therefore not charge from a USB-C power bank, even though the same device charges happily from a dumb USB-A wall wart.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> Cables are a further failure point: a charge-only USB-C cable will pass current but neither data nor PD negotiation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1558" title="EEVblog 1558 - Mailbag">1558</a></sup>

## Why it is worth the trouble

Dedicated USB PD test hardware exists to make the negotiation visible — inline meters that display volts, amps and watts, automatically detect which protocols a source supports, and offer trigger modes to force a particular PD contract.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1549" title="EEVblog 1549 - Mailbag Teardown Art, USB Power, and Multimeter">1549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1319" title="EEVblog #1319 - NEW TS80P Portable USB PD Soldering Iron Review">1319</a></sup> These are how a design's negotiated rail is verified in practice: confirming a soldering station has taken the 21 V mode and is drawing 82 W, or that a scope has settled on 20 V and 56 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup>

The payoff is conversion [[efficiency]] and convenience. Powering a laptop directly from a battery station's USB-C output, rather than going out through a 240 V AC [[inverter]] and back in through the laptop's own mains brick, eliminates a full stage of conversion loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup> The same reasoning underlies programmable PD adapters that take a single PD brick and derive arbitrary voltages for barrel-jack and terminal-block loads, with keyed connectors so polarity cannot be reversed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1749" title="EEVblog 1749 - Werewolf VFLEX USB-C Power Supply Adapters - Very Cool!">1749</a></sup>
