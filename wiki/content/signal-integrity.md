Signal integrity is the degree to which a signal arrives at its destination with the shape it left with — whether the edges are clean or carry [[overshoot]], undershoot, [[ringing]] and settling artefacts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> It is a property of the interconnect and the probing, not of the logic: a bus can be functionally correct and still have terrible signal integrity, and it can have beautiful signal integrity right up to the point where the edge rate outruns the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup> It is one of the core competencies separating someone who can place parts on a board from someone who can design a high-speed board, alongside EMI, design for manufacture, and knowledge of how boards are actually made, assembled and tested.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-83" title="EEVblog #83 - Do You Suck At Hardware Or Software?">83</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup>

## When it matters

Signal integrity is a function of edge rate rather than clock [[frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-316" title="EEVblog #316 - More PFANG, More 13GHz Scope, &amp; More Pulser">316</a></sup> A design clocked at around a megahertz does not need to worry about it much,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> and a multi-layer board running a 68000 under 10 MHz with [[ground]] planes and abundant bypassing presents essentially no signal integrity problem at all — such a board can be handed to an auto-router without concern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-523" title="EEVblog #523 - REPAIR: HP 35660A Dynamic Signal Analyser">523</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> The same latitude applies to any board where the interconnect is deliberately not high speed: a board-to-board [[connector]] carrying a non-critical parallel interface can be built with signal integrity be damned and no controlled [[impedance]] whatsoever.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1239" title="EEVblog #1239 - Mailbag">1239</a></sup>

The concern rose with the interconnect. Older systems spread logic across large boards and backplanes, using one big [[ground-plane|ground plane]] and heavy decoupling; those traces were not controlled impedance and did not need to be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Modern high-speed serial lines, controlled-impedance routing and terminations changed that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> In-circuit emulator design became a particularly hard case, where flight time down the interconnecting cables alone can ruin a design, which is why the emulation [[processor]] is placed as physically close to the target connection pins as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Similar reasoning drove point-to-point [[coaxial-cable|coax]] runs inside older instruments when a signal could not survive the trip down an edge connector, through the motherboard and back up another board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup> The requirement is not exclusive to digital work: an analog delay line carrying colour video is judged as a [[transmission-line|transmission line]], with reflections and bounces mattering exactly as they would elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup>

## Board-level practice

The return path is the first-order concern. A ground plane that is not flood-filled under a trace forces the return [[current]] to take a longer route, and the consequence is blunt — the rule is never split your ground planes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> Losing the return path destroys the timing budget along with everything else: "You've ruined your timing budget. You've ruined your signal integrity. You've ruined everything."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> A circuit whose ground return is completed only through [[mains-earth|mains earth]] — out through the power board and back — will function for something as slow as a UART while its signal integrity is entirely compromised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup>

Where high-speed signal integrity is required on a multi-layer board, the standard construction is microstrip: the controlled-impedance trace on the top layer with the ground plane immediately beneath.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Differential pairs are length-matched, including small serpentine wiggles added to trim one leg of a pair against its companion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> Local removal of a plane is sometimes deliberate — scope input channels may have the bottom ground plane cleared beneath them for signal integrity reasons.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> A single-sided board carries a real performance penalty by comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup>

DDR memory layout illustrates the full set of effects at once. Intersymbol interference arises when the bus runs faster than the line can settle, so that residual energy from the previous symbol corrupts the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> Parallel buses routed side by side couple into each other, and crosstalk becomes serious in a way that simple crossing traces never are.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> Even the glass weave of the substrate matters: a trace running along a weave sees a different dielectric constant than one running across it, changing [[propagation-delay|propagation delay]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> The [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] is a case where signal integrity is decisive — its trace should be as wide as possible to reduce [[inductance]], and with a 0.5 V/ns edge rate, 50 mV of VREF [[noise]] translates into 200 ps of timing error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> Dave Jones's own DDR designs decoupled and [[inductor]]-isolated VREF heavily for this reason, and kept it spaced away from adjacent signals to avoid crosstalk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

Packaging and connectors contribute. A surface-mount pad is preferred over a through-hole lead where signal integrity is paramount, because the lead adds inductance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup> [[bga|BGA]]-mounted modules help for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-342" title="EEVblog #342 - Agilent 90000 Oscilloscope Teardown">342</a></sup> Through-hole pins are fatal for a millimetre-wave [[antenna]] array, where they cannot work at 28 GHz and beyond.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1369" title="EEVblog #1369 - Xiaomi Mi Air Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1369</a></sup> A zero-insertion-force BGA test socket inevitably introduces signal integrity issues that must be designed around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-532" title="EEVblog #532 - Silicon Chip Wafer Fab Mailbag">532</a></sup> Insufficient bypassing produces [[ripple]] on the supply rail with ramifications throughout — glitches on circuit transitions and ground bounce among them<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> — and bypass capacitors placed in a tidy row for appearance rather than adjacency are poor layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-936" title="EEVblog #936 - Mailbag">936</a></sup> A missing coupling [[capacitor]] on an auxiliary rail entering from a video connector is enough to warrant a bodge wire on production boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup> Placing large copper on the outer layers is thermally superior but trades against signal integrity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> EDA suites address this class of problem through simulation, impedance control and crosstalk analysis tools.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-968" title="EEVblog #968 - Mystery EDA Package">968</a></sup>

At the interface level, an input whose incoming clock quality is unknown — particularly one arriving from an external board — should at minimum be buffered by a Schmitt trigger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup> Capacitive loading of a driven line is a related failure: one module may be driven fine while daisy-chaining several introduces intermittent faults.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup>

## Probing

Probing is where signal integrity is most often lost, and in RF work probing is decisive — an antenna-like ground lead picks up enough rubbish to make a healthy design look broken.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-368" title="EEVblog #368 - EPIRB Teardown">368</a></sup> The ground lead is an inductor, and getting rid of the long clip lead is the first step above all others when measurements are meant to be taken seriously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> The same applies to logic analyzers, where short ground wires are essential and the probe set is the most valuable part of the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 - Part 2 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> A cheaply designed [[logic-analyzer|logic analyzer]] input is not a serious tool, because so much bears on it: noise, skew across channels, transition times, metastability, [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]] and inductance, rise and fall times, and crosstalk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 - Part 2 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> A logic analyzer also reports only a one or a zero against a fixed threshold and takes no account of signal integrity at all, so a poorly built circuit can produce plausible but wrong results and send an investigation chasing red herrings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MC9_4mpqVgU" title="eevBLAB #5 - Are Mixed Signal (MSO) Oscilloscopes Worth The Money?">•</a></sup>

Active FET probes put the buffer amplifier at the tip, which is how they reach roughly one picofarad of input capacitance; the shortest possible leads between probe and circuit remain necessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-309" title="EEVblog #309 - Mailbag">309</a></sup> They are the choice when signal integrity at high frequency genuinely matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup> A home-made resistive probe — a 1 kΩ [[series-resistor|series resistor]] into coax — can match the signal integrity performance of a multi-thousand-dollar, even ten-thousand-dollar, 10 GHz probe if built correctly, though there is considerable art in getting it right and characterising it requires the same class of equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> In one direct comparison the do-it-yourself resistive probe recovered from undershoot faster than a $2,500 active FET probe, at the cost of loading the circuit far more heavily.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup> Resistive probes are also more tolerant of longer ground leads than active FET probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup> Hands-free probing fixtures inherit the same [[grounding]] limitations as a conventional probe, but the tradeoff is usually worth it for debugging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup> Fixed ×1/×10 probes avoid the attenuation distortion that poor switch contacts and impedance mismatches in a switchable probe can introduce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LeVZ5n3K2io" title="eevBLAB 111 - The TMA Banning x1/x10 Switchable Probes?">•</a></sup>

The instrument itself is part of the measurement path. Enabling a second channel on a scope has been observed to visibly change the fidelity of the [[waveform]] on the first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Conversely, a higher-[[bandwidth]] scope is what reveals a termination or signal integrity problem that a lower-bandwidth instrument smooths away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> Averaging or high-resolution acquisition modes can clean up what the probe delivered, but proper grounding technique remains the better answer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Knowing when not to care

A consistent bench discipline is to distinguish measurements from presence checks. When the question is only whether a signal exists and is doing roughly the right thing, an awful ground path is acceptable — clipping to the chassis, relying on the mains earth connection shared between scope and equipment under test, or leaving deliberately long wires attached for handling convenience.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-879" title="EEVblog #879 - R&amp;S HMO1202 Scope Bandwidth Hack Investigation">879</a></sup> The overshoot seen through a hideously long ground lead is then simply ignored, because data presence rather than edge quality is what is being examined.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1320" title="EEVblog #1320 - Premature Oscilloscope Triggering">1320</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Short leads still buy confidence: when leads are already short, a suspect capture can reasonably be attributed to something other than signal integrity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1462" title="EEVblog 1462 - Why Dymo Label Printers SUCK!">1462</a></sup>

The corresponding hazard is diagnostic. Unpopulated termination resistors and a plausible signal integrity story can absorb an entire debugging session — timing diagrams, protocol analysis, careful probing setup — when the actual fault is a hardware failure on a clock pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> Signal integrity is a genuine explanation often enough to be a seductive wrong one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup>

## Measurement of signal integrity proper

The eye diagram is the standard instrument for characterising high-speed differential serial links: the data waveform overlaid repeatedly against a clock recovered from the signal itself, driving the horizontal axis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-340" title="EEVblog #340 - USB 3.0 Eye Diagram Measurement">340</a></sup> A clean eye reflects correct controlled-impedance routing, correct terminations, correct [[shielding]] and low clock jitter, and the demonstration cases represent an ideal a real system may never reach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-340" title="EEVblog #340 - USB 3.0 Eye Diagram Measurement">340</a></sup> Serious characterisation is slow work — setting up a single critical measurement to standard, over a specified cable length and into a specified load, can take days, and fully characterising a product against a standard such as [[usb|USB]] 3.0 can take weeks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-340" title="EEVblog #340 - USB 3.0 Eye Diagram Measurement">340</a></sup> Signal integrity remains the practical fix at that level too: a USB 3.0 link that would not work reliably was resolved by removing the series components on the USB signals and falling back to USB 2.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1496" title="EEVblog 1496 - Winning Mailbag">1496</a></sup>
