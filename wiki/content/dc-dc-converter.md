A DC-to-DC converter takes one DC [[voltage]] and produces another, using a switching element, an [[inductor]] or [[transformer]] as the energy storage element, and a rectifier and smoothing [[capacitor]] on the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Because the switch chops the input into an alternating [[waveform]] before it is stored and rectified again, a DC-to-DC converter is an AC circuit internally, however DC its terminals appear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> It is the near-universal way of generating supply rails in modern equipment: almost any product with a [[battery]], a processor, or more than one voltage rail contains at least one, and often a dozen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15" title="EEVblog #15 Part 2 of 2 - Fluke 189/289 multimeter review">15</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-851" title="EEVBlog #851 - Mailbag">851</a></sup>

## Topologies

The three basic arrangements are step-up (boost), step-down (buck), and inverting; they differ only slightly in circuit configuration, and the design formulas for each differ only slightly as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> A SEPIC converter accepts an input either above or below its output, which makes it attractive where a [[battery-voltage]] crosses the target voltage during discharge — for example charging a three-cell lithium-ion pack of over 12 V from a 5 V [[usb]] input, where a plain boost would not cover the whole range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup> The penalty is part cost: a SEPIC constant-[[current]] constant-voltage charger chip such as the LT1512 ran at $3.85 even in hundred-off quantity, which made it the most expensive semiconductor in the design it was considered for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

At its simplest the whole converter is a single [[transistor]] switch driving an inductor, a [[diode]], and a smoothing capacitor, with the IC controlling the switch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup> Constant-current output — as required to drive an [[led]] — is obtained by having the controller regulate against a [[current-shunt|current sense shunt]] rather than an output [[voltage-divider]], and the sense [[resistor]] is usually identifiable by its physical size.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup> Output voltage in a voltage-mode part is set by a feedback divider against an internal reference; for the MC34063 the relationship is 1.25 V times one plus R2 over R1, rearranged to R2 equals Vout over 1.25 minus 1, times R1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Other parts use different references — 1.245 V in one commonly used chip — and the same divider ratio therefore gives a different output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup> The upper feedback resistor can also be chosen to bound the maximum output a converter can reach, which is a useful safety feature when something else is modulating the feedback node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup>

## Switching frequency

[[frequency]] is a direct trade against inductor size: the higher the [[switching-frequency]], the smaller the [[inductance]] required and therefore the physically smaller the inductor, at the cost of higher switching losses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> The MC34063 tops out at 100 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Modern single-chip solutions switch up around a megahertz or more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> Converters are normally kept above the audible band, above 20 kHz and typically a couple of hundred kHz or into the megahertz region, precisely so they do not whine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup> Some do sit low enough to be heard: a faint high-pitched noise on power-up is audible evidence that a converter is running, and its absence is a diagnostic clue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup>

Not all converters run at a fixed frequency. Many vary switching frequency with output power in order to hold [[efficiency]] up at very low currents, and a converter may change conduction mode — from [[pwm|pulse width modulation]] to pulse frequency modulation — as the load changes, which shows up as a visible change in the shape and amplitude of the output ripple with load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

## Efficiency

Efficiency is output power divided by input power, and everything not delivered to the load is dissipated as heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1401" title="EEVblog 1401 - DC Fundamentals Part 6: DC Power, Efficiency, &amp; Maximum Power Transfer Theory">1401</a></sup> For ordinary designs the ceiling is around 90%: 85 to 90% is common, a good one reaches the 92 to 93% range, and even the best converters barely push 90% at a spot figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-734" title="EEVblog #734 - Giroptic 360cam Kickstarter Prototype">734</a></sup> A wide-input-range brick measured at 69.7 W in and 52.4 W out — about 75% — which left 17.3 W to be dissipated by a very small [[heat-sink|heatsink]]; at roughly 6 °C per watt of [[thermal-resistance]] the implications for temperature rise are immediate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Efficiency is optimised for one particular input voltage and one particular output power range, and outside that window whatever efficiency results has to be lived with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> In a product this is not a serious constraint, because the designer knows the load and sizes the inductor and switching transistors for it; on a universal supply module it is largely unavoidable without great complexity and expense.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup>

Gallium nitride devices change these numbers. Their very fast rise and fall times combined with high breakdown voltage allow extremely efficient converters, and high-power dense brick designs using them can exceed 99% efficient — which is why very small mains adapters can deliver 60 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o2NxHu5Bsnk" title="Discussion with Shahriar from The Signal Path">•</a></sup>

The switching element matters. A [[mosfet]] with a couple of ohms of on-[[resistance]], such as the 2N7002, is not suitable for a converter because too much power is dissipated in the switch; parts with far lower RDS(on) in the same SOT-23 package are the appropriate choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup> MOSFETs are generally preferred over BJTs as the switching element, and the advantage grows at high frequencies into the megahertz region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>

## Characterising a converter

Characterising a converter means measuring input voltage, input current, output voltage, and output current simultaneously, which is the standing argument for keeping four multimeters in a lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> An [[electronic-load]] substitutes for a bank of power resistors and allows the output to be swept from near zero up to full rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> Some loads add rise and fall time measurement, so the transient response of a converter can be captured without an [[oscilloscope]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup>

The output current axis should be logarithmic, because the interesting behaviour is spread over several decades.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Sweeping one converter from 100 µA to about 320 mA produced a curve above 80% from roughly 20 mA up to its rated 100 mA, never exceeding 90% at any point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Below about 100 µA there is no point continuing, because the measurement has reached the [[quiescent-current]] of the converter itself and that particular part is simply not optimised for low currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Deliberately driving past the rated output shows how the curve tails off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Alongside efficiency, switching frequency can be recorded by probing the inductor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup>

When testing an unknown converter, setting the supplying [[power-supply]]'s [[current-limit]] conservatively low can itself cause trouble: power-on surge current pushes the supply into current limit, the supply voltage collapses, and the converter misbehaves as a result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Characterising one brick properly, over every input voltage and every output current, is a matter of days or weeks, not an afternoon.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup>

Two measurements illustrate what the [[datasheet|data sheet]] does and does not tell. One boost part reached 96% maximum efficiency and operated down to 0.35 V input, but would not start below 0.65 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-320" title="EEVblog #320 - Mailbag Monday">320</a></sup> An isolated 2 W brick specified for a 4.5 V minimum input continued to deliver full output current down to about 2 V input, though once the output had collapsed it required the load to be removed and a minimum threshold to be re-established before it would restart.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup>

## Ripple, output capacitors, and stability

Ripple on a converter output is at the switching frequency — tens to hundreds of kilohertz, sometimes into the megahertz region — and tens to hundreds of millivolts is typical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1116" title="EEVBlog #1116 - How to Remove Power Supply Ripple">1116</a></sup> A worked design produced almost 200 mV peak-to-peak, roughly double the calculated target, which an added LC output filter reduced to around 20 mV per division on the screen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup>

Output capacitors are not free choices. Converters commonly specify a maximum ESR — 0.1 ohm is a typical figure — and sometimes a minimum or a window, because the loop stability depends on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Peak ripple currents in converters can be very high, and a single capacitor may not cut it; parallel capacitors spread the ripple current and the resulting temperature rise, and provide redundancy against a defective part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> The redundancy argument is real: an EV charger failed with one of two paralleled output capacitors measuring 260 µF against a 470 µF rating, the design margin having not been there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> A [[frequency-response]] analyser with a voltage injection transformer measures control-loop stability directly and lets the required output [[capacitance]] be calculated rather than guessed, for in-house converters and off-the-shelf regulators alike.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1103" title="EEVBlog #1103 - Omicron Labs Bode 100 Review &amp; Experiments">1103</a></sup>

## Selecting a part

Selecting a converter IC begins with the switching current the topology demands. As a rough rule of thumb the switch current is approximately Vout divided by Vin, times the output current — 5 V out from a 0.8 V minimum input at 0.5 A implies about 3.1 A, and relaxing the cutoff to 1 V input brings it to about 2.5 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> That number then drives a [[parametric-search]] through a distributor's switching-converter category, which runs to many thousands of parts, most of which operate at low voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> The graphs in the data sheet, not the headline numbers, are where the selection is actually made.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Parts with the diode built in lower the component count and cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup> Working through this for a single simple step-up converter takes far longer than the compressed account suggests, and it is what a design engineer spends a great deal of time doing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

The cheap end of the market is dominated by long-lived jellybean parts: the Motorola MC34063 is a classic and is how cost is driven out of low-end products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-411" title="EEVblog #411 - MiniPro TL866 Universal Programmer Review">411</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> At the other end, a step-up such as the Maxim MAX629 can on its own cost more from regular sources than an entire finished instrument built around surplus stock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> Integration continues to compress the physical solution — a buck IC can be embedded underneath the inductor so that the entire converter becomes a single pick-and-placeable part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup>

## Modules and bricks

Off-the-shelf converter modules take the guesswork out and save a great deal of design time, and the settled engineering judgment is not to roll your own where a module will do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1019" title="EEVblog #1019 - Mailbag">1019</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> A Texas Instruments PTH08T240 brick appears in a [[function-generator]] whose designers chose to spend their engineering time elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup> A source measure unit uses TDK Lambda 9 V in, 5 V out isolated bricks at around $16 each in tens, which is rational when a few tens of units are built at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> The same logic explains Linear Technology power modules in a high-end oscilloscope and up to nine converter bricks inside a logic analyser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> Boards needing very large currents on many low-voltage rails almost always use bought-in power modules.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1019" title="EEVblog #1019 - Mailbag">1019</a></sup> Bricks also solve retrofit problems: a 12 W module taking 5 V in and giving 12 V out, set by a single resistor, provides a rail that a product never had.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1515" title="EEVblog 1515 - Dumpster Tektronix TDS540D 500MHz Oscilloscope LCD Upgrade">1515</a></sup>

Isolated single-in-line bricks are a commodity item in industry-standard footprints from countless manufacturers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> A representative 2 W part offers 1000 V isolation between input and output, with higher ratings available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> Many such parts are unregulated, so the output voltage follows the input — perfectly acceptable when a regulator follows the converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup> Isolation is the reason they appear in handheld multimeters and oscilloscopes to separate the input section from the processor ground, and in RS-485 interface designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup>

## Batteries and constant-power loads

A DC-to-DC converter presents a constant-power load to the battery driving it, not a constant current or constant resistance one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> This is why [[battery-capacity]] is more usefully specified in watt-hours, and why the constant-power discharge curve is the right one to consult when estimating product [[battery-life]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-393" title="EEVblog #393 - LiPo Battery Discharge Testing">393</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-140" title="EEVblog #140 - Battery Capacity Tutorial">140</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> It also has an unpleasant consequence at low battery voltage: as the cell voltage falls the current must rise to maintain power, which snowballs the lower the voltage gets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

The distinction underlies a common specification abuse. A battery bank's advertised capacity is often input-referred — the internal cell voltage multiplied by the cell capacity — and therefore excludes the converter's losses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup> One 77 Wh-rated bank delivered 61.8 Wh at its output, implying an 80% efficient converter; what matters to the user is the output-referred figure, and only output-referred figures can be compared between products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1648" title="EEVblog 1648 - USB Battery Bank mAh Capacity ratings are a LIE!">1648</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

The same arithmetic disposes of clip-on boost converters sold to extract residual energy from primary cells. A product that cuts out at 1 V has already used about 90% of the cell's energy, and the best efficiency obtainable from an optimised converter is around 90%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Measured properly, a product with a 1.1 V cutoff drawing 100 mA gains at best just over 10% extra capacity before converter losses, which over the whole range can net out at zero or worse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> Drawing the real curve on real axes showed the available extra to be about 4% at that current, which is not worth the extra bill-of-materials cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-772" title="EEVblog #772 - How To Calculate Wasted Battery Capacity">772</a></sup> A converter small enough to fit inside an AA form factor is additionally constrained in output capacitance and peak current capability, which matters for loads that draw current in bursts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup>

Where a converter is genuinely necessary it is accepted as a cost. A [[multimeter]] running from AA cells needs a [[boost-converter]] to generate a diode-test voltage high enough for white LEDs or a 15 V diode mode, whereas a 9 V battery supplies it directly at the price of poorer capacity; two cells in series would sag below 15 V under load and would still need a boost converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> Some meters have no converter at all and run everything from four AA cells directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-868" title="EEVblog #868 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Torture Test">868</a></sup>

## Noise, layout, and system interaction

A converter is a noise source, and precision analog circuitry has to be protected from it. Switch-mode power supply sections are shielded in full metal cans in portable products specifically to stop high-frequency garbage escaping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-321" title="EEVblog #321 - Google Nexus 7 Tablet Teardown">321</a></sup> Placing an analog input connector in the middle of a group of converters forces extra board layers to route the signal through an internal channel away from the switching noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-810" title="EEVblog #810 - Micsig MS310 Handheld Oscilloscope Teardown">810</a></sup> Instrumentation with wide dynamic range needs many rails and therefore many converters, with the switching kept away from the measurement path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup> A converter running a Peltier element in a thermal chamber raised the [[noise-floor]] of a [[resistance-measurement]] noticeably.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-835" title="EEVblog #835 - Wekomm Resistance Standard Part 2">835</a></sup>

Post-regulation does not automatically fix this. A [[linear-regulator]]'s power supply rejection falls with frequency; one part offering 75 dB at 120 Hz manages only 20 dB at 1 MHz, which is exactly where a high-efficiency converter's switching noise sits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> Where the converter's noise cannot be rejected, it can sometimes be placed: some converter and [[inverter]] chips accept an external clock so their switching can be synchronised to an ADC's sampling clock, letting the sampling system reject those frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Alternatively the switcher can be used as a tracking pre-regulator that follows the linear regulator's output a fraction of a volt above it, so the linear stage stays efficient and needs only a small heatsink regardless of the output voltage set.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup> Such a tracking loop can be closed in software by measuring the output and adjusting a digital [[potentiometer]] in the converter's feedback path, or in analog with no software at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup>

Every additional rail costs money, board space, and bill-of-materials complexity — an [[fpga]] requiring a separate core voltage forces extra converters onto the board, and large FPGAs can need three, four, or half a dozen I/O voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> The converter's output voltage can also be made switchable: a MOSFET altering the feedback divider can raise the FPGA core rail to 1.5 V for JTAG programming and drop it to 1.2 V for operation to minimise [[power-consumption]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

## Failure modes and troubleshooting

The first check on a suspected converter is whether it is switching at all. Identical DC voltages on both ends of the inductor mean the switch pins are not pulling to ground and the converter is off — a conclusion reachable with a multimeter, without an oscilloscope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-366" title="EEVblog #366 - USB PSU Troubleshooting">366</a></sup> The same signature identifies a converter held off by an upstream MOSFET whose gate drive is missing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> Where a converter is a self-contained module, mains present on its input pins with nothing on its output condemns the module outright.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup>

Thermal imaging finds converters in distress quickly: on a failed compute module the converter was the hottest part on the board at over 100 °C, too hot to touch, alongside an overloaded Ethernet chip, and the fault in the load is a plausible cause of the converter's destruction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fpDnVSKfhew" title="Raspberry Pi Compute Module CM4 FAILURE">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-E1IqcGcZKHE" title="RPi CM4 Compute Module FAILURE Part 2: Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RjfStZa4Si8" title="AERL Battery Firmware Update">•</a></sup>

Mechanically, the converter's inductor is the largest and heaviest component on many boards and is the part most likely to shear off its pads in a drop test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-66" title="EEVblog #66 - Death &amp; Destruction of a Fluke Multimeter">66</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-868" title="EEVblog #868 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Torture Test">868</a></sup> Assembly errors have their own class: a missing inductor guarantees a dead converter, and a tall converter module can foul a display that the mechanical layer of the CAD drawing said would clear it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-357" title="EEVblog #357 - USB Supply Power-up Testing">357</a></sup>
