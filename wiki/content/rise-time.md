Rise time is the interval a signal takes to climb through a transition, conventionally measured between the 10% and 90% points of the edge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup> It matters for two reasons: it is the direct measure of how fast a circuit or instrument can actually respond, and through a simple formula it stands in for [[analog-bandwidth|analog bandwidth]], so an edge fast enough to outrun the equipment under test becomes a [[bandwidth]] measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-70" title="EEVblog #70 - Turn your Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope into a 100MHz DS1102E (Hack)">70</a></sup> In digital systems it is equally a constraint rather than a figure of merit — logic parts specify how slowly an input edge may transition before the part misbehaves, and an edge that is too slow is a real failure mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup>

## Relationship to bandwidth

There is a direct relationship between the rise time observed on an [[oscilloscope]], assuming a perfect input pulse, and the instrument's actual analog bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> For traditional Gaussian-response instruments — the older analog [[crt|CRT]] types — bandwidth equals 0.35 divided by the rise time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup> The same relation run the other way turns a fast [[square-wave|square wave]] into a bandwidth check: feed in a very fast edge, measure the rise or fall time, and divide 0.35 by it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-70" title="EEVblog #70 - Turn your Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope into a 100MHz DS1102E (Hack)">70</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-433" title="EEVblog #433 - Mailbag">433</a></sup> The technique works on analog instruments that have no [[function-generator|function generator]] fast enough to sweep them to their corner.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup> It was also the basis for characterising a home-built high-[[voltage]] probe, whose measured edge implied roughly 30 MHz of bandwidth even though the available [[oscillator]] only reached 3 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-85" title="EEVblog #85 - High Voltage Oscilloscope Probe Design">85</a></sup>

Modern digital oscilloscopes generally do not have a Gaussian rolloff, and a coefficient of 0.4 is used instead; for a 500 MHz instrument this predicts a rise time of 0.4 divided by 500 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Applying the Gaussian 0.35 factor to such a scope overestimates the bandwidth substantially — on one instrument by tens of megahertz at minimum, which is a significant error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> Which coefficient is correct depends on the particular instrument and the method it implements, and using the wrong one silently corrupts the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup>

## The pulse source and its contribution

A pulse generator never produces a perfect edge, so its own rise time contributes to what the oscilloscope displays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> The practical rule of thumb is that if the source is five times better than the rise time being measured, its contribution can be neglected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup> Classic avalanche-[[transistor]] pulsers of the Jim Williams type produce edges in the region of a few hundred picoseconds — 300 to 400 picoseconds is typical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-306" title="EEVblog #306 - Jim Williams Pulse Generator">306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup> Commercial parts go considerably faster: a compact pulse head specified at 40 picoseconds for 10 to 90% rise time, with 1 volt amplitude, is available at modest cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup>

Layout dominates at these speeds. Any lead has [[inductance]], so the generator has to plug straight into the instrument input, and the board traces themselves must be laid out properly or the edge is degraded and the [[waveform]] acquires [[overshoot]] and undershoot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup> Designing a very sharp edge is described as something of an art, and the avalanche transistor — a 2N2369 in the standard circuit — is not interchangeable between samples; parts are selected on test to find a good one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup>

Degrading the source demonstrates the point directly. Rebuilding one pulser with 35 cm of [[coaxial-cable|coax]] in place of the original [[capacitor]] gave 270 picoseconds rise and 1 nanosecond fall, good enough to serve as the reference for measuring every other instrument in the lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> With only the original 5 picofarad capacitor the same circuit gave about 295 picoseconds rise and roughly 600 picoseconds fall.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> A much longer coax run produced an edge in the region of 800 picoseconds to 1.4 nanoseconds and was useless for bandwidth work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> Fed through an attenuator, the reference pulser measured 254 picoseconds rise against 420 picoseconds fall.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup>

## Measured values across instruments

A 13 GHz, 40 GSa/s sampling oscilloscope was needed to see the reference pulser at all, and read about 288 picoseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> A mid-range instrument measuring the same source landed between 610 and 630 picoseconds, mostly around 630 — a figure that, punched into the 0.4 relation, gave a bandwidth estimate somewhat above the rated number.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> Amplitude that runs off the screen corrupts the reading: with the waveform partly off-screen the same edge read 590 picoseconds, and the whole waveform must be on screen for the measurement to be accurate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup>

Other measured edges: a 1 GHz instrument read 440 picoseconds when driven by the 40 picosecond pulse head.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> A 350 MHz instrument read 360 picoseconds on a single channel, but the reading degraded once a second or third channel was enabled and the [[sample-rate|sample rate]] dropped to 2 GSa/s.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> A hand-built handheld pulser generated 1.4 nanosecond rise and 1.9 nanosecond fall, sufficient to verify a probing system up to about 250 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-433" title="EEVblog #433 - Mailbag">433</a></sup> Built-in function generators are much slower — 18 nanoseconds rise and fall with 500 picoseconds of square-wave jitter is typical of a 20 MHz generator embedded in a scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> A [[multimeter]]'s square-wave output managed 500 nanoseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup> Where a retro instrument's function generator output degenerates at the top of its [[frequency]] range, its separate TTL output supplies the sharp edge instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup>

## Probes

The probe, not the oscilloscope, is usually the limiting element. A passive probe rated for 350 MHz in ×10 mode collapses to well under 10 MHz in ×1 mode, and the rise time specification shows the same collapse: 900 picoseconds at ×10 against 40 nanoseconds at ×1 on one probe,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> and 1 nanosecond at ×10 against 58 nanoseconds at ×1, with the ×1 bandwidth given as DC to 6 MHz, on another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> The specification is printed in the manual, so the difference is documented rather than hidden.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> Between two probes of the same family, near-identical [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]] — 17 pF against 16 pF — nevertheless corresponds to a 150 MHz versus 350 MHz rating and correspondingly different rise times.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup>

## Rise time in digital design

Logic datasheets specify an input transition rise and fall time, expressed as ΔT/ΔV: how fast the input signal is permitted to ramp, measured between the relevant threshold voltages rather than rail to rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-sr1DOHnJi8I" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 4 - Digital Logic Datasheets Explained">•</a></sup> The switching specifications elsewhere in the [[datasheet]] are themselves conditioned on a fast test edge; one part's numbers are all measured with a 6 nanosecond rise and fall pulse applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> Violating the input transition specification does not merely slow the circuit — it can drive the part metastable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

Open-collector outputs are the common source of trouble because the rising edge is produced by a passive pull-up charging the bus [[capacitance]], while only the falling edge is actively driven. An [[optocoupler]] used as a clock source illustrates the asymmetry: the output falls in 500 nanoseconds with the transistor pulling down, but the rise is far slower and is specified only for a nominal pull-up and load capacitance — 4.7 kΩ and 30 pF, roughly the input capacitance of a gate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> Adding devices to an I²C bus adds capacitance and lengthens the rise time on the low-to-high transition, which is why the usual 2.2 kΩ pull-up is sometimes reduced to 1 kΩ for faster operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup> These typical datasheet figures also shift with temperature and production spread, so a design that is already marginal on the nominal number has no margin at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

Fast edges are also deliberately created for measurement. A 1 MHz HCMOS oscillator with about 2 nanoseconds of fall time — and a comparable rise time — provides the sharp transition needed to make bypass-capacitor behaviour visible on the [[power-rail|power rail]]; the frequency is irrelevant, only the transition time matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> Conversely, a Schmitt trigger's tolerance of slow inputs is demonstrated by deliberately stretching the clock edge from around 45 nanoseconds out to 500 nanoseconds, 1 microsecond and beyond while the downstream counter continues to work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

## Deliberately slowing edges

Fast edges generate emissions, so many modern parts include slew-rate control on their outputs specifically to reduce EMC problems: instead of a really fast edge the output is slewed, and the emitted energy falls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup> This sits alongside the other standard mitigation, keeping [[current]] loops tight and short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-111" title="EEVblog #111 - Live Show #5">111</a></sup>

Slew limiting is also a designed characteristic in some equipment. A DC [[electronic-load|electronic load]] programmed at 10 mA per millisecond takes 200 milliseconds to complete its rise and fall, matching the programmed slope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-862" title="EEVBlog #862 - BK Precision 8601 DC Electronic Load">862</a></sup> A bench supply's output ramps up to 30 volts under gate control with no overshoot at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> In a linear supply, an [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]] in the control path shapes the output edge into a visible exponential, and shortening it produces a faster rise at the cost of the op-amp having to work harder to compensate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup>

## Instrument measurement functions

Rise time is a standard automatic measurement on essentially every [[digital-oscilloscope|digital oscilloscope]], sitting alongside period, frequency, width and [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]] in the horizontal measurement group.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> Quick-analysis modes display rise time, fall time, mean and peak values at once, though on some instruments enabling that mode restricts the analysis to a single channel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-793" title="EEVblog #793 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1002 Oscilloscope Hands-On">793</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Some instruments include on-screen help that explains, with example waveforms, what the rise-time measurement function actually does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup> Beyond simple measurement, rise and fall time variation can be used as a trigger and search criterion for hunting anomalies in a stored acquisition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup>

Even at ordinary timebases the concept is visible: a 1 kHz probe-adjust square wave rising within a single microsecond division is an edge no multimeter would ever show.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> Logic-analyser front ends without proper termination display visibly degraded edges, which is precisely why viewing the analog waveform alongside the digital decode is worth having.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup>

## Terminology note

Picoseconds are sometimes rendered verbally as "puff" — the same shorthand normally reserved for picofarads — as a matter of long habit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup>
