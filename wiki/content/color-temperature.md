Color temperature is the description of a light source's apparent whiteness expressed in kelvin, and for white LEDs it is set by the balance between the blue emitter's spike and the phosphor's re-emission across the rest of the visible spectrum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> It matters in two distinct ways: it determines whether a workspace is comfortable to work in, and it determines whether a camera set to a fixed white balance will record accurate colors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup> It is also one of the most commonly misstated numbers on [[led|LED]] lighting products, which is why measuring it rather than trusting the label is treated as standard practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup>

## Physical origin in white LEDs

A white LED is a blue emitter with a phosphor over it. The blue die produces a sharp spike, and the phosphor converts part of that energy into longer wavelengths; the shape of the phosphor emission — how far it extends toward the red — combined with the size of the residual blue spike is what sets the resulting color temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> A cool LED shows a large blue spike with comparatively little phosphor contribution, while a warm one, around 3,000 K, shifts the balance the other way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Spectra of nominal 6,000 K panels show this structure directly: a blue peak at 450 nm, a green peak at 532 nm, and a broad phosphor hump peaking around 600 nm in the yellow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-915" title="EEVblog #915 - Dumpster Dive LCD TV Salvage">915</a></sup>

Color temperature is independent of color rendering index, and the two are routinely quoted together on lighting products — a panel may be specified as 5K 90, meaning 5,000 K at CRI 90.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1303" title="EEVblog #1303 - New Lab LED Panels">1303</a></sup> Panels reaching quoted efficacies as high as 70 lm/W are generally doing so at an extreme 6,500 K with an overwhelming blue content.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup>

## Values in practice

For office lighting, task lighting, paperwork and electronics work, 4,000 K is widely regarded as close to ideal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Domestic lighting sits lower, generally between 2,700 K and 3,500 K with a common preference near 3,000 K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-361" title="EEVblog #361 - LED Ceiling Panel Lighting 101">361</a></sup> Studio and portrait photography and videography cluster higher, around 5,000 to 5,500 K, with 5,500 K being the most common rating for softboxes and similar studio fixtures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-764" title="EEVblog #764 - New Lab Ceiling LED Lighting Installation">764</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup> Adjustable desk fixtures commonly span the whole domestic-to-daylight range, 2,700 K to 6,500 K, by switching between banks of differently colored LEDs — orange and yellow emitters mixed in with white ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1286" title="EEVblog #1286 - Mailbag">1286</a></sup> Tri-color tube lamps work the same way, offering a switch that selects 3,000 K, 4,000 K, 5,000 K or 6,000 K by turning different color groups on and off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QxMmEAb2JME" title="Dungeon LED Light Upgrade">•</a></sup>

Fluorescent tubes carry the same specification; a daylight tube is 5,000 K, and quad-phosphor lab tubes are available at 5,000 K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Lrhee7hz53U" title="Dumpster Light Box">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-307" title="EEVblog #307 - Lab Lighting &amp; Measurement">307</a></sup> Older tubes with no color grading measured around 4,000 K, consistent with cool white 4,200 tubes that may also have drifted in color with age.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-307" title="EEVblog #307 - Lab Lighting &amp; Measurement">307</a></sup> Industrial high-bay LED modules are sold at 5,700 K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-773" title="EEVblog #773 - 80W INDUAL LED Light Teardown">773</a></sup> Architectural [[led-strip|LED strip]] is commonly 4,000 K.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1617" title="EEVblog 1617 - Architectural LED Lighting Build + Test">1617</a></sup> Single-die white LEDs used in tube retrofits, such as the Philips Lumileds Luxeon 4014 low-power part, are specified at 4,000 K with 80 CRI.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup>

Not every application cares. A general-purpose everyday-carry flashlight has no meaningful color temperature requirement, unlike a video light where both color temperature and CRI matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup>

## Matching a lab to a camera

The core discipline is that every light in a room is set to a single color temperature, and the camera's white balance is then fixed to that value rather than left on automatic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1451" title="EEVblog 1451 - How I Shoot EEVblog Videos">1451</a></sup> A lab standardized at 4,000 K keeps its camera permanently at 4,000 K; when the lights were later moved to 4,200 K, the camera followed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-336" title="EEVblog #336 - Drive Time Rant">336</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-764" title="EEVblog #764 - New Lab Ceiling LED Lighting Installation">764</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-414" title="EEVblog #414 - Apple Macintosh SE Teardown">414</a></sup> Standardizing on 5,000 K across ceiling panels, wall panels, softboxes and adjustable studio lights means the camera can be locked at 5,000 K and no white balance correction is needed in the edit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1451" title="EEVblog 1451 - How I Shoot EEVblog Videos">1451</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ddQF9U5CRNA" title="New Grey ESD Mat &amp; Camera Exposure">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QxMmEAb2JME" title="Dungeon LED Light Upgrade">•</a></sup> Working this way, high-CRI 5,000 K studio LEDs are also reused in ancillary fixtures such as a magnifier lamp and a homemade photography light box so that everything stays matched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RT3godBXkOg" title="Magnifying Lamp Sony Battery LED Hack">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6ZH2KeplSrs" title="DIY Product/PCB Photography Light Box - Part 3">•</a></sup> The same fixed white balance is what allows a monitor under test to be judged as genuinely blue-tinted rather than mis-rendered by the camera.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1758" title="EEVblog 1758 - A Greek Dumpster Tragedy">1758</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OdovWOP7ik4" title="OF COURSE this happened">•</a></sup>

Where a fixture is replaced, matching drives the choice of replacement as much as brightness does. Existing 4,000 K tubes were deliberately paired with 4,000 K LED replacements to avoid mixing them with 5,000 K tubes elsewhere in the room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-531" title="EEVblog #531 - LAB LED Fluoro Lighting Tube Replacement">531</a></sup> A later upgrade went the other way, replacing 4,000 K battens with 5,000 K units specifically so the whole space would be color-matched, gaining output from 2,200 to 3,300 lumens per fixture in the process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QxMmEAb2JME" title="Dungeon LED Light Upgrade">•</a></sup> When a 4,200 K panel was unavailable and only 4,000 or 5,000 K were offered, 5,000 K was chosen because it sits nearer the daylight photography range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-764" title="EEVblog #764 - New Lab Ceiling LED Lighting Installation">764</a></sup>

Mismatch is the failure mode this discipline exists to prevent. Running a mixture of color temperatures in one room makes accurate color reproduction impossible and shows up on camera as a persistent cast.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1173" title="EEVblog #1173 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz Power Supply Bonanza">1173</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup> Perceived brightness is also colored by color temperature, so lighting changes are not judged on lux alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup>

## Measurement and mislabelling

Advertised color temperature is frequently wrong. Panels sold as 5,000 K repeatedly measure otherwise, to the point that finding a genuine 5,000 K ceiling panel is difficult — suppliers overwhelmingly stock 4,000 K or 6,500 K daylight, and 5,000 K is something of an oddball value in that market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1303" title="EEVblog #1303 - New Lab LED Panels">1303</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup> Concrete cases: strip lights advertised at 5,500 K measured 6,400 K in one installation and 6,800 K in another; a 48 W panel specified at 5,500 K came in around 6,700 K; and nominal 5,000 K T8 tubes measured out of spec.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup> By contrast, tubes claimed at 4,000 K have measured about 4,200 K, which is close enough to be usable when mixed with other lab lighting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-531" title="EEVblog #531 - LAB LED Fluoro Lighting Tube Replacement">531</a></sup> The engineering conclusion is blunt: LED lighting specifications should not be taken on trust.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1343" title="EEVblog #1343 - DEAD Deck Cree LED&#x27;s">1343</a></sup>

Two measurement approaches are used. The cheaper one is a professional camcorder's manual white balance function against a proper white balance card, which returns the scene color temperature in kelvin directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p4cK-Cks0NQ" title="Got Duped On LED Lighting - TWICE!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1206" title="EEVblog #1206 - Repair: Sony NEX VG30 Video Camera">1206</a></sup> The more capable one is a dedicated spectrometer such as the HP 320, which reports color temperature in kelvin alongside CRI, lux, chromaticity coordinates and the full spectrum with its peak wavelength.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup> Without a specialized instrument, the actual color temperature and CRI of an unknown light source are simply unknown.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup>

## LCD backlights

[[lcd|LCD]] backlights are a deliberate exception to normal color temperature practice. Because the panel's red, green and blue color filters subtract heavily from the [[backlight]], the backlight itself is specified at an extremely high color temperature — in the region of 12,000 to 13,000 K — so that the filtered output lands on accurate screen colors, prioritizing color gamut over the raw spectrum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup> A backlight salvaged from a dumpster-found LCD television measured 12,569 K with a CRI of 76.5, which makes such assemblies unsuitable for repurposing as room or work lighting despite their high output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1700" title="EEVblog 1700 - Do Dumpster LCD TV&#x27;s Make Good LED Light Panels?">1700</a></sup>

## Related specifications

Color rendering index accompanies color temperature on lighting specifications and is a separate quantity: greater than 80 is common and unremarkable, while high-CRI studio LEDs reach 90 or even 98.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QxMmEAb2JME" title="Dungeon LED Light Upgrade">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1303" title="EEVblog #1303 - New Lab LED Panels">1303</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WW_-Clzs2Bo" title="Dungeon Benches and Lighting">•</a></sup> For general lab use where video is not being shot, CRI can reasonably be ignored while color temperature is still checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1252" title="EEVblog #1252 - LED Panel Lighting Flicker Investigated">1252</a></sup> Unified Glare Rating is a third figure on panel datasheets, running from 0 to 40 with lower being better; a UGR below 19 marks a low-glare panel, which reduces reflections on products under test and eye fatigue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1303" title="EEVblog #1303 - New Lab LED Panels">1303</a></sup> Illuminance is separate again — office standards call for something like 300 lux minimum at the bench, while over 1,000 lux is needed for usable video.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QxMmEAb2JME" title="Dungeon LED Light Upgrade">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1286" title="EEVblog #1286 - Mailbag">1286</a></sup>
