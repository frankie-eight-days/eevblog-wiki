A precision resistor is a [[resistor]] bought for the tightness of its tolerance and the stability of its value rather than for its nominal [[resistance]] alone, with grades running from 0.1% down through 0.01% to 0.005% and better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup> It is the component that sets the accuracy of measurement instruments, [[current]] sources, [[voltage]] dividers and [[calibration]] standards, because in those circuits nothing downstream can be more accurate than the resistor the signal is developed across.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> It is also, for its size, one of the most expensive passive parts on a board: a single fine-tolerance resistor can cost several dollars even in thousands, against fractions of a cent for an ordinary one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup>

## Tolerance versus temperature coefficient

Absolute tolerance and [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] are separate specifications, and which one matters depends on whether the circuit can be trimmed or calibrated. Where a trimmer or a software calibration exists, initial tolerance is nearly irrelevant and tempco is everything: a [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] 10% off nominal is perfectly usable provided its value does not move with temperature, mechanical stress or time, because the offset can be calibrated out once and then stays out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> The same logic explains a 1% part sitting in a high-end [[multimeter]] front end — it is there as a low-tempco resistor that the instrument software calibrates around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup> Conversely, ranges with no trimmer must carry the tolerance in the part itself, which is why 0.1% resistors appear on a microamp range while the trimmed milliamp shunt beside them is only 1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

The extreme case of this is a nominally loose part chosen entirely for its stability. Prema instrumentation used resistors specified at only 1%, but with practically zero tempco and zero drift, reportedly costing a couple of hundred dollars each, in a unit whose overall drift is 1 ppm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup> Circa-trim IRC 10K and 5K parts marked ±5% appear in temperature-stabilised assemblies for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1126" title="EEVblog #1126 - Mystery Teardown!">1126</a></sup> Where ordinary resistors are used with a trimpot in place of precision parts, tempco is the thing that has to be watched, because trimming corrects the value once and does nothing about how it moves afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

## Grades, values and prices

A 0.1% resistor is cheap enough to specify as a baseline: an entire design can be built to roughly 0.1% simply by buying 0.1% parts wherever the tolerance matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> A 0.01% part can be had from a distributor for a few dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> Reels of 10 ohm 0.05%, 10 ppm parts exist but are not always in stock, and committing to a production run has meant ordering a thousand of them when no other source in the world had them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup>

At the top of the practical range, a Vishay 10 k resistor sells for around $20 and is specified at ±0.005% (50 ppm) absolute at 25 °C, ±0.2 ppm/°C tempco, with a further 0.005% or 50 ppm of drift over its load life; the matching 1 k part is 0.01%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup> Bench-grade reference resistors of this class are typically kept as a 10 k at 0.005%/50 ppm and a 1 k at 0.01%/100 ppm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> Genrad decade box elements have been found marked to 0.008%, which on a 10 k element amounts to 0.8 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup>

Not every position needs the best grade. In a $35,000 programmer, 0.5% parts are adequate where the requirement is only that a rail land within a tenth of a percent or so.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> In a resistance chain, the top decades carry 0.01% parts while the bottom decades are only 1%, since a low-order decade contributes too little to the total to justify the tolerance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup> The same grading appears in a DC voltage standard, where the top elements are 0.005% and the first decade is 0.02%, each one trimmed to its actual value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

## Low-value shunts

The genuinely hard part to buy is a low-value [[current-shunt|current shunt]] at a tight tolerance. A 10 milliohm shunt at 0.1% costs upwards of $4 per resistor even in volumes of thousands, and getting anything better than typical 1% tolerance in a low-value shunt is difficult and expensive to source at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> One workaround is to lay out ten half-watt footprints in parallel and rely on the statistical distribution of the individual parts to give a combination tolerance better than that of any single resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

Where the budget allows, specialist manufacturers will sell a 0.01% 10 milliohm resistor, and such a part is normally used four-terminal, with the two inner pins tapping the resistive element itself so that lead and [[solder]]-joint resistance falls outside the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> Four-terminal 0.1 ohm 0.1% shunts appear in older instrument front ends.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup> A four-terminal 1.25 ohm 0.02% part is good enough to give a clean Kelvin measurement even when the circuit around it is built on a [[breadboard]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

## Setting a current or a voltage

The canonical use is a precision [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] driven across a precision resistor, which by Ohm's law produces a precise current: 1.25 V across 1.25 ohms gives exactly 1 amp, and the accuracy of the source is the error budget of just those two parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> The same pairing turns a bench voltage reference into a current standard cheaply enough to be a practical way of checking a multimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> A precision 10 K resistor used this way, with 10 V developed across it, has been measured to land within 0.008% of target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup>

Such a circuit still has to be analysed for current that does not flow through the resistor. In one 1 amp source, a precise 1.25 V really was present across a precise 1.25 ohm resistor, and 1 amp really did flow through it, yet a separate 2 milliamp error current returning to [[ground]] meant the load did not see 1 amp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> [[thermal-management|Thermal management]] matters as well: the precision resistor in that source does not strictly require a [[heat-sink|heat sink]], but fitting a small one as a matter of course is sound practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

References below about 1.2 V cannot be made directly, because band gap physics sets a floor, so a lower reference is produced by dividing a higher one down with 0.1% resistors and buffering the tap with an op-amp follower — or by dividing with ordinary resistors and trimming, at the cost of having to watch the tempco.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Where a divider is fed from panel-meter-grade circuitry, ordinary 1% resistors are enough to introduce a 16 mV offset that has to be trimmed out with an added series part before the display reads zero for zero input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-102" title="EEVblog #102 - DIY Constant Current Dummy Load for Power Supply and Battery Testing">102</a></sup> Avoiding ranging altogether sidesteps a good deal of this: a fixed 10 V range removes the need for precision ceramic divider networks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> A strain gauge bridge, by contrast, cannot avoid it, needing a high-stability voltage reference and high-stability bridge resistors — junky ones will not do — feeding a high-precision [[differential-amplifier|differential amplifier]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup>

Precision parts occasionally appear outside instrumentation for identification rather than measurement, as in the centre-pin resistor of a laptop power adapter, where the value must be right for the machine to accept the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1279" title="EEVblog #1279 - Best Dumpster PC Find Yet!">1279</a></sup>

## Inside instruments

A resistance calibrator such as the Fluke 5450A is, functionally, nothing more than a set of precision resistors in a box with automation around them; it measures nothing itself, and provides two- or four-terminal connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup> Its resistors carry Fluke part numbers and appear to have been made in-house, including a 40.5 k at 0.005%, and the technology behind manufacturing them is as much of the design as the analog section that switches them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup> Recreating such an instrument today would change only the sourcing — the custom Fluke resistors would be ordered from a distributor at $20 each — while the terminal compensation, the low-leakage relays and the rest of the analog design would be essentially unchanged, with the low-range elements possibly still hand-wound to reach the required performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-545" title="EEVblog #545 - Vintage Design Rant">545</a></sup>

Decade resistance boxes follow the same pattern. A commercial decade box rates each internal element at 0.5 W, which rises to a maximum of 2.5 W when several are dialled in series, and specifies accuracy as ±0.1% plus a fixed 0.036 ohm offset present even at a dialled setting of zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup> Genrad elements are individually wound and hand-tweaked at the end of manufacture.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup> In a Wheatstone bridge instrument, the dial readings are only meaningful because the arm resistors are precise and individually trimmable; once the bridge is calibrated the device under test is read straight off the dials at balance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup>

Kelvin-Varley and Kelvin dividers put a constraint on the lowest resistance in the chain: keeping the smallest element at 1 K rather than something much lower means switch [[contact-resistance|contact resistance]] is nowhere near large enough to matter, so the divider can be manufactured without worrying about contact resistance at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup>

Analog multimeters are built almost entirely from precision resistors, in some cases hand-wired directly onto the [[range-switch|range switch]] with no [[pcb|PCB]] at all, each resistor in its own moulded spacer with walls between.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup> Their values are not E12 or E96 series but obscure figures calculated specifically for the required range dividers, and grades around 0.5% with very low ageing are what gives such a meter its accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1013" title="EEVblog #1013 - Mailbag">1013</a></sup> Old reference-resistor assemblies of around half a percent appear on turret and panel-board construction of the same era.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup>

Thick-film hybrid precision resistor networks are the mark of a high-accuracy digital meter's front end; their absence is expected in a budget instrument and notable in one claiming 0.025% class accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup> A discrete thick-film hybrid precision resistor of around 20 megaohms is an unusual thing to find where no hybrid network is otherwise present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup> Thin-film precision networks work in essentially the same way as an ordinary 0805 thin-film [[surface-mount-technology|surface mount]] resistor, with a serpentine trace pattern visible internally, and a discrete precision Vishay part is manufactured very similarly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-730" title="EEVblog #730 - Thin Film Resistor Networks">730</a></sup> Manufacturing them by the older method meant coating a ceramic body with metal powder and cutting a helix of metallisation with a micro lathe, reliably reaching ±0.1%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup> In 1968 Standard Electric Lorenz of Stuttgart required 5,000 precision resistors of 1/20 watt, 1 kilohm, 0.1% tolerance for a spacecraft, ordered through TRW and manufactured by the Ingstrom Corporation of Van Nuys, California.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BUW6h88weXU" title="History of ESD with Dan Anderson - 1080p Restored footage">•</a></sup>

Improvements in off-the-shelf parts have removed one historical reason for analog ASICs: matching of resistors and switch resistance that once required a custom IC can now be bought as a 0.01% resistor and a modern switch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Bench standards and calibration

A small set of precision resistors, typically a 10 k and a 1 k, functions as a working resistance standard for checking meters, and a homemade box containing two such parts of 0.01% and 0.005% class serves the purpose adequately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup> Keithley's recommended set for generating currents from an applied test voltage is 10K, 10 meg and 100 meg, mounted inside an earth-shielded box.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup> Held against such a standard, a good handheld meter reads spot on to nearly the least significant digit, and holds that reading with the meter heated to 38 °C and beyond in a thermal oven.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> The same standard resistors are the routine check for repaired and reviewed instruments, and for testing whether a meter survives [[mains-voltage|mains voltage]] applied to its ohms range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-556" title="EEVblog #556 - Mailbag">556</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1424" title="EEVblog 1424 - Fluke 23 Multimeter Repair">1424</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-252" title="EEVblog #252 - Multimeter Ohms Overload">252</a></sup> They are also a diagnostic tool: connecting a known precision 10 k to a suspect meter and getting readings that change when the range switch is fiddled — including impossible negative ohms — localises the fault to the switch contacts rather than the analog circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup>

Precision resistors used this way, together with a voltage standard, are what allows manufactured units to be cross-checked against each other and programmed pass-fail limits to be set for [[production-testing|production test]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ux7WdK6oym4" title="uCurrent GOLD Kickstarter First Manufacture">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-576" title="EEVblog #576 - Advantest R6142 Current Voltage Generator">576</a></sup> On an automated test fixture the expense is not a concern, since only one such board is ever built, so a real expensive voltage reference and real expensive precision resistors are the obvious choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## Measuring and buying them

Verifying a low-value precision resistor demands more care than the resistor's own tolerance. For a 0.1% part of 10 ohms, the tolerance band is 0.01 ohms, whereas ordinary test leads contribute around 0.29 ohms of residual resistance — so lead resistance must be zeroed out before the reading means anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> A precision part carries a marking with the extra digit of resolution its tolerance implies, such as 10R0 on a 10 ohm part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> Confirming that the right part is installed can also be done functionally, by driving a known current in and checking the output: 99.7 nA out for 99.9 nA in is not a 0.1% verification but is enough to show the correct value resistor is fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup>

In procurement, precision resistors are the parts to watch. They are not sold at fractions of a cent, so partial reels have to be re-reeled by the distributor to avoid buying a full one — and re-reeled components cause their own feeder problems on a pick-and-place machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup> Assemblers normally ask for a few percent of extra parts to cover pick-and-place losses, which is trivial for jelly-bean passives but not for loose precision resistors at $4 or $5 a piece in quantities of a couple of thousand; it is worth asking the assembler to be extra careful with the expensive parts specifically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-m9tza_c4sxc" title="uCurrent GOLD Assembler Parts Shipping">•</a></sup> The same arithmetic applies to package choice, since a 5–10% wastage allowance on a dollar-apiece high-precision low-tempco resistor in a very small package adds materially to assembly cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> In hand assembly, the precision parts are placed first along with the other small components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-562" title="EEVblog #562 - More SMD Oven Reflow">562</a></sup>

Where the tolerance is not needed, it is not spent: instrument boards routinely mix precision resistors with ordinary carbon parts in the positions where tempco and precision are not critical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup>
