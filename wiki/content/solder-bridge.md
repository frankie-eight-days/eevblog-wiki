A solder bridge is a continuous path of [[solder]] spanning two adjacent pads or pins that should remain electrically separate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> On a fine-pitch package it is the dominant hand-assembly defect, and it is also the dominant defect the board designer is expected to design against, since the fix for most bridging is in the layout rather than in the operator's technique.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> The same physical thing, made deliberately, is a standard configuration and repair element: a blob of solder placed across two pads to close a link, select an address, or reroute a track.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup>

## Why bridges form

Bridging is fundamentally a question of whether molten solder has anywhere better to go than the gap between two pins. Excess [[solder-paste|solder paste]] is the simplest cause -- too much deposited on the pads and adjacent pads short together on reflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> In [[hand-soldering|hand soldering]], dragging a loaded tip along a row of pins leaves shorts wherever the tip carried more solder than a single joint needed, and this happens even to experienced operators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup>

The structural cause is a missing or inadequate [[solder-mask|solder mask]] dam between pins. Where a manufacturer omits mask between the individual pads of a fine-pitch part, there is nothing to stop paste from two pads merging, and a bridge across the row is the expected outcome rather than an accident.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> A dam that is present but too thin fails in two ways: it may not adhere to the fiberglass and simply peel away, and on close pin pitches it is too small to arrest the solder even when it survives -- some solder mask is better than none, but not by much once it is thin enough.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1353" title="EEVblog #1353 - WHY Are These Pins Shorted?">1353</a></sup> Design tools can produce the same condition without the fabricator being at fault: an incorrect [[solder-mask-expansion|solder mask expansion]] on a quad flat pack's pads yields one solid mask opening over the whole row instead of a gap per pin, and [[soldering]] such a board bridges the pins together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-255" title="EEVblog #255 - DIPtrace PCB CAD - First Impressions">255</a></sup>

[[wave-soldering|Wave soldering]] adds a directional cause. As the wave crosses a package it drags solder across the pins much as a chisel tip does, and at the trailing end of the row -- the last two pins to leave the wave -- the accumulated solder has nowhere to shed and shorts across.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> Components must be glued down for the process to work at all, and the pad geometry is what prevents solder dags forming between pads as the wave passes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

## Design countermeasures

Correct solder mask between pins is the primary defence, and on a properly masked board a stencil-and-reflow cycle can produce a full fine-pitch row with no bridges and every pin wetted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> A bridge that does form in paste will often pull itself apart as the solder reflows and surface tension draws it onto the pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>

For wave soldering, the countermeasure is a solder thieving pad: an oversized pad placed past the end of the pin row, so that solder preferentially flows to and stays on the larger pad rather than shorting the last two pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> Because the effect is directional, thieves are only strictly needed at the trailing end for a known travel direction through the machine, but they are conventionally placed at both ends so the panel can be run either way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup> Slightly enlarged pads at the end of a chip serve the same function in reflow, catching the solder so it does not form vortices and short out adjacent pins, and peeling it away from the pin before it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-856" title="EEVblog #856 - Mailbag">856</a></sup> The resulting asymmetry is diagnostic after the fact: extra solder on the pins at one end of a package indicates which way the board travelled through the wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

Carrier substrates can address the problem mechanically. A grooved or well-based pad system confines the solder to each pin's channel, so drag soldering along a 0.4 mm pitch row produces no bridges and a consistent solder volume per pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-408" title="EEVBlog #408 - Schmart Board 0.4mm QFN SMD Soldering">408</a></sup>

## Technique and rework

Where drag soldering along a row risks shorts, dragging outward from the pins instead of along them helps prevent individual shorts between adjacent pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-434" title="EEVblog #434 - SMD Thermal Pad &amp; Drag Soldering Tutorial">434</a></sup> A bridge that does occur is not a serious defect, since it is removable: [[solder-wick|solder wick]] lifts it away, with fine-gauge wick appropriate to fine pitch, and a wicking tip does the same job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> Reheating between the two shorted pins and drawing the iron out through the middle will also collect the excess.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EjpUYGRXzRQ" title="Sagan Soldering!">•</a></sup> On packages with no mask between pads, a bridge formed during reflow is anticipated and simply cleaned up afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup> Rework under a camera is materially harder than at the bench, and a practice run before committing to an important board is worthwhile regardless of experience.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup>

## Detection

Small bridges are found visually, often as an incidental observation while inspecting an assembled board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> Under high magnification on a hot air rework job, bridged solder balls between adjacent pads are directly visible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1659" title="EEVblog 1659 - Quick 861 Pro Hot Air Station REVIEW">1659</a></sup> In diagnosis of a dead board, a stray solder blob is one of the standard suspects for an unexplained short, alongside failed devices and shorted bypass capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup>

Electrical detection at production scale is what JTAG boundary scan is for. Writing a one to a pin and reading it back detects a short to [[ground]] -- a pin that always reads zero is grounded -- and driving one pin while reading another detects a bridge between them, with the combinations chosen according to the board layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-499" title="EEVblog #499 - What is JTAG and Boundary Scan?">499</a></sup>

## Deliberate bridges

Solder bridges are routinely used as a zero-component configuration link. Rows of paired pads on an instrument board, marked with abbreviations for the function they select, are bridged to set a board's address so identical boards can be stacked in a system -- a crude technique, but not an uncommon one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup> A prototyping arrangement can bring a [[microcontroller]] pin to a pad set where it is bridged either high or low, with an optional [[series-resistor|series resistor]] and a 0.1 inch header on the same node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> A supply rail run as a loop around the edge of a breakout board with bridge pads along it lets any I/O pin be tied to 3.3 V at will.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1410" title="EEVblog 1410 - Mailbag">1410</a></sup>

Matrix prototyping boards invert the stripboard convention: pads default to disconnected rather than connected, and any two points are joined with a small solder blob across a gap of a few thousandths of an inch, with traces running vertically, horizontally and diagonally so connections can be made in any direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup> The result behaves like routing cells in an [[fpga|FPGA]] fabric.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup>

In modification and repair work, pads placed on the top or bottom of a small mod [[pcb|PCB]] are simply bridged onto the target with solder, a technique on equal footing with castellated edge holes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1158" title="EEVblog #1158 - How To Create PCB Mod Boards">1158</a></sup> Where a track is damaged, scraping back the solder mask and bridging across the break is a legitimate repair, ideally reinforced with [[adhesive]] to make the joint mechanically robust.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1727" title="EEVblog 1727 - REPAIR: Casio CT-X700 Keyboard">1727</a></sup> Bridging individually to each conductor of a damaged flexible [[connector]]'s traces is possible after scraping the coating off, though only worth attempting when getting the unit running again is essential.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-o4S-TkTuHZs" title="Samsung Galaxy Tab A Troubleshooting">•</a></sup>

The deliberate and accidental cases are close enough in appearance that a bridge across a track can be presented, straight-faced, as a manufacturing feature added to increase [[current]] handling capacity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup>
