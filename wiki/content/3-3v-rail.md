The 3.3 volt rail is the default supply [[voltage]] for digital logic in contemporary electronics, to the point that almost every board of any complexity carries one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TE3il-V6XCE" title="This is NOT a repair video">•</a></sup> It powers microcontrollers, memory interfaces, processors and general-purpose digital sections, and on a board whose [[silkscreen]] carries no rail markings its presence can be assumed rather than deduced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TE3il-V6XCE" title="This is NOT a repair video">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup> Because it is so nearly universal, the 3.3 V rail is also the rail most often probed first during fault diagnosis, the one most often given a dedicated copper plane, and the one whose failure most often takes a product down entirely.

## Where the rail sits among other supplies

A 3.3 V rail rarely appears alone. The commonest arrangement pairs it with 5 V, with 5 V serving legacy peripherals, displays and analog sections while 3.3 V feeds the digital logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-888" title="EEVblog #888 - Mailbag">888</a></sup> Alongside a high-density VLSI [[asic|ASIC]] or [[fpga|FPGA]], 3.3 V typically serves the I/O ring while a lower core rail — commonly 1.2 V — supplies the die itself, so finding 3.3 V and 1.2 V together on the same board is unremarkable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TE3il-V6XCE" title="This is NOT a repair video">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eCKRl_Txa18" title="Is This Dumpster Hisense TV Worth Repairing?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1515" title="EEVblog 1515 - Dumpster Tektronix TDS540D 500MHz Oscilloscope LCD Upgrade">1515</a></sup> Modern instrument boards multiply this further: a single [[oscilloscope]] mainboard may carry 0.9 V, 1 V, 1.8 V, 2.5 V and 3.3 V rails together, with separate regulators for CPU core, transmission-line termination and analog supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1503" title="EEVblog 1503 - Rigol HDO4000 12bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1503</a></sup>

The rail's presence can sometimes be inferred from the energy-storage components around it. A bank of supercapacitors rated 2.7 V each, wired in series, implies a downstream rail of at least 3.3 V, since nothing on the board would operate below the single-cell voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup>

A measured value of about 3.25 V on a [[microcontroller]]'s supply pins is close enough to identify the rail as a nominal 3.3 V supply rather than something else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup>

## Generating the rail

The 3.3 V rail is most often derived by regulating down from a higher rail rather than generated directly from the mains supply. A common chain takes an unregulated DC input through a 78L05 with series [[diode]] protection to make 5 V, and drops that to 3.3 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup>

The choice of regulator is constrained by dropout. An LM1117-class low-dropout part will produce 3.3 V from a [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] without difficulty, which is a standard jellybean arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> An [[lm317|LM317]] cannot: it requires roughly 3 volts of headroom between input and output, so a 5 V input leaves nowhere near enough margin to hold 3.3 V in regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Where the rail comes from a system [[power-supply|power supply]] rather than a local regulator, it may not be independent. On the LeCroy 9384C the manual documents the 3.3 V rail as being taken off the 14 amp capable 5 V rail, which means load testing the supply properly requires loading both rails together rather than either one in isolation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>

## PCB layout and distribution

Because 3.3 V has to reach essentially everything digital on a board, it is normally distributed as a plane rather than as traces, which is one of the principal reasons multilayer construction is needed at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> A board may dedicate one entire layer to the 3.3 V rail as a flood fill, a generous allocation that can be traded away for cost by sharing the layer or using split planes instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Alternatively a single internal layer may be split, with one region dedicated to 3.3 V and another to 5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> A consequence for repair is that the rail cannot be physically traced from component to component, since it disappears into an internal plane that may snake anywhere across the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>

The 3.3 V plane is usually placed directly adjacent to a [[ground-plane|ground plane]], separated only by a thin layer of FR4 prepreg. On a six-layer board that gap is small enough that mechanical damage can plausibly crush the dielectric and short the two planes together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

For high-[[current]] digital loads the connection between plane and chip is made with large numbers of vias, giving a low-[[inductance]] path — the approach used to feed the main graphics chip of the Nintendo 64 directly from the 3.3 V plane with extensive [[via-stitching|via stitching]] and local bypassing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-491" title="EEVblog #491 - Nintendo 64 Game Console Teardown">491</a></sup> From an inductive standpoint, a solid 3.3 V plane and a solid ground plane spanning the whole board is the baseline; further improvement requires moving bypass capacitors to the underside of the package, directly beneath the pins, with vias running straight up to the balls so individual pins are bypassed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Split planes are reserved for genuinely separate analog sections rather than applied indiscriminately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup> Piling on large numbers of bypass caps beyond a couple of bulk decoupling parts adds little on an ordinary 3.3 V rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup>

Dave Jones's practice when laying out a board is to route all the signals first and only then work on the power rails, including 3.3 V — though how early power distribution should be tackled depends on how power-centric the design is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

## Measuring and identifying the rail

Well-documented boards mark expected voltages at test points, allowing a rail to be verified directly against the printed value; a point labelled 3.3 volts reading 3.335 V is within normal tolerance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1100" title="EEVblog #1100 - Dumpster Photocopier Repair">1100</a></sup> Many boards provide no such help, with no top-side test points and no silkscreen labelling, forcing the rail to be found by back-probing component pins and [[capacitor]] terminals with fine needle-point probes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> Through-hole capacitors on the rail make convenient clip-on points when a scope probe needs to be attached without a free hand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1324" title="EEVblog #1324 - Oscilloscope Reference Waveforms are USEFUL">1324</a></sup>

[[ripple|Ripple]] on the rail is checked by probing the bulk capacitors with the scope in [[ac-coupling|AC coupling]]; figures in the tens of millivolts are unremarkable for a digital supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> Capturing genuinely fast rail transients requires appropriate equipment — a dedicated [[power-rail|power rail]] probe resolves high-[[frequency]] [[ringing]] that ordinary probing misses, and transients originating on a 3.3 V rail can couple across into an adjacent 5 V rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup>

The rail is also useful as a trigger source. Triggering a single-shot capture on the 3.3 V rail's positive-going edge and then powering the unit up records the entire power-up sequence, letting rails be compared for simultaneity by zooming in on the ramp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1324" title="EEVblog #1324 - Oscilloscope Reference Waveforms are USEFUL">1324</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1320" title="EEVblog #1320 - Premature Oscilloscope Triggering">1320</a></sup>

## Shorts and rail failures

A dead 3.3 V rail while every other rail on the board remains correct is a characteristic failure signature. On the LeCroy 9384C the rail measured about 0.03 V with the 5 V rail entirely healthy, and [[resistance]] across the rail measured roughly 0.11 to 0.17 ohms — an implausibly low figure for a working power rail even allowing for lead resistance, which has to be zeroed out at these levels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup> The usual suspects for such a short are the bypass capacitors or one of the semiconductors on the rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

Tantalum capacitors are prime candidates. A tantalum measuring 0.08 ohms across a rail, lower than its neighbours, identifies itself as the likely culprit; where a [[schematic]] shows three tantalums on the 5 V rail and only two others exist on the board, those two can be assigned to the 3.3 V rail by elimination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> The diagnostic method is to lift one end of the suspect part, or to desolder capacitors from the rail one at a time using two irons with wedge tips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> This becomes tedious quickly: a channelised board may carry two capacitors on the 3.3 V rail per channel plus seven further ceramics elsewhere, and removing every [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] on the rail can still fail to clear the short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>

When capacitor removal is exhausted, a common next step is to connect a high-current supply to the rail and attempt to burn the short out. A 40 amp supply set to 3.3 V will drive practically any load without entering [[current-limit|current limit]], unlike the unit's own supply rated at around 6 amps on that rail, which shuts the 3.3 V rail down while leaving all other rails up when the fault draws roughly double its rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

Thermal imaging can discriminate between candidate devices, but only when the dissipation is genuinely concentrated. Four ASICs on the LeCroy's 3.3 V rail all reached the same temperature within three or four degrees Celsius, which was insufficient to single one out and inconsistent with one device dissipating the full fault power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> The resolution came from [[resistance-measurement|resistance measurement]]: removing one of the four chips raised the rail resistance from 0.11 to 0.14 ohms, exactly the change expected if all four were shorted in parallel and one quarter of the short had been removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup> The conclusion was that a spike or similar event on the rail had shorted all four ASICs internally, making the board effectively unrepairable even though the fault was fully understood.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

A low 3.3 V rail can also produce misleading secondary symptoms. A chip fed from the 5 V rail was observed running to 100 °C, but the overheating vanished when the 3.3 V rail was brought back to normal — the device was not itself faulty, but was being driven into excessive dissipation by wrong voltages on its inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

Other failure patterns are less dramatic. A rail can be present at the regulator output yet absent at the load, as in a [[multimeter]] where 3.3 V was confirmed at the regulator but nothing reached the microcontroller.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> A rail can be conditional on the power source, disappearing when a product runs from [[battery]] alone rather than external supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> And a pin expected to sit at 3.3 V reading 0.17 V once the board is connected indicates the load is pulling the rail down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pKV_JiauAE4" title="Dumpster Dive - HP Envy Touch PC Repair">•</a></sup>

Where a board is healthy, the corresponding check is prophylactic: before first power-up of newly assembled hardware, both the input cap and the capacitor on the 3.3 V rail are buzzed out for shorts, and only then is power applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup>

## Bench powering and injection

Substituting an external 3.3 V supply for the on-board rail is a standard technique both for diagnosis and for making a circuit work when the on-board source is inadequate. A device programmer unable to supply enough current to power a target chip directly can be supplemented by joining grounds and connecting an external 3.3 V supply straight onto the power rail, after which the chip reads out normally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1071" title="EEVblog #1071 - (UPDATED) Dumpster Dive HP Envy Touch PC REPAIR">1071</a></sup> The same substitution allows a board with a shorted rail to be run from a lab supply clipped to the 3.3 V rail — in the LeCroy's case drawing 10 to 11 amps — while the remaining rails come from the original supply, with an attempt made to bring all rails up together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

A microcontroller powered from a 3.3 V rail through its proper VCC pin draws its own current through that path, so any current taken by loads such as LEDs on I/O pins flows directly from the pins rather than through a protection diode, reducing the drop across that diode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup>

Because the rail feeds expensive silicon directly, [[overvoltage-protection|overvoltage protection]] on the bench supply matters. Setting an over-voltage limit slightly above nominal — 3.4 V for a 3.3 V rail — protects a prototype board against the supply being knocked or misadjusted, a failure that can destroy every chip on an expensive assembly in an instant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup>
