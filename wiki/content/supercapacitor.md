A supercapacitor — also called a supercap, an ultracapacitor, or an electric double layer [[capacitor]] — is a capacitor whose [[capacitance]] is measured in whole farads rather than microfarads, achieved at the cost of a very low working [[voltage]] per cell, typically around 2.5 to 2.7 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup> The farad-scale capacitance makes it a charge *store* rather than a filter element: it is used where a circuit needs to keep running, keep counting, or shut down cleanly after its power source goes away, and where a [[battery]] would be the obvious alternative if it did not leak, wear out, or need replacing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> Modern parts have made capacitance values that were once implausible into ordinary stock items — a 1 F part now fits in a cylinder about 6 mm in diameter and 9 or 10 mm tall, and surface-mount double-layer capacitors are available in tape and reel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

The distinction between one supercap and another is not cosmetic; construction, leakage, ESR, and derating behaviour vary enough between families that "A supercap is not a supercap."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1741" title="EEVblog 1741 - Mailbag!">1741</a></sup>

## Voltage rating and series stacking

The low cell voltage is the defining constraint. Because nothing much runs below 2.7 V, a supercap bank intended to hold up a 3.3 V or [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] has to be built from cells in series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> Series operation raises a balancing problem: the cells do not share the applied voltage equally on their own, so balance resistors are placed across each cell to force the split.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> Omitting or losing one of those resistors leaves one cell overvoltaged, and a missing balance [[resistor]] has been found on a board whose series pair subsequently failed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup>

Series stacking is contentious as a design practice, and the alternative — a single cell followed by a boost or DC-DC converter — trades the balancing problem for a converter and its own set of requirements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> Neither choice is wrong in principle; it is a trade-off, and the series approach is defensible provided the balance resistors are actually accounted for in the design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup>

Derating matters as much as balancing. Supercap life is strongly sensitive to both temperature and applied voltage, on the order of a halving of life for each 10 °C rise or each additional 0.1 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> A part run at its nameplate voltage in a hot enclosure is being run at its limit in two dimensions at once.

## Memory and clock backup

The oldest and most common application is holding up volatile state across a power interruption. A supercap on a portable computer's main board keeps the contents of a 128K static [[ram|RAM]] pack alive while the batteries are being swapped, which is only workable because [[sram|SRAM]] — unlike dynamic RAM — draws almost nothing in retention.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> The same role recurs across decades of equipment: a 5.5 V 0.047 F NEC part for RAM retention,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-382" title="EEVblog #382 - Cambridge Z88 Teardown">382</a></sup> a 5.5 V 0.22 F NEC part standing in for the rechargeable or primary lithium cell that would otherwise be sitting in an [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] waiting to leak,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> a 0.1 F part for backup in networking gear,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-851" title="EEVBlog #851 - Mailbag">851</a></sup> 5.5 V 0.47 F parts in salvaged industrial boards,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9VVTGE7ABIk" title="Mystery Dumpster Teardown - 2020-05-30">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-745" title="EEVblog #745 - Mailbag">745</a></sup> a 0.02 F part on a camcorder's DC input board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-375" title="EEVblog #375 - Sony Video 8 Camcorder Teardown">375</a></sup> and 4.7 F at 2.7 V used outright as the real-time clock battery replacement in an [[oscilloscope]], glued down and paired with a soldered-in 32 kHz watch crystal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup> Similar backup parts appear in a professional GPS receiver,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-918" title="EEVblog #918 - REPAIR: Sony Pyxis GPS">918</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup> a music keyboard,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup> telephone systems,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-214" title="EEVblog #214 - Commander Phone System Teardown">214</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-363" title="EEVblog #363 - Gold Phone Teardown">363</a></sup> a [[multimeter]] whose time and date are lost if the batteries are out too long,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup> and a high-security electronic lock that has no internal battery at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1483" title="EEVblog 1483 - Holy Mailbag Bomb Batman!">1483</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1484" title="EEVblog 1484 - Kaba Mas X-09 High Security Electronic Lock Teardown">1484</a></sup>

Avoiding a battery is a substantial part of the appeal: a supercap does not leak [[electrolyte]] onto a board over twenty years the way a backup cell will, and needs no service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> Where a design has both, the supercap is doing a different job from the battery — the battery holds the clock, while the supercap is sized to keep the [[processor]] alive long enough to shut down in an orderly way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup>

## Graceful shutdown and ride-through

The last-gasp shutdown case is a distinct application from long-term retention. In a mains-powered solar monitor, the supercap exists only to detect the power failure and supply the brief interval needed to close out writes so the stored data is not corrupted, and to send one final cellular report that power has been lost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> Emergency-call systems use the same idea, holding enough energy for the single outgoing call after the vehicle's power has gone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup>

Ride-through of the opposite kind — supplying a large transient rather than surviving a loss — also suits the technology, because a supercap's ESR is low enough to source [[current]] a battery of the same size cannot. Some trucks buffer an [[inverter]] with a large supercap tied to the alternator so that an oversized load plugged into the outlet does not disturb everything else downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Slot car racing tracks use pairs of two-farad capacitors alongside lead-acid batteries to cover the current demanded on acceleration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1214" title="EEVblog #1214 - Insanely Fast Slot Cars!">1214</a></sup> The energy involved is not trivial: transferred into a part with the very low ESR a supercap offers, the charge held in an AA-sized cell is enough to do real damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1043" title="EEVblog #1043 - Mailbag">1043</a></sup> A 5 F 2.7 V bank removed from a failed board retained a hazardous charge for a considerable time after the equipment was dead, and required deliberate discharging — with visible voltage recovery afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup>

## Energy harvesting

Supercaps are the natural storage element for harvesting circuits, where the source delivers microwatts to milliwatts and the load runs in bursts. Thermoelectric harvesting ICs provide a dedicated storage pin onto which a farad-class capacitor can be connected, accumulating energy from the generator in parallel with running the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> Piezoelectric harvesters work the same way, and can be fed from a [[solar-panel|solar panel]] through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] into the element's internal bridge; no series blocking [[diode]] is needed to prevent back-discharge into the chip, because the chip simply shuts down when the input voltage is insufficient to charge the capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup> Indoor [[solar-power-system|photovoltaic]] evaluation kits offer a supercap in place of a battery, with charge curves plotted against storage capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-S-UqkM8iDKM" title="#1179 - PowerFilm Flexible Indoor Solar Cell">•</a></sup>

That last curve is the central design tension of harvesting: more storage capacitance means more energy available to the application, but a proportionally longer wait before the circuit has enough voltage to start up and do anything at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-S-UqkM8iDKM" title="#1179 - PowerFilm Flexible Indoor Solar Cell">•</a></sup> The related failure of the concept is straightforward energy budgeting — a solar-powered calculator built around an internal high-capacity supercap was abandoned because the cells could not supply even the 100 to 200 microamps the [[lcd|LCD]] needed, and a calculator that has to be charged before use is not a calculator anyone wants.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup>

## Where supercaps are the wrong answer

Against a battery, the supercap loses badly on energy per unit volume, and this rules it out of a large class of low-power designs. In a memory-LCD product drawing about 2 microamps in standby — roughly as low as a practical design gets, with maybe a factor of two available through further engineering but not an order of magnitude — a 1 F part yields a run time fifty times shorter than a physically large alternative, and the physically large alternative is too big to use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> The conclusion is blunt: supercaps aren't any good at all if you need small size and long discharge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

Where a supercap or a rechargeable cell is used as the retention element, it must be isolated from the rest of the product with a series diode so that powering down the main circuitry cannot drain the store backwards through it; diode leakage then becomes part of the budget, at tens of nanoamps for an ordinary small-signal part such as a 1N4148.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

Supercaps are also awkward in manufacturing. They are among the temperature-sensitive components — along with batteries — that cannot go through [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]] or reflow and must be hand-soldered at final assembly, sometimes with preheating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="Electronex Expo 2011 - EEVblog #207 (1 of 3)">207</a></sup>

## Failure modes

Supercaps are wet-electrolyte parts and fail like one. A series pair in a mains-powered solar monitor vented, and the electrolyte ran down the board under gravity and wicked into a [[connector]], tracking across the [[pcb|PCB]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> The leak took out a diode, [[desoldering]] it completely, and reflowed a ceramic-bodied resistor so violently that the resistor snapped and resoldered itself in the opposite orientation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> The board burned through, and the PCB tracks in the affected area went black and began to corrode; where electrolyte has soaked into the laminate the affected material has to be drilled out because it can go conductive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> The equipment continued to draw everything the [[transformer]]'s primary side could supply, overloading a primary-side resistor, because there was no fusing on the output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup>

Contributing factors in that case were a missing balance resistor across the series pair and an installation on the afternoon-sun side of a [[fuse]] box, where the enclosure would get hot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> The parts themselves were otherwise consistent with a good-quality build.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup> A degraded supercap does not necessarily read as failed: both cells still charged when tested, though extremely slowly, using nothing more than an ohmmeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup>

## In pseudoscientific products

Supercaps appear routinely as the concealed energy store in products claiming an impossible power source. Wireless-power ventures repeatedly turn out to be RF energy harvesting into a bank of supercaps or batteries, which then trickle-charges the phone — energy harvesting on the order of milliwatts, presented as power transmission, and constrained by transmitters rated at 3 W that would not suffice even at 100 % [[efficiency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1408" title="EEVblog 1408 - $5M WiGL Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1408</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1532" title="EEVblog 1532 - Another $2.8M WiGL Wireless Power Grift BUSTED (Part 3)">1532</a></sup> A "nuclear diamond" battery marketed as never needing recharging pairs a roughly 100 microwatt source with a small supercapacitor at the top of an AA-sized package; the supercap is what allows any meaningful burst of current to be drawn, and the underlying source remains a couple of hundred microwatts at best.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup> The word graphene is generally attached.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1595" title="EEVblog 1595 - 50 Year Chinese Nuclear Diamond Battery!">1595</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 - WAVJA Solar Balls, 60x Better Than A Solar Panel? - Part 1">1633</a></sup>

The tell is consistent: where a claimed generator produces microwatts and the demonstration delivers watts, the storage element is doing the work, and its presence in the teardown or the marketing copy is the giveaway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1408" title="EEVblog 1408 - $5M WiGL Wireless Charging BUSTED!">1408</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1532" title="EEVblog 1532 - Another $2.8M WiGL Wireless Power Grift BUSTED (Part 3)">1532</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1333" title="EEVblog #1333 - Nuclear Diamond Self-Charging Battery DEBUNKED!">1333</a></sup> The same reasoning identifies a supercap-sized part as the likely storage in an automotive "voltage stabiliser", though in that case the component was probably too small to even qualify as a proper supercap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup>
