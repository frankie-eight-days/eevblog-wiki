The BM235 is a compact 6,000-count true-RMS handheld [[digital-multimeter|digital multimeter]] designed and manufactured by Brymen, sold both under the Brymen name and as an EEVblog-branded rebadge for which Dave Jones held the exclusive Australian distributorship.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AzIuayXzCvs" title="Brymen / EEVblog BM235 Multimeter Unboxing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> It sits at the top of a small family that also includes the 231 and 233, the lower models lacking features such as the low-[[impedance]] test range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AzIuayXzCvs" title="Brymen / EEVblog BM235 Multimeter Unboxing">•</a></sup> It matters chiefly as a reference point: a small, fast, well-protected general-purpose bench meter against which larger and more expensive instruments are repeatedly measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-izCDWjK_pV4" title="121GW Autoranging Speed">•</a></sup>

Brymen took over two years to bring the meter from original concept to first shipment, which is a long development cycle for a comparatively simple [[multimeter]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> The EEVblog version is not a separate design: the branding is a label change, a few extra ranges were added in [[firmware]], and there was minor input on internal mechanical details, but the electrical and functional specification is identical to any other BM235.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AzIuayXzCvs" title="Brymen / EEVblog BM235 Multimeter Unboxing">•</a></sup> Brymen treats the internals as proprietary and has declined to release schematics even to its dealers, or to identify the multimeter chipset used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup>

## Architecture and chipset

The meter uses a two-chip architecture rather than a single integrated multimeter chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> The [[processor]] is marked CPU HO8A and includes an 8 × 8 hardware multiplier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> The exact multimeter chipset has never been disclosed by Brymen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup> One diagnostic signature of a chipset change in this class of meter is how the ranges are organised — a design that places volts AC and volts DC on the same range position is using a different chipset from one that separates them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup>

Firmware is held in a one-time-programmable part and cannot be field-updated; revisions are introduced at the factory and ship with new stock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0Oo2J66FLZA" title="BM786 Firmware Update Test">•</a></sup> The meter has passed through roughly five firmware revisions over its production life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0Oo2J66FLZA" title="BM786 Firmware Update Test">•</a></sup> The installed version is read by holding the HOLD button while switching the meter on; early units shipped with Rev-B and were later updated to Rev-C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d5CxW3VBoZU" title="BM235 Firmware Quirk">•</a></sup> Manufacturing date wheels moulded into the case interior give the build date, and the case tooling appears unchanged between 2015 and 2019 production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uZ1vXaBPLTY" title="BM235 Multimeter Faulty Switch FIXED + (OZ) GIVEAWAY">•</a></sup>

## Measurement performance

True-RMS AC is specified only to 440 Hz. The converter continues to work well above that figure, but measurably degrades somewhere around 1.5 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> The meter does not carry the dedicated true-RMS converter chip found in higher-end instruments such as the BM869, and consequently reads low on signals whose content extends past its [[bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cBNOxg8Px8M" title="Playing with short current pulses on multimeters">•</a></sup>

That limitation is clearest with short [[current]] pulses. Against a repetitive [[square-wave|square wave]], the BM235 is the first of a group of meters to lose accuracy as pulse width shrinks: it is already degrading at 500 microseconds and has dropped off by 100 microseconds, whereas comparable meters hold on until roughly 150 to 200 microseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cBNOxg8Px8M" title="Playing with short current pulses on multimeters">•</a></sup> The same architecture is an advantage at the other end of the behaviour: when the signal is removed, the BM235 falls to zero quickly rather than displaying the long residual countdown seen on meters with a settling true-RMS converter and its associated stored charge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cBNOxg8Px8M" title="Playing with short current pulses on multimeters">•</a></sup>

[[resistance-measurement|Resistance measurement]] tops out at 60 MΩ.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0z5dGXlRibA" title="A Very Strange BM235 Fault">•</a></sup> The meter includes a 50 mV range and a built-in [[electric-field|electric field]] detector for tracing mains wiring, though a dedicated [[voltage]] detection stick gives far better discrimination between individual conductors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-AzIuayXzCvs" title="Brymen / EEVblog BM235 Multimeter Unboxing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

Continuity detection is reliable on pulses down to around 18 to 20 milliseconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1681" title="EEVblog 1681 - Mailbag: Nice DIY CC Load,  Amazing Proto Boards, Ian Johnston">1681</a></sup> The meter is also resistant to continuity latching, showing no false beep on a test that provokes a marginal response from the newer BM2257.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1671" title="EEVblog 1671 - Beware of Multimeter Continuity Latching">1671</a></sup>

[[auto-ranging|Autoranging]] is fast — fast enough that stopwatch timing is not worth attempting, with range settling occurring in well under a second. It is quicker than the [[121gw|121GW]], the BM869 and the Keysight U1282A on the same test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-izCDWjK_pV4" title="121GW Autoranging Speed">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uUCQuIp_hzU" title="121GW Ohms Autoranging Speed Update">•</a></sup>

## Low-impedance mode

The low-Z path in the original design runs through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] and a PTC, then into a Zener clamp to [[ground]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> Because the Zener does not conduct until roughly 7 volts, the low-impedance load is not presented at low voltages: below about 8 volts the meter simply shows its normal 10 MΩ [[input-impedance|input impedance]] instead of switching in the load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> This differs from meters that present a fixed low impedance — on the order of 900 kΩ — across the whole range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

## Input protection

[[input-protection|Input protection]] is built around metal oxide varistors that clamp the incoming transient, backed by PTCs, series input protection resistors and [[diode]] clamping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup> Under a 5 kV impulse the clamping action is clean, with no [[overshoot]] visible on the clamped [[waveform]], and the meter survives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup> This is protection by design rather than by luck: cheaper meters that carry only a PTC and no MOV survive such events, when they survive at all, without any engineered clamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup>

The 600 mA current range is fused, but the supplied [[fuse]] is a 400 mA part rather than a 600 mA one — an ASTM HV610, the same fuse specified across the BM235 and BM786.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1377" title="EEVblog 1377 - The Amazing UNPREDICTABILITY of Fuses!">1377</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1051" title="EEVblog #1051 - 121GW Multimeter Mass Turbulence">1051</a></sup>

## Display

The [[lcd|LCD]] bias voltage is fixed by a [[resistor]] rather than being adjustable, set to a point Brymen judged acceptable; the resulting viewing angle is not optimally chosen, although in practice the meter compares well against other handhelds at typical bench angles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-898" title="EEVblog #898 - LCD Contrast Experiments">898</a></sup>

The display carries a rolling averaging filter that produces a visible pause: roughly every tenth or eleventh count, the reading freezes briefly instead of advancing smoothly. Driven with a very slow ramp the effect is unambiguous, and the newer BM2257 shows no such pause at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ZqrPCed1ss8" title="BM235 Display Lag/Pause Testing">•</a></sup>

## Known faults

Several distinct faults have been documented in returned units:

- **Autorange lockup on ohms.** A returned meter displayed an ER error on [[resistance]] in autorange for values from 100 kΩ down to 100 Ω, and would sometimes stick at a wildly wrong reading such as 16 MΩ for a 100 Ω resistor. The same meter measured correctly on manual range, isolating the fault to autoranging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0z5dGXlRibA" title="A Very Strange BM235 Fault">•</a></sup>
- **Spurious insertion error.** Switching quickly from Auto-V through the off position to volts with no probes plugged in briefly raises an insertion error with an accompanying beep, in both switching directions. The meter continues to work normally afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d5CxW3VBoZU" title="BM235 Firmware Quirk">•</a></sup>
- **[[battery|Battery]] drain when switched off.** Some residual current is expected for the microprocessor waiting on a wake-up button, but in the off position the switch should physically disconnect the battery, implying something ahead of the switch — a protection device or similar — draws power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup>
- **[[range-switch|Range switch]] failure.** One returned unit switched itself off spontaneously, which was not a battery problem. Transplanting the board into a different case cured it, locating the fault in the front case switch assembly rather than the [[pcb|PCB]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uZ1vXaBPLTY" title="BM235 Multimeter Faulty Switch FIXED + (OZ) GIVEAWAY">•</a></sup>
- **Main chip failure.** A small number of units have simply had the main chip die over the meter's seven-to-eight-year selling life, consistent with a broader pattern of Brymen processors failing over long periods.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>
- **Board flex.** An early issue in the area near the inductors caused board flex damage that broke inductors, ruining the ground path and the measurements with it. This was a separate problem from the battery drain and was corrected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup>

At least one design issue uncovered through repair work was subsequently fixed by Brymen, and the design has been refined over several such rounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup>

## Relationship to the BM257

The BM235 and BM257 share the same case, including the two transparent holes intended for an infrared serial link.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup> The BM257 carries an infrared [[led|LED]] that transmits only — despite the case having both a transmit and a receive aperture, there is no receiver fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup> The BM235 PCB has an unpopulated pad for a diode in almost exactly the corresponding location, lining up closely with the case hole, which makes an infrared-transmit retrofit superficially plausible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup>

Internally the two diverge substantially. In the BM235 the piezo transducer is mounted on the board rather than wired separately, there is less internal wiring overall, the insulated plastic shield present in the BM257 is absent, the amps and milliamp/microamp jacks are reorganised, and the chipsets are placed on the top side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup>

## Accessories and construction

Supplied probes are gold-plated, which measurably improves contact reliability in [[continuity-testing|continuity testing]] compared with non-gold-plated types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uV9mW0rpRxg" title="eevBLAB #33 - Why Gold Plated Probes Matter">•</a></sup> A magnetic hanger accessory slots into the back of the case for hanging the meter on a metal shelf or cabinet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rZqfp2XKvjE" title="BM786 Magnetic Quirk">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The case uses captive screws so none can be lost during servicing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup>

The audible indicator is an electromagnetic buzzer with its own internal [[oscillator]], driven from about 5 V, not a piezo transducer. Bringing a magnet near the side of the case modulates the continuity beeper's tone, and does no damage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rZqfp2XKvjE" title="BM786 Magnetic Quirk">•</a></sup>

## Form factor

The BM235 is physically small for a full-function meter, a quality that makes it preferable for general bench work over large industrial-field instruments that consume a lot of bench space.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup> Its size served as the design target for the 121GW: the case that was eventually adopted for that meter, after an earlier and much bulkier design was rejected on shipping volume, weight and usability grounds, was made suitable for both meters and came out roughly on par with the BM235 in bulk.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup>
