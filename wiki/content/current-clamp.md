A current clamp is a split-core [[current-transformer|current transformer]] that measures the [[current]] in a conductor by enclosing it, without breaking the circuit or making [[electrical-contact|electrical contact]] with it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Because the wire simply passes through the jaws, a clamp can be fitted to live mains wiring in a switchboard and left there permanently, which makes it the standard sensing element for household energy monitoring as well as a bench instrument for measurements that would otherwise require cutting a circuit open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Everything a clamp reports follows from the [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] around the conductor, so what it sees depends entirely on which conductors are inside the jaws and which way the current flows through them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup>

## Operating principle and directionality

The clamp responds to the net current enclosed by its core. Passing both the active and the neutral of a circuit through the same clamp puts equal and opposite currents inside the core, which cancel and give zero output; any earth leakage — water ingress, a person contacting a live part, an insulation failure — unbalances active against neutral and produces an output of one polarity or the other depending on the direction of the fault current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup> This is the mechanism behind residual current protection, and the same clamp is capable of measuring the imbalance directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup>

Clamps used for energy monitoring are directional and carry an arrow indicating the orientation of positive current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EYx46kRv2Bw" title="Myenergi Zappi 7kW EV Charger Installation &amp; Testing">•</a></sup> A clamp fitted back to front on a bidirectional circuit reverses the sign of everything it reports, so an installation that turns out to read backwards is normally corrected by rotating the clamp rather than by reconfiguring the monitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1719" title="EEVblog 1719 - 75kWh Home Storage Battery Expansion!">1719</a></sup> The symptom is unambiguous: a reversed clamp reads backwards at all times, not intermittently, which is a useful discriminator when diagnosing a monitoring system that only reads negative some of the time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup>

## Home energy monitoring

In a domestic solar installation the clamp sits after the [[inverter]], on the AC side, so it never measures the DC from the panels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> Measuring production this way is essentially foolproof — one clamp on the AC output feeds the monitoring hardware, and the type of inverter, [[microinverter]] or string, makes no difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> Separate clamps measure household consumption and the current flowing out to the grid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> Per-circuit clamps extend the same idea to individual loads: total consumption, hot water, pool, and EV charging can each carry their own clamp, and a per-load clamp is what makes an anomaly such as an oscillating heat pump visible at all, since a whole-house consumption figure buries it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-vDtxmikcYuc" title="Who Screwed With The Solar Analytics Code?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Uq553ckJ0uQ" title="MURPHY! - Reclaim Hot Water Heat Pump FAIL">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1630" title="EEVblog 1630 - Solar Panels for Water Pre-Heating?">1630</a></sup>

Placement is dictated by the physical wiring rather than by what is ideal to measure. A consumer unit typically has many circuits effectively in parallel with no single point through which all load current passes, so putting one clamp on the load side is difficult on most [[fuse]] boxes; measuring the true isolated house load would require the board to be rewired so that one wire fed all the other breakers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> Common practice is therefore to fit the consumption clamp outside the board, on the grid side of the main breaker, where the single incoming conductor carries both import and export.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup>

## The multi-system blind spot

Placing the clamp on the grid side has a consequence that appears whenever more than one generating system is present. Each monitoring system knows only about its own production plus whatever the grid-side clamp reports; it has no knowledge of a second system feeding the same board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup> With a house drawing 1 A and two solar systems each producing 2 A, 4 A flows in and 1 A is consumed, so 3 A flows outward past the clamp and the system reports a consumption of minus three amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> After dark both systems drop to zero, the outward flow stops, and the same clamp correctly reports the 1 A load as positive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> The result is a consumption trace that goes negative during the day and positive at night, tracking the sun — a pattern that superficially resembles a reversed clamp but is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> A grid-side clamp is a perfectly sound arrangement provided no second, unmonitored generator exists; the fix where one does is to parallel the current transformers so a single input sums both sources.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup> In practice this can mean sacrificing an existing measurement channel, for instance repurposing an air-conditioning clamp to measure a newly added array and folding the air-conditioning power back into the total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1390" title="EEVblog 1390 - NEGATIVE Household Solar Consumption? WHY?">1390</a></sup>

The same blind spot is exploited deliberately by hybrid inverters and diverting loads. A [[battery]] inverter running zero-export or AC-couple mode does not need visibility of the house load at all; a single grid-side clamp telling it whether current is flowing out is enough to decide whether to divert excess into the battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1634" title="EEVblog 1634 - 15kWh AERL LFP Home Storage Battery Install!">1634</a></sup> EV chargers work the same way, using a grid-side clamp to charge from surplus solar, with additional clamps optional for finer reporting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1437" title="EEVblog 1437 - Zappi 7kW Electric Car Charger TEARDOWN + EXPERIMENT">1437</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EYx46kRv2Bw" title="Myenergi Zappi 7kW EV Charger Installation &amp; Testing">•</a></sup>

Differential measurements made from two clamps degrade at low power. A monitor computing standby consumption as the real-time difference between an import clamp and an export clamp behaved poorly during the day and appeared to hit a floor around 120 W; repeating the audit at night, with generation absent, resolved the same house down to 109.4 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup> When only a differential is wanted, the absolute accuracy of either clamp matters less than the stability of the difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup>

Accumulating independent systems means accumulating clamps: two solar systems, a whole-house monitor, per-circuit channels and an EV charger with its own sensing can put half a dozen or more clamps into one switchboard, "current clamps out the wazoo", to the point where reworking the board becomes a job of tracking down which clamp is on which conductor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-X83SPqsf5kY" title="Smart Meter Installed!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1651" title="EEVblog 1651 - Home Electrical Switchboard UPGRADE!">1651</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7WbO1lnsLC8" title="Some Interesting Home Solar Data">•</a></sup> Clamps are also the main obstacle to a tidy switchboard, since they must sit around individual conductors and cannot be absorbed into a wiring loom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1651" title="EEVblog 1651 - Home Electrical Switchboard UPGRADE!">1651</a></sup> Consolidating the two solar feeds onto a single link so one clamp can enclose both is a way to reduce the count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1651" title="EEVblog 1651 - Home Electrical Switchboard UPGRADE!">1651</a></sup>

## Bench and instrument clamps

A passive clamp is nothing but a current transformer with a burden [[resistor]], requiring no batteries and producing a millivolt output proportional to current, so it works with any [[multimeter]]'s millivolt range rather than only with the meter it shipped with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> A representative low-cost example is rated to 600 A maximum with scaling of 600 mV at 100 A, 100 mV at 10 A, and 1 mV at 1 A — resolution that makes it a high-current instrument, not a low-current one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Some meters provide a dedicated clamp mode displaying amps AC and amps DC directly, though not necessarily with selectable ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1731" title="EEVblog 1731 - $50 Ziboo 17B Pro Multimeter vs $150 Fluke REVIEW">1731</a></sup> Clamps intended for [[oscilloscope]] use are specified in volts per amp instead; a 0.1 V/A clamp driving a scope channel set to current mode yields a display in amps, for example 5 A per division, with cycle-RMS statistics computed from the [[waveform]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1659" title="EEVblog 1659 - Quick 861 Pro Hot Air Station REVIEW">1659</a></sup>

Energy-monitoring inputs distinguish between two output types. A current-output clamp is a plain current transformer, while a [[voltage]]-output clamp contains its own burden and delivers a voltage proportional to input current; where the instrument must compute power it needs the current transformer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup> [[transformer|Transformer]] ratio is a configured parameter, with settings such as 1000:1, 100:1, 10:1 and 1:1, and split-core meter clamps are commonly specified in the same terms — 100 mA per 100 A, for instance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1719" title="EEVblog 1719 - 75kWh Home Storage Battery Expansion!">1719</a></sup>

Flexible Rogowski-style clamps trade some accuracy for the ability to encircle very large or awkward conductors and reach far higher ranges than rigid jaws, up to 2500 A and 4000 A in wireless multimeter accessory sets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup>

Compared with a positional field probe, a clamp is far less sensitive to exactly where the probe sits relative to the conductor, so where a wire is available to pass through the jaws a clamp gives the easier absolute measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup> Clamps intended for probing high-voltage, high-energy switch-mode supplies carry a safety rating for the job — 300 V CAT II in one such accessory — and finger guards to keep hands behind the barrier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

## Practical technique

DC-capable clamps must be zeroed before use, and re-zeroed after being moved, because the Earth's magnetic field biases the sensor depending on orientation; a properly zeroed clamp resolves standby currents in the tens of milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xC8zbL79-EM" title="Nissan Dualis ABS Modulator Failure Update">•</a></sup> Where a monitoring system's own reading is in doubt, a clamp applied directly to the conductor is the independent check — used to confirm that an inverter with a failed network link is still producing, or to verify a suspicious DC current reading against a second clamp before concluding that the reported power does not match the measured volts and amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uE0x8YR7nMg" title="Enphase Envoy S FAILING?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kCtGoymiShU" title="Deye Hybrid Inverter Gen Port Troubleshooting">•</a></sup> A clamp also offers a way into circuits that resist [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]], such as tracing which cells a switching board is drawing from when the board itself cannot be read.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1460" title="EEVblog1460 - REPAIRING a LED Studio Light with a DUMPSTER LAPTOP!">1460</a></sup>
