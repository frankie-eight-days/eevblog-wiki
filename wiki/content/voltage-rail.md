A voltage rail is one of the regulated supply voltages distributed through a piece of equipment — the 3.3 V, 5 V, 12 V or ±15 V nets that every active device on a board hangs off. A modern product rarely has one: high-density logic such as [[fpga|FPGAs]] demands a spread of low-[[voltage]] supplies, not just 3.3 V but 2.5 V, 1.8 V, 1.2 V, and 0.8 V or 1 V cores, so half a dozen distinct rails inside a single product is unremarkable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Because the rails are the common dependency of everything else on the board, they are simultaneously the first thing a designer must plan and the first thing a troubleshooter must measure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-65" title="EEVblog #65 - Umm, I Design Computers">65</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

## Rails in system design

Instrument and computer designs carry rails for different purposes at different voltages. Analog sections typically want a bipolar pair — ±12 V, ±15 V, ±20 V — while the digital side runs from 3.3 V and 5 V, which is the usual reason a supply is built with multiple taps in the first place.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1489" title="EEVblog 1489 - Mystery Teardown!">1489</a></sup> Where a product runs from a single [[battery]] at much lower voltage than its analog signal range demands, DC-DC converters are the mechanism that manufactures the required rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1090" title="EEVblog #1090 - Sony Mystery Teardown">1090</a></sup> Processors of any complexity may need a dedicated power management controller that generates all the various rails and handles the power-up sequences, rather than being hooked directly to 3.3 V the way a small [[microcontroller]] can be.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup>

The count of rails costs money as well as board area. With three or even five voltage regulators per product, the per-unit price of a jelly-bean regulator becomes a real line in the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] at volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Setting a rail's value also interacts with BOM consolidation: a feedback divider that needs an odd value such as 4.99 K to hit the tolerance on a 1.2 V rail can often be built from two 10 K resistors in parallel instead, avoiding another reel, another BOM line, and another pick-and-place slot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> Layout for bleeding-edge parts pulling substantial power means holding many different rails to tight tolerances under dynamic, AC and static power conditions at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> At the package level, a device's multiple power pins may belong to different internal cores rather than one rail, and manufacturers commonly specify a [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] per pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1512" title="EEVblog 1512 - Why Bypass Your PCB Like THIS?">1512</a></sup>

## Sequencing

Rails do not exist independently at power-up. An FPGA datasheet may carry a recommended or even a strict power-up rail sequence — bringing up the 1.2 V core first and the 3.3 V I/O some 10 ms later, with deliberate delays in between.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-995" title="EEVblog #995 - Power Supply Sequencing">995</a></sup>

Powering rails independently from bench supplies exposes the coupling between them. With four rails brought up one at a time, switching on the 3.3 V rail first forced the unpowered [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] up to roughly 1.5 V through back-[[diode]] protection paths and similar mechanisms, and simultaneously dragged the −5.2 V rail high by about 500 mV; the 5 V rail only snapped to its real value when its own supply was switched on and presented a low-[[impedance]] output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-995" title="EEVblog #995 - Power Supply Sequencing">995</a></sup> A separate 15.7 V rail in the same setup stayed at 0 V until its own supply was enabled, showing no such coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-995" title="EEVblog #995 - Power Supply Sequencing">995</a></sup> Rails held at intermediate voltages by these paths can prevent circuitry from starting up correctly in ways that are difficult to anticipate from the [[schematic]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-995" title="EEVblog #995 - Power Supply Sequencing">995</a></sup>

## Rails as the limit on analog signals

A rail sets a hard ceiling on what the circuitry between the rails can do. A typical [[op-amp|op amp]] cannot swing its output closer than about 1.5 V to the positive rail, so a 10 V peak output requires V+ of at least 11.5 V; lowering V+ toward that figure brings on clipping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> The same constraint applies to inputs: the inputs of an LM3914 will not operate all the way up to the rail, which is why a [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] used as the measured signal is divided down rather than applied directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> In [[comparator]] operation the output saturates hard at V+ for the smallest positive input difference and slams to the negative rail when the difference reverses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup> Rails also fix the arithmetic of threshold circuits — ±10 V rails with a 9 K and 1 K divider give the hysteresis of a Schmitt trigger — and of simple DC analysis, where a midpoint sitting at half rail is a useful sanity check.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> A logic-level output likewise swings to whatever rail powers it: [[pwm|PWM]] from a microcontroller on a 3.3 V rail switches between 0 V and 3.3 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

## Troubleshooting by the rails

The first rule of [[troubleshooting]] is "thou shalt test voltages": check the voltages and check the voltage rails before anything else, then work down from there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-65" title="EEVblog #65 - Umm, I Design Computers">65</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Rails are checked early for the same reason on any repair — a unit with a blown [[resistor]] and an open [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] gets its regulators and rails verified as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup> Checking that the rails are present at the supply is not sufficient; the rails must also be confirmed at the pins of the chip that consumes them, which is easily done at a device with known power pins such as an [[eeprom|EEPROM]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup> A rail measured on an intercom board at 5.21 V is high but within specification, and can be taken as good.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

Symptoms often point at a rail indirectly. A display that dims can only be dimming because the rail feeding it is dipping, since a rising rail would brighten it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup> A [[signal-generator|signal generator]] whose output amplitude is low under load suggests insufficient [[current]] drive and a rail sagging, though a board with no test points for the rail makes this awkward to confirm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1525" title="EEVblog 1525 - Commisioned Mailbag">1525</a></sup> A [[power-supply|power supply]] that refuses to bring the system up may be reporting a fault because a single rail — a −5 V rail, for example — is dead, which is what a properly designed system does rather than half-starting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup> Where a driver chip is not working, tracing its VCC back to the 3.9 V rail that should feed it, and finding that rail absent, localises the fault to the circuitry deriving it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup>

Measuring every rail rather than the obvious one is the discipline that makes the method work. On a universal programmer showing signs of shutdown, the 5 V, 12 V, 48 V, −5 V and 2.1 V rails were all measured before conclusions were drawn; the 12 V, 48 V, −5 V and 2.1 V rails were fine and the 5 V rail was dead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup> On a Tektronix 475 the unregulated 50 V section read 67 V and the 5 volt rail read 8.6 V, with plus 5, plus 8, minus 8, plus 50 and further rails all needing to be swept.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-jmb9ICnI8xI" title="Dumpster Tektronix 475 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 1">•</a></sup> A 3.3 V rail that failed during operation measured 18 ohms — effectively a short — which is a far easier fault to chase than a vague misbehaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

Rails can also be exonerated. On a dead [[lcd|LCD]] television all the measurable rails checked out fine, with 5.3 V and around 10 mV of [[ripple]] on the supplies, which is grounds to stop suspecting the electrolytics and look at the panel instead; reflexively replacing capacitors is not diagnosis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> On an [[oscilloscope-channel|oscilloscope channel]] fault, verifying the voltage rails and the signals out of the hybrid was what built confidence that the hybrid module was not at fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

### Intermittent and coupled faults

Faults that appear only after warm-up require several rails to be watched simultaneously. Four or five multimeters attached to the rails at once, with the probes fitted before power is applied, catch a rail that drops out after five or ten minutes without anyone probing live circuitry — which matters where dangerous voltages such as a 58 V rail and a 2.2 kV EHT supply are present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup> Instrumenting the rails first and then going hands-off is the safe approach on high-voltage equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup>

Rails that share a reference fail together in a distinctive pattern. In an HP1740A [[oscilloscope]] every voltage rail reference was tied to the 15 V reference, so a failure on the 5 V rail dragged the 15 V rail down, and the 15 V rail dragged all the remaining rails with it; the giveaway was that every rail dropped by exactly the same percentage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> A shorted rail can equally be pulled down by a fault on a different board fed from the same supply, so the board holding the fault is not necessarily the board showing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup> The usual failure mode on a rail is over-voltage, and a rail that has gone over-voltage tends to take multiple parts with it — a failed −15 V rail took out at least one op amp and three voltage regulators, which is a reason to suspect the other devices on that rail after the first faulty part is found.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup> Not every rail is [[ground]]-referenced: inside a [[multimeter]] the rails are shifted rather than sitting at a common reference, which makes probing them without a schematic misleading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

## Design for serviceability

Access to the rails is a design decision. Some equipment fits labelled links in series with each rail so the rail can be broken and its current measured, or the voltage checked at a marked test point such as one labelled plus 8.6 — links placed deliberately for ease of servicing and troubleshooting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> A power-on link on a television supply serves the same purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1145" title="EEVBlog #1145 - Dumpster Diving 4K TV!">1145</a></sup> Rail voltages silkscreened on the board, as 1V0, 1V8 and 1V2 were on one oscilloscope revision, and pinouts printed next to a supply [[connector]], make rails identifiable without a schematic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1145" title="EEVBlog #1145 - Dumpster Diving 4K TV!">1145</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup> The opposite practice is a legitimate criticism: connectors labelled only with reference designators rather than their system function, with no test point voltages anywhere on the board, make a supply unfriendly to service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>

Rails are exercised deliberately during development and production too. A prototype loom loaded with simulated loads on the different voltage rails is used to test power supply modules before they go into a final board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup> Old-school universal programmers went further, driving the voltage rails to their extremes during verification so a device was checked over a much wider range than its nominal supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup>
