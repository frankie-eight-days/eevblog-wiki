An oscilloscope is an instrument that measures [[voltage]] with respect to time, displaying voltage on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> Its essential advantage over a [[multimeter]] is speed: it can display signals changing in microseconds or nanoseconds, which is where all modern digital electronics operates, giving a visual window into circuit behaviour that no other instrument provides.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Amplitude is read against a screen grid called the graticule, scaled in [[volts-per-division|volts per division]], while the horizontal [[time-base|time base]] spans seconds down to nanoseconds per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup>

## Analogue and digital

The fundamental distinction between analogue ([[crt|CRT]]) and digital oscilloscopes is that a digital instrument can store the [[waveform]]: single-shot capture freezes a one-time event for later analysis, and captured data can be zoomed, measured, and processed after the probe is disconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> Digital scopes also add automated measurements, math functions, and intensity-graded persistence displays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup>

Second-hand analogue CRT scopes remain a valid low-cost entry point: a working 20 MHz dual-channel unit typically sells for under US$100 and often for around US$50, with Tektronix, HP, Hitachi, Philips, and Kikusui among the usual brands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-498" title="EEVblog #498 - How to get a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay">498</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> When buying second-hand, a listing should at minimum show a trace on screen or state the unit is tested, since a beginner is poorly placed to repair a dead channel; scopes sold explicitly as parts or non-working cannot usually be returned.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-498" title="EEVblog #498 - How to get a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay">498</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1450" title="EEVblog 1450- $99 Fluke Combiscope Ebay SCORE!">1450</a></sup> A quick functionality check consists of confirming waveforms on both channels, confirming they trigger, and spot-checking a high and a low volts-per-division range against a known signal source, which establishes correct operation with high confidence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1492" title="EEVblog 1492 - $5 Oscilloscope Repaired! + Oz GIVEAWAY">1492</a></sup>

## Triggering

Triggering stabilises the display by starting each acquisition at a defined signal level; if the [[trigger-level|trigger level]] is set above the signal's excursion, the display stops updating entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup> Beyond basic edge triggering, several subtleties matter in practice.

- **[[trigger-holdoff|Trigger holdoff]]** is a dedicated control, present on most higher-end analogue scopes and as an option on digital scopes, that inhibits retriggering for a set interval; it is widely misunderstood and rarely used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-159" title="EEVblog #159 - Oscilloscope Trigger Holdoff Tutorial">159</a></sup>
- **Trigger jitter on bursts** arises because an edge trigger presented with a burst of identical cycles cannot distinguish which edge to use, producing jitter as wide as the burst packet itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup>
- **Separate trigger path**: the trigger system is a separate analogue system from the ADC and display path, and the external trigger input is physically a different channel, so the scope can genuinely trigger on a signal that the acquisition system never captures and the screen shows nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1320" title="EEVblog #1320 - Premature Oscilloscope Triggering">1320</a></sup>
- **Arming delay**: on a slow timebase in single-shot mode, a scope may fail to capture an event because it had not finished arming; with the trigger point centred, acquisition memory is split into "50% pre-trigger data and 50% post-trigger data", and the full pre-trigger record must be collected before the trigger is armed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1678" title="EEVblog 1678 - Oscilloscope Trigger Warning">1678</a></sup>
- **Persistence for intermittent faults**: leaving a scope running overnight with infinite persistence captures rare runt pulses, glitches, or missed clocks that would otherwise be missed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3jDRH-6IvZc" title="Analog VS Digital Scopes for Glitch Captue">•</a></sup>
- Some instruments drop their [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]] dramatically when not triggering — in one observed case to roughly 20 Hz — rather than maintaining the continuous update users expect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-617" title="EEVblog #617 - Tektronix Oscilloscope Anomaly">617</a></sup>

## Bandwidth, sample rate, and memory

[[sample-rate|Sample rate]] matters more than headline [[bandwidth]] for single-shot work: a scope without roughly ten times its bandwidth in sample rate has limited usable single-shot performance — a 500 MS/s instrument has a usable single-shot bandwidth of only about 50 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> The minimum practical rule is four times oversampling; a 1 GS/s scope meets this at 200 MHz on one channel, but interleaving that rate across two channels halves it to 500 MS/s and limits usable bandwidth to about 125 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup>

Deep acquisition memory is a defining feature of modern digital scopes and is the reason they have displaced logic analysers for serial-protocol debugging of [[spi|SPI]], I²C, and RS-232, the scope's two-to-four-channel count being its main limitation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-44" title="EEVblog #44 Part 1 - Logic Analyzer Tutorial">44</a></sup> Memory architectures vary by manufacturer in how much record is available outside the visible window: one design may offer only the 20K points on screen despite 200M points of installed memory, requiring the time base to be changed to access deeper records, while others dedicate full memory to a single-shot capture by abandoning ping-pong buffering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

Waveform update rate is a major differentiator. Rates of one million waveforms per second were industry-leading when introduced in 2011 and previously cost US$10,000–20,000 to obtain; mid-range instruments more typically deliver 120,000–140,000 updates per second, and entry-level instruments a few thousand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-114" title="EEVblog #114 - Electronex 2010 Electronics Exhibition">114</a></sup> High update rates, achieved through custom acquisition ASICs that bypass the CPU, are also why such instruments can appear visually "noisy": more overlapping acquisitions are displayed per unit time, so for valid [[noise]]-floor comparisons between scopes the [[memory-depth|memory depth]], [[analog-bandwidth|analogue bandwidth]], intensity grading, update rate, and sample rate must all be matched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-610" title="EEVblog #610 - Why Digital Scopes Appear Noisy - Part 2">610</a></sup>

## ADC resolution and noise

ADC resolution has moved upward across the market: 12-bit converters are now standard even at entry level, and 14-bit instruments exist, which directly improves the ability to zoom into small signals riding on large DC levels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup> Comparing 12-bit scopes meaningfully requires measuring the actual full-scale ADC range and [[noise-floor|noise floor]] to ensure an apples-to-apples comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>

Practical noise figures at a 20 MHz [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]] are in the tens of microvolts: measured mean noise floors of 38 µV and 65 µV were recorded on comparable instruments under matched conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1223" title="EEVblog #1223 - Oscilloscope Standard Deviation Noise Measurement">1223</a></sup> Front ends designed for higher bandwidth are inherently noisier, because the amplifier ASICs must be built for the full 1.5 GHz-class bandwidth even when the user bandwidth-limits to 20 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> A 500 µV/division genuine vertical range is uncommon and valuable for low-level work such as power-supply measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> The 20 MHz bandwidth-limit setting exists specifically for noise measurement and is a long-standing standard method, though full bandwidth should be used when assessing signal fidelity and transients.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup>

Oscilloscopes are not precision instruments for absolute voltage: absolute accuracy is typically on the order of half a percent to a couple of percent, so their strength lies in relative and time-domain measurement, where fine vernier control of the vertical gain can be used to maximise usable resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup>

## Probes and probing

The standard passive probe is a ×10 compensated design. Switchable ×1/×10 probes are a common source of error, because the switch is easily bumped and no passive means exists for the scope to detect its position; the resulting readings are an order of magnitude out. Professional probes omit the switch for this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> The probe-attenuation setting in the scope's menu is purely a software multiplier — the hardware input ranges from 1 mV to 10 V per division are fixed by physical amplifiers and attenuators — so a mismatch between probe and setting corrupts every vertical reading, and can make a scope appear unable to reach its lowest ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup> Some probes carry a detection pin that sets the attenuation automatically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup>

Other probe types covered:

- **Active FET probes** place amplifier electronics in the probe head rather than the scope, require power (usually drawn from a proprietary scope interface), and are used for high-[[frequency]] work; units with standard 50 Ω outputs can connect to any scope with a 50 Ω input or an inline terminator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>
- **DIY resistive probes**: a 1 kΩ [[series-resistor|series resistor]] into a 50 Ω-terminated input forms a 21:1 divider presenting 1 kΩ DC [[impedance]], a cheap way to exploit a 1 GHz front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>
- **High-voltage differential probes** are essential for mains and power-supply work, allowing connections at arbitrary points without the [[ground]]-loop hazard of an earth-referenced probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>
- **Optically isolated fibre probes** are the only practical option for high-frequency, high-voltage switching measurements (such as GaN gate drive) where channel-to-channel time correlation must be maintained and differential probes cannot be used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>
- **[[current|Current]] probes** output a volts-per-amp signal that scopes can display directly as amps per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

At high frequencies, channel-to-channel probe delay differences matter: oscilloscopes include a probe-skew (delay) [[calibration]] function, and a centimetre-scale cable-length difference required a 262 ps correction in one demonstration at 160 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> Unterminated transmission lines produce reflections that can make a signal at the scope input effectively vanish at certain frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>

## Grounding and safety

The BNC shells and probe ground clips of a bench oscilloscope are connected to protective [[mains-earth|mains earth]], a safety requirement for the instrument itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Xg_niU86bhI" title="Wens 540 Handheld Oscilloscope Multimeter Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> Connecting the ground clip to any point in a device under test that is not at earth potential creates a low-impedance short through the probe shield, the scope chassis, the mains earth wiring, and back into the device, destroying the clip lead and potentially the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-279" title="EEVblog #279 - How NOT To Blow Up Your Oscilloscope!">279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup> This is the single most common way beginners damage equipment, and high-voltage differential probes or properly isolated instruments are the standard remedy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Handheld scope-meters achieve isolated, floating channels precisely because they are not earth-referenced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-807" title="EEVblog #807 - Mailbag">807</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup>

## Common pitfalls and artefacts

- **[[aliasing|Aliasing]]**: a scope without effective anti-aliasing can display a 10 MHz input as an apparent 1 Hz waveform at slow time bases, complete with a confident (and wrong) automatic measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-F0HQJIPcDYs" title="Oscilloscope Anti Alias?">•</a></sup>
- **Microphonics**: multilayer ceramic capacitors in scope front ends are piezoelectric, so tapping the case injects a visible signal; the effect is present in almost every scope on the market. The same effect lets a ×10 probe double as a crude vibration or acoustic sensor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - A Shocking Oscilloscope Problem!">983</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1743" title="EEVblog 1743 - Mechanical Vibration Detection with your Oscilloscope Probe">1743</a></sup>
- **Electrostatic pickup**: standing up from a chair can inject a roughly 100–120 MHz impulse into a shorted probe, reproducible across many scopes, bandwidths, and probe types, independent of the ground-lead loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14" title="EEVblog #14 - An unusual oscilloscope phenomenon!">14</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-20" title="EEVblog #20 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon Part 2">20</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21" title="EEVblog #21 - The Unusual Oscilloscope Phenomenon - Part 3">21</a></sup>
- **Probe-setting mismatch** producing an apparent inability to reach 1 mV/div (see Probes above).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-778" title="EEVblog #778 - Oscilloscope Vertical Confusion">778</a></sup>
- **Interpolation traps** when zooming deep-memory captures, where a sparsely sampled record is sin(x)/x-interpolated for display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup>

## Analysis features and techniques

Modern digital scopes apply math operators — add, subtract, multiply, divide, differentiate, integrate — to captured data in real time as well as on stopped acquisitions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-662" title="EEVblog #662- How &amp; Why to use Integration on an Oscilloscope">662</a></sup> Integration has direct application in power measurement, since true power in a pulsed load is the area under the current waveform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> Other established techniques include:

- **[[frequency-response|Frequency response]] (Bode) plotting** by sweeping a [[function-generator|function generator]] and recording amplitude at spot frequencies, or with a built-in frequency-response analyser; the scope's [[fft|FFT]] is generally unsuitable for this because a scope does not natively display against frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LbqnHtNPt9Y" title="Siglent Oscilloscope - Function Gen USB Connection Test">•</a></sup>
- **FFT spectral display**, where best resolution requires deliberately tuning centre frequency, span, and time base.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup>
- **Phase measurement** between channels by automatic measurement, cursors, or arcsine of voltage levels, with roughly half a dozen distinct methods available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup>
- **XY mode** for curve-tracer-style component characterisation, historically built into some 20 MHz analogue scopes as a component tester.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup>
- **Serial triggering and decode** (e.g. SPI), allowing data-level correlation between captured waveforms and [[firmware]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-240" title="EEVblog #240 - Power Supply Design Part 8">240</a></sup>
- **Power-supply [[ripple]] and noise** measurement with [[ac-coupling|AC coupling]], a 20 MHz bandwidth limit, and the lowest-noise front end available, ideally with a genuine 500 µV–1 mV/div range; the DC-offset control is used to null the rail voltage and zoom onto the ripple.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-765" title="EEVblog #765 - LED Panel Lighting Switching Noise">765</a></sup>
- **Reference waveforms**, stored captures displayed alongside live channels for comparison, complementing the four-channel capability now available even in sub-US$400 instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1324" title="EEVblog #1324 - Oscilloscope Reference Waveforms are USEFUL">1324</a></sup>
- **Roll mode** for very slow signals such as heart rate, typically entered automatically at slow time bases.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-665" title="EEVblog #665 - Polar Wearlink Heart Rate Transmitter Teardown">665</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>

## Market tiers and architecture

Entry-level digital scopes were redefined by the Rigol DS1052E, which established the roughly US$400 price point in the compact form factor pioneered by the Tektronix TDS220, the industry's first small real-time digital storage scope; its four-channel successor, the 50 MHz DS1054Z at US$399, became the benchmark entry instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup> Below that tier, [[usb|USB]] scopes and pocket "nano" scopes are regarded as poor value relative to a US$400 bench instrument or a US$50 used analogue scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> Above it, spending beyond roughly US$1,000 is generally more than a hobbyist or general-purpose lab needs, covering serial decode, mixed-signal analysis, and a few hundred megahertz of bandwidth well before that figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ln_XJDPKJlc" title="eevBLAB 103 - Is an $800 Oscilloscope OVERKILL?">•</a></sup>

The high-end market — Tektronix, Keysight, Rohde & Schwarz, and LeCroy — prices differently for structural reasons: high-end manufacturers may keep the same model in production for 10–20 years with managed last-time buys, because military, industrial, and government customers write procedures and test environments around a specific instrument and require consistent availability, something lower-tier manufacturers replacing models every couple of years do not provide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3t9G80wk0pk" title="eevBLAB 101 - Why Are Tektronix Oscilloscopes So Expensive?">•</a></sup> Within a product line, two-channel models exist mainly as a cost saving; on a 350 MHz MDO4000-class instrument the saving was roughly US$3,000 on a US$16,500 instrument, and multi-channel system requirements beyond eight channels are more often met by synchronising multiple scopes or modular acquisition systems.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1632" title="EEVblog 1632 - WHY sell 2CH Oscilloscopes? + Test Engineering">1632</a></sup>

Internally, the classic digital scope architecture funnels ADC data through an [[fpga|FPGA]] or [[asic|ASIC]] into dual-port memory that the CPU reads to update the display, making the CPU the bottleneck; the performance generation of the early 2010s solved this with custom ASICs handling acquisition, memory, and display plotting directly, relegating the CPU to a secondary role and enabling million-waveform-per-second update rates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> That class of ASIC has since appeared in instruments starting around US$450.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> A further generation shift has brought 12-bit resolution and 350 MHz four-channel instruments with 4 GS/s and 500M-point memory below US$1,000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup>
