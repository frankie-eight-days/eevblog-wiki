Quiescent current is the [[current]] a circuit or device consumes to perform its own function, independent of whatever it delivers to a load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> In a three-terminal [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] it is the small current that flows in the input pin and out the [[ground]] pin rather than to the output, so by Kirchhoff's current law the input current equals the quiescent current plus the [[output-current|output current]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> It matters because it is the floor on consumption: a load can be switched off or reduced to nothing, but quiescent current is paid continuously, which makes it the dominant term in [[battery-life|battery life]], [[standby-power|standby power]] and micropower design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup>

## Definition and the current-law view

A regulator has only three legs, so any current entering it must leave either through the output pin or through the ground pin; the ground-pin share is the little bit the regulator itself needs to do its job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> With a capacitive load only, the [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] is an open circuit at DC, so the output current equals the load current and nothing flows into the cap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> That gives the arithmetic directly: with a 50 microamp load and a 7.5 microamp quiescent current, the input draw is 57.5 microamps, with a worst case of perhaps 70 microamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

The same idea extends to whole products. A circuit's quiescent current is what it draws with no load or with its output disabled, and it appears as a fixed baseline on top of which the load current sits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> In pulsed systems the baseline is literal: a GSM handset shows a quiescent level with bursts of transmit current superimposed on it at a fixed repetition rate of 217 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-23" title="EEVblog #23 - GSM mobile phone audio design">23</a></sup>

## Reading it from a data sheet

Quiescent current is [[voltage]]- and temperature-dependent in ways the headline number hides. A regulator specified at 7.5 microamps typical may be as high as 14 microamps maximum, that maximum being the figure over the full temperature range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Parameters can also vary with the selected [[output-voltage|output voltage]], so the correct table must be used: the same part reads 7.5 microamps in the 3.3 V table but 18 microamps typical and 22 microamps maximum in the 15 V table.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Where an internal [[constant-current-source|constant current source]] sets the bias, quiescent current stays essentially flat across the input voltage range rather than rising with input voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

Some manufacturers split the specification in two. Quiescent current is then quoted for an output current of precisely zero — a no-load figure only — while a separate ground current parameter covers the loaded case, for instance 25 microamps for output currents up to 10 milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Technically the loaded ground current is the number a designer should use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Notes attached to these tables carry real content and should be read: they cover heat sinking, safe operating area, and the fact that line and load regulation figures may exclude self-heating, which matters when a part with meaningful quiescent current runs from a 40 V input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Data sheets are not static documents. A revision of the NE5532 removed a long list of parameters from the spec table while lowering the supply current from 8 milliamps to 6 milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup>

## When it can be ignored

The practical test is orders of magnitude. A linear regulator's quiescent current sits in the microamp region while a [[multimeter]]'s load sits in the milliamp region, and as a rule of thumb anything more than two orders of magnitude below the quantity of interest can be ruled out — that is a 1% error, which does not matter, and anything lower is simply insignificant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> This tolerance is what makes back-of-envelope design by inspection robust: a data-sheet figure that turns out to be double the assumed value changes nothing when it is still three orders of magnitude below the competing term.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> In engineering terms 100 microamps against an assumed 500 microamps is near enough, because the comparison is being made in decades, not percentages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

## Zener shunt regulation versus linear regulators

The classic demonstration of quiescent current as a design driver is the Zener shunt regulator. Even with no load current at all, a Zener regulating 5.1 V can be throwing away 50 to 70 milliamps purely to hold the rail, which makes it very inefficient at low currents and hopeless as a general [[voltage-regulator|voltage regulator]] driving no load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> A 7805 regulates the same 5 V while taking a negligible quiescent current by comparison.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Against a micropower linear regulator the gap is a factor of a thousand: 80 milliamps for the Zener circuit against 80 microamps for the regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> Experienced practice has long held Zener shunt circuits to be wasteful, and designers moved to linear regulators as soon as they became available; the remaining reason to use a Zener is that it is cheap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

## Minimum load current: the related trap

Quiescent current has a counterpart that catches designers of low-power circuits. The [[lm317|LM317]] requires a minimum load current of 3.5 milliamps to maintain regulation, which is its entire job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> The 78xx series has no minimum load current requirement but a higher quiescent current instead, so the two families end up in the same place for low-power work — neither is suitable for micropower circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Minimum load current specifications themselves carry conditions: half a milliamp may be specified only at an input range of 10 V, rising to 1 milliamp above that, with the detail buried in a note.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> Among jellybean regulators, quiescent currents around 4 milliamps typical with a 6 milliamp maximum are common, which rules them out for low-power applications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Modern parts do far better. A sub-cent SOT-23 LDO specifies 25 microamps of quiescent current — tens of microamps in practice — while still supplying 250 milliamps with 0.2 V of dropout at 90 mA output, so at a 100 mA load the input current effectively equals the output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Energy-harvesting parts go lower still: 950 nanoamps quiescent with no load on the output, and 450 nanoamps input quiescent current in ultra-low-voltage lockout mode, over a 2.7 to 20 V input range with up to 100 milliamps of output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup>

## Quiescent current as dissipation

Quiescent current is dissipated as heat inside the part, and at high supply voltages that dissipation is not trivial. A device with roughly 10 milliamps of quiescent current running from a 30 V supply dissipates 0.3 watts before it does any work at all, which is significant for a DIP-8 package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup> Confirming that op-amps draw their data-sheet quiescent values is a standard step when hunting for an unexplained hot spot on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>

## Switching converters and light-load modes

Switching converters carry their own quiescent penalty, which becomes dominant at light loads because it is paid regardless of output and is then compounded by conversion [[efficiency]]. A typical [[boost-converter|boost converter]] might specify 300 microamps of active quiescent current, on top of which the efficiency — perhaps 70 to 80% in real use rather than the headline 90% — further erodes what reaches the load, a combination that makes such converters a poor fit for low-current products.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-751" title="EEVblog #751 - How To Debunk A Product (The Batteriser)">751</a></sup> Device quiescent current is accordingly one of the first parameters checked when selecting a DC-DC converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

Charge pumps and switchers address this with light-load modes. The LM2776 uses PFM, or pulse-skipping, operation to minimise quiescent current at light load, allowing the charge pump to switch less when the output current is below 40 milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> The mode change is visible as a step in [[output-ripple|output ripple]] at the transition, since the device is changing [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] rather than merely reducing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup> Older single-mode parts such as the 7660 have no such mode; output ripple simply tracks output current, and part-to-part variation between vendors shows up as differences in quiescent current and efficiency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1115" title="EEVblog #1115 - Traps In Chips - And the 7660">1115</a></sup>

## Bench measurement

Quiescent current is measured with the load disconnected or the output disabled, and it is a fast diagnostic. On an unfamiliar or repaired supply the first check is for gross excess current faults: 90 milliamps for the standby draw of an old-school linear supply board is unremarkable, and the measurement is used to decide whether to proceed rather than to characterise anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup> The inverse case is a fault found by this method — a carbonised [[pcb|PCB]] that formed a low-[[impedance]] path across the output, measuring tens of kilohms before the failure and 47 ohms afterward, drew enough quiescent current to trip a current-limited source into hiccup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup> A functioning supply of the same type draws about 2 watts quiescent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup>

The no-load draw of a switching supply module can differ between output-off and output-on states even with nothing connected — 0.39 W with the output off against about 0.5 W with it on — so the output-on figure is the one that belongs in an efficiency calculation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> The output-off figure represents the module's own electronics and does not change with the programmed output voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1031" title="EEVblog #1031 - $25 DPS3003 PSU Module Characterisation">1031</a></sup> Larger bench supplies show the same fixed overhead: 940 milliwatts quiescent for a 360 W supply, and around 15 to 20 milliamps for a linear-plus-switching design including its [[lcd|LCD]] and [[microcontroller]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

Typical measured values across instruments and modules span several decades: 2.8 microamps for a low-power handheld multimeter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup> 1.3 milliamps in normal operation and about 1 milliamp in DC volts for a Fluke 117, rising to 20 milliamps with the [[backlight]] on — currents low enough against a roughly 800 mAh [[alkaline-battery|alkaline cell]] to comfortably meet a 400-hour battery life claim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup> A DIN-rail supply draws 2 mA at 9 V input including its indicator [[led|LED]], falling to 1.1 mA at 48 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1656" title="EEVblog 1656 - MAILBAG: ElectroPermanent Magnet, DIN PSU&#x27;s, Sponge, Beelink EQ14 N150 PC">1656</a></sup> An electropermanent magnet driver idles at about 27 mA even though its coil takes no power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1656" title="EEVblog 1656 - MAILBAG: ElectroPermanent Magnet, DIN PSU&#x27;s, Sponge, Beelink EQ14 N150 PC">1656</a></sup> A boost module shows 4.6 mA at 12 V input with no load,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup> an electric fence controller 14 mA,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1277" title="EEVblog #1277 - Electric Fence Controller Teardown">1277</a></sup> a precision [[current-source|current source]] circuit about 4 mA,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> and a 1980s boombox around 70 mA — though with a genuine mechanical power switch, off means off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1756" title="EEVblog 1756 - Sharp 1980&#x27;s Boombox Bodge Repair">1756</a></sup> A [[usb|USB]] programmer that does nothing but flash an LED draws roughly 100 milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup>

## Standby power and battery life

Claims of zero standby power do not survive measurement, since the external supply that feeds the switched-off product still draws from the mains — a plug pack for a 24 V DC monitor measures 300 mW.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-971" title="EEVblog #971 - Zero Standby Power TV - BUSTED!">971</a></sup> Products that keep a switching stage running while nominally idle can draw a substantial quiescent current for nothing: a portable power station's [[inverter]] consumes 11 W with the output disconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1707" title="EEVblog 1707 - REPAIR: Goal Zero Yeti 400 Lithium Battery">1707</a></sup> Excessive quiescent current is a plausible explanation for equipment found with a flat [[battery]] after long storage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1720" title="EEVblog 1720 - Mailbag: USSR Bonanza + Logic Training + $69 Logic Analyzer">1720</a></sup> Quiescent current drawn by mains products in standby has been the specific target of criticism where the alternative is a battery-powered solution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

Where the load is intermittent, the quiescent baseline sets the achievable runtime. An LM3914 battery gauge draws roughly 5 milliamps with no LEDs lit; in bar mode each lit LED adds at least 2 mA on top of the chip's operating current, so switching to dot mode by leaving pin nine floating is what makes the design practical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

## Design practice

For a linear supply feeding a low-power circuit, the design method is to look up the regulator's quiescent current in the correct data-sheet table, add it to the measured or estimated load current, and check whether it changes the answer at all — usually it does not, and that conclusion is itself the useful result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1533" title="EEVblog 1533 - How To Estimate Product Battery Life">1533</a></sup> For electret microphones the equivalent step is to obtain the manufacturer's specification for the quiescent current, then size the bias [[resistor]] around that figure and the available supply rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-611" title="EEVblog #611 - Electret Microphone Design">611</a></sup>
