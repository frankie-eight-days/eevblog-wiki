Temperature coefficient — universally shortened to *tempco* — is the rate at which a component's parameter changes with temperature. Every component, regardless of type, has one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup> It is the parameter that decides whether a precision instrument holds its [[calibration]], because absolute error can be trimmed or calibrated away once, while drift with temperature reappears every time the ambient changes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup> Understanding that a temperature coefficient exists at all, alongside nonlinearity, is treated as a precondition for doing electronics rather than an advanced topic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup>

## Why it outranks absolute accuracy

In precision measurement the initial tolerance of a reference or a shunt is close to irrelevant. What matters is drift per degree Celsius, expressed as parts per million per degree.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> A [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] specified at plus or minus 5% absolute is perfectly usable if its tempco is low, because the absolute value is trimmed once at calibration and thereafter the part only has to stay where it was put.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> A reference standard with 5% absolute accuracy can still be worth a fortune if its tempco is low enough.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup>

The consequence for part selection is that parametric sorting by tolerance discards exactly the parts worth having: the best reference in the industry may appear on a distributor listing as a 3% part, and must be found by sorting on tempco instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> A reference of that class in a $10,000 [[multimeter]] is an ordinary catalogue chip costing tens of dollars; what makes the instrument is the trimming and the carefully engineered surrounding circuitry that preserves the low tempco.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

The same logic governs [[current]] shunts. A shunt of 1% tolerance is entirely adequate where the meter or supply calibrates the value out in software — what cannot be calibrated out is a shunt whose value moves with temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> A 10 milliohm precision shunt with essentially no tempco costs a fortune, and its accuracy grade can be as loose as 10% provided it does not change with temperature, mechanical stress, or anything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> Precision resistors bought purely for stability follow the same pattern: 1% parts costing hundreds of dollars each, chosen because they exhibit practically zero tempco and zero drift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup>

## Units and notation

Tempco is quoted in parts per million per degree Celsius, or equivalently as a percentage per degree. 25 ppm per degree C means a given [[resistor]] changes 25 parts in a million for each degree Celsius, which converts directly to 0.0025% per degree C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Some manufacturers instead specify per 10 degrees Kelvin — a meter quoting 0.2% per 10 K over a 0 to 55 degree range is using that convention.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-481" title="EEVblog #481 - Mailbag">481</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-482" title="EEVblog #482 - Retro Iskra Multimeter Teardown">482</a></sup> Others avoid ppm entirely and give total deviation over the full operating range in millivolts, or in least significant bits per degree, leaving the ppm figure to be calculated from the range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> Learning to work in ppm is unavoidable, since coefficients and accuracies are both commonly expressed that way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup>

Six-band resistors encode the coefficient in the final band: brown is 100 ppm, red 50 ppm, orange 15 ppm, yellow 25 ppm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-215" title="EEVblog #215 - Gaussian Resistors">215</a></sup> Tempco is also a standard [[parametric-search|parametric search]] field at the distributors, alongside tolerance, power, composition, and operating temperature range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1180" title="EEVblog #1180 - Component Parametric Search Tutorial">1180</a></sup>

## Resistors

Resistor tempco sets the floor on what a [[resistor-divider|resistive divider]] or shunt network can achieve. Ordinary thick-film chip resistors are deliberately formulated from special oxides so that their coefficient changes sign, giving a curve that is flat around room temperature and rises again both when heated and when cooled — the intent being that a circuit's behaviour does not move if its temperature wanders slightly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup> Precision metal-foil parts do far better: a Vishay 10k rated at plus or minus 0.005% absolute carries a tempco of plus or minus 0.2 ppm per degree C, with a further 0.005% of load-life drift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup> A metrology-grade 10k transfer resistor characterised at half-degree intervals specifies 0.1 ppm calibration uncertainty, 0.3 ppm per degree C tempco, and 1 ppm per year stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-733" title="EEVblog #733 - Mailbag">733</a></sup> A [[resistance]] calibrator's internal 10k, at 50 ppm accuracy and 3 ppm over 90 days, specifies 1.5 ppm per degree C, which is not dramatically better than a modern precision Vishay part costing around twenty dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup>

Where matching matters more than absolute value, resistor networks on ceramic substrate are used, since a common substrate gives a more even temperature coefficient across the elements and allows them to be matched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1391" title="EEVblog 1391 - Mailbag">1391</a></sup> The high-end approach appears in eight-and-a-half-digit meters and also in handheld instruments built around a ceramic hybrid divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1391" title="EEVblog 1391 - Mailbag">1391</a></sup> In a decade resistance box the tempco is frequently omitted from the published specification even when the tolerance is given: a 0.1% box may be built from 25 ppm resistors without that figure ever appearing on the panel, and designing such a box requires accounting for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-212" title="EEVblog #212 - DIY Decade Resistance Substitution Boxes">212</a></sup> Metrology-grade decade boxes go further and use the same material for wiring, contact pads, and wipers so that tempco and thermal EMF effects are common throughout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-461" title="EEVblog #461 - Genrad Decade Resistance Box Teardown">461</a></sup>

Dissipation matters as much as ambient. A shunt built as ten paralleled resistors dissipating well under its rating barely self-heats and therefore barely changes value, so low-grade parts work well there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Conversely, [[pcb|PCB]] copper has a different temperature coefficient from the [[current-shunt|current shunt]] bonded to it, so a shunt sensed through trace resistance rather than at its own sense terminals produces an error that is not merely offset but temperature-dependent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup>

## References, junctions, and semiconductors

[[voltage|Voltage]] references are the canonical tempco-dominated part. A DC voltage standard with a plus or minus 2% initial tolerance is unusable as bought, yet specifies 0.0001% or 1 ppm per degree Celsius, which makes it entirely usable once tweaked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> Reference-[[diode]] grades exist precisely to sell tempco: the 1N821 family runs up to the 1N829ARU top grade with the lowest coefficient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-210" title="EEVblog #210 - Krohn-Hite DC Voltage Standard Teardown &amp; Calibration">210</a></sup> The same tiering appears throughout the jellybean shunt references, where a higher grade at slightly higher cost buys better initial accuracy and better tempco, and where a common part works out at roughly 50 ppm per degree C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> A precision reference is often chosen over a trimmed regulator solely because the regulator cannot deliver the coefficient required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1438" title="EEVblog 1438 - The TOP 5 Jellybean Regulators &amp; References">1438</a></sup> Concrete grades in production instruments span a wide range: 5 ppm for the top-grade MAX6198AESA with 2 mV initial accuracy,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup> 15 ppm for an LT1009 in a sub-ten-dollar process meter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1248" title="EEVblog #1248 - INSANE! A 0.02% Process Meter for $9? WTF!">1248</a></sup> and the 100 ppm ICL8069 variant in a $25 multimeter, where a 50 ppm version exists but the cheaper grade is adequate for 10,000 counts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup>

At the junction level, the base-emitter drop of a [[bipolar-transistor|bipolar transistor]] moves by roughly 2 mV per degree C, which in a tracking pre-regulator makes the whole circuit's coefficient depend on that junction alone and produces about 100 millivolts of movement over a 50 degree range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-329" title="EEVblog #329 - Tracking Pre-Regulator LTspice Simulation Part 2">329</a></sup> Diode sensing exploits the same effect deliberately: a fast [[battery]] charger senses cell temperature with a diode right down on the contacts, which is crude but entirely sufficient when what is wanted is a temperature differential over a couple of minutes rather than absolute accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> Zener regulation calculations are only complete when the diode's coefficient is taken from the [[datasheet|data sheet]] and applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Integrated parts carry the effect internally — the tempco and absolute accuracy of an LM3914's internal 1.25 V reference and divider vary chip to chip, and the application notes recommend keeping external resistor values low so that the internal divider's coefficient does not swamp the design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup> A [[current-source|current source]] built from jellybean 2N3904s drifts about 15% over a 50 degree rise, roughly 0.3% per degree C — essentially the same coefficient as the purpose-built LM334.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup>

## Capacitors

[[ceramic-capacitor|Ceramic capacitor]] codes encode temperature behaviour directly: the first character sets the minimum temperature, the second the maximum, and the third the temperature coefficient, meaning the [[capacitance]] change over that range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> R corresponds to about plus or minus 15% and is markedly better than the V and U codes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> This must not be confused with the initial tolerance letter — an X7R or X5R part with a K tolerance is plus or minus 10% as purchased, while the plus or minus 15% figure is its variation over the full temperature range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> Aluminium electrolytics are far worse, varying from roughly +5% to −40% or −50%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> Even stable film dielectrics move: polypropylene and PET reference capacitors have a measurable, negative coefficient, and cooling one visibly raises its value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1496" title="EEVblog 1496 - Winning Mailbag">1496</a></sup>

## Sign, curvature, and measurement

A tempco specification is usually published as a symmetric plus-or-minus figure that says nothing about direction, yet real parts and instruments have a definite sign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-835" title="EEVblog #835 - Wekomm Resistance Standard Part 2">835</a></sup> A hand-selected, hand-aged resistance standard characterised at 5.5 degree intervals showed a slight negative coefficient, its resistance falling by a small fraction of an ohm-in-ten-thousand as temperature rose from 20 to 25 degrees.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-834" title="EEVblog #834 - Wekomm Resistance Standard">834</a></sup> Measuring that standard on a bench DMM produced a discrepancy roughly four times larger than the resistor itself could account for, which was attributed to the meter: its 10k range is specified at 2.5 ppm per degree C, with a data sheet footnote restricting that figure to conditions more than plus or minus 5 degrees from the calibration temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-834" title="EEVblog #834 - Wekomm Resistance Standard">834</a></sup> Deliberately changing only the meter's temperature reproduced the discrepancy almost exactly, confirming that the instrument's reference has a substantial negative coefficient, still within specification but plainly visible when measuring down in the [[noise]], along with thermal lag between the instrument and its surroundings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-835" title="EEVblog #835 - Wekomm Resistance Standard Part 2">835</a></sup>

Coefficients can also be nonmonotonic. The oxide chip resistors used as thermometers in dilution-refrigerator wiring work precisely because their coefficient changes sign, going flat around room temperature and rising in resistance both above and below it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1594" title="EEVblog 1594 - Inside a Quantum Computer! with Andrea Morello">1594</a></sup>

Verification is done by ramping the part or instrument in a thermal chamber. A thermal oven is one of the more obscure but genuinely useful lab items, since practically every component parameter — down to op-amp [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] — drifts with temperature, and a product intended for sale should be tested across the standard commercial operating range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup> A revised handheld meter fitted with an improved voltage reference and lower-tempco divider resistors is characterised the same way, ramped against reference meters in the chamber.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Wwz_fdU17aQ" title="121GW Multimeter Thermal Test">•</a></sup> Results are often undramatic when the part is within its rated range: a meter specified to hold accuracy to 50 degrees C loses only about one significant digit over a 20 degree internal rise,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup> and an op-amp's AC performance shifts around 0.3% at 39 degrees.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup> Simulation offers a cheaper first pass — a temperature sweep over 0 to 50 degrees in 5 degree steps reveals spreads that a nominal-temperature simulation hides entirely, and skipping it is a common cause of a circuit that works in simulation and misbehaves once built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-301" title="EEVblog #301 - LTspice Temperature Sweep Tutorial">301</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-329" title="EEVblog #329 - Tracking Pre-Regulator LTspice Simulation Part 2">329</a></sup>

Below the tempco of individual components lies thermal EMF. In an eight-and-a-half-digit-class meter whose own coefficient is of the order of parts per million, a temperature gradient across components or dissimilar-metal joints generates small thermoelectric voltages that shift the reading; simply breathing on the input circuitry changes it significantly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> This is why metrology laboratories hold voltage standards, thermal voltage converters, and resistance standards in a tightly controlled environment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1039" title="EEVblog #1039 - Keysight Metrology Standards Lab">1039</a></sup> Mechanical consequences appear too: PCB cutouts are milled around a critical reference to isolate it from thermal expansion of the board, since even a good laminate expands enough to stress a package's pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup>

## Instrument and system specifications

Whole-instrument tempco is quoted the way component tempco is. A benchtop DMM adds 0.0004 of reading per degree C outside a plus or minus 5 degree window around calibration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> A bench multimeter's nominal 5 ppm per degree C is around four times better than a voltage-current generator's 20-odd ppm per degree C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-576" title="EEVblog #576 - Advantest R6142 Current Voltage Generator">576</a></sup> Electronic loads in the 0.05% class commonly share a 100 ppm coefficient across model variants,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> a triple-output supply specifies 100 ppm plus 3 mV,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> and a wireless meter's temperature module quotes 0.01% of full scale per degree C alongside half a degree absolute accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup> High-precision loads justify very low tempco metal for their main input shunts on the strength of 0.05% class voltage and 0.1% class [[current-measurement|current measurement]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup>

Some materials are effectively immune. The potassium lead silicate isostatic glass used in glass delay lines has a coefficient of a couple of ppm per degree C at most and is described as zero temperature coefficient glass; the delay through such a line changes with neither [[frequency]] nor temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup> At the other extreme, whole systems are governed by tempco: [[solar-power-system|photovoltaic]] output [[efficiency]] falls as panels get hotter, which is why panels sealed in thick glass without [[airflow]] underneath underperform rooftop mounting, and why output tracks the thermal behaviour of the panel through a day of intermittent cloud.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-743" title="EEVblog #743 - Solar Roadways Test Results">743</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup> Even a resistive fault behaves this way — a short measured at milliamp test currents will not hold the same resistance at ten amps, because it heats up and has a tempco of its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-798" title="EEVblog #798 - Mailbag">798</a></sup>

Not everything needs the treatment. Regular carbon resistors are used freely alongside precision parts wherever tempco and precision are not critical,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup> and a set of precision calibration weights needs no particular coefficient at all, since thermal expansion changes their size rather than their mass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-177" title="EEVblog #177 - Baby Scale Calibration">177</a></sup>
