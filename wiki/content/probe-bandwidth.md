Probe bandwidth is the [[frequency]] limit of the probe itself, as distinct from the [[bandwidth]] of the [[oscilloscope]] it plugs into. Because the probe sits ahead of the instrument's front end, it sets the true ceiling of the measurement: a 200 MHz scope fed by a probe that rolls off at 8 MHz is an 8 MHz system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> The specification printed on a probe is normally its best case — the times 10 attenuating position — and several common configurations fall an order of magnitude or more below it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

## The times one trap

A switchable times 1 / times 10 passive probe does not carry its rated bandwidth into the times 1 position. A 100 MHz or 200 MHz rated probe has a drastically lower bandwidth there, typically an order of magnitude or more down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> Published figures cluster tightly: DC to 8 MHz on one 350 MHz rated probe,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> DC to 6 MHz on a Siglent PP510 sold as a 100 MHz probe,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup> about 5 MHz on another,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup> roughly 6.5 MHz on a typical switchable unit,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> and around 10 MHz as a generous upper bound.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1362" title="EEVblog #1362 - A Very Skookum Mailbag">1362</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup> Six megahertz is close to a universal standard value for the times 1 position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup>

The consequence shows up in [[rise-time|rise time]] as much as in frequency. The same probe specified at DC to 6 MHz in times 1 lists a 58 ns rise time there against 1 ns at full times 10 bandwidth;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> another compares 900 ps in times 10 against 40 ns in times 1.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup> The cause is not the switch shorting the divider through but the compensation network in the probe head, and the loss is real: on a 350 MHz rated probe in times 1 mode, measured response was 0.707 at 10.1 MHz, with severe attenuation by 150 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-453" title="EEVblog #453 - Mysteries of x1 Oscilloscope Probes Revealed">453</a></sup>

Measured performance can beat the [[datasheet]]. A Rigol PVP3150 specified at DC to 20 MHz in times 1 peaks slightly — under half a dB — at around 25 MHz, with an actual −3 dB point in the region of 50 to 60 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup> A Siglent PP510 specified at 6 MHz rolls off smoothly with no pre-peak, reaching −3 dB just over 10 MHz and 5.5 dB down at 20 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup>

This matters directly for [[noise]] work. The times 1 position is preferred for measuring supply [[ripple]] and noise because the signal is not divided down, giving better fidelity;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> but noise is conventionally measured over a 20 MHz bandwidth — the reason oscilloscopes provide 20 MHz bandwidth limits — and a standard times 1 probe rolling off at 6 MHz is not delivering that bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> A probe that genuinely reaches 20 MHz in times 1 is therefore unusually useful for the application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup>

## Passive probe ceilings

Passive probe technology tops out well below active probing. Most passive probes stop around 500 MHz;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup> a 700 MHz passive probe, such as those shipped even with the 1.5 GHz models of the Agilent 4000X series, represents strong engineering;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup> and only one or two on the market reach a gigahertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup> The Tektronix TPP1000 is a 1 GHz passive probe at 300 V, 10 MΩ, and 3.9 pF [[input-capacitance|input capacitance]], costing roughly 800 dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

The reason passive probes are limited is deliberate construction. Probe cable is lossy [[coaxial-cable|coax]], using a resistive nichrome-type conductor with a wiggle in the middle rather than plain copper, so that the probe is matched to the 1 MΩ-and-[[capacitance]] front end of an oscilloscope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup> Plain coaxial cable achieves far higher bandwidth than any passive probe but is less forgiving, since it must be terminated properly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup> Purpose-built low-[[impedance]] resistive probes made from coax can reach many gigahertz, even 10 GHz, when constructed and terminated correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

Fixed times 10 probes, supplied with higher-bandwidth scopes, avoid the handle switch entirely and can be optimised for performance — 500 MHz with the same 10 MΩ [[input-impedance|input impedance]] and 9 MΩ divider [[resistor]] as the switchable types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup> For general work a fixed times 10 probe is the better default, since the times 1 switch is rarely used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1367" title="EEVblog #1367 - 5 Types of Oscilloscope Passive Probes COMPARED">1367</a></sup>

## Active, differential, and isolated probes

Above the passive ceiling the cost rises sharply: active probes start in the four-digit range and run to five digits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup> Typical examples are a 2 GHz probe with a 10:1 divider ratio and 1 MΩ input impedance, and a 1 GHz Siglent active probe at 1 MΩ and 1.2 pF.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup> A 12 GHz Infiniium 1169A probe costs on the order of 11,000 Australian dollars on its own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ulSTHy5kItM" title="$140K Agilent Oscilloscope Unboxing">•</a></sup> Where an active probe and the best passive probe share the same nominal bandwidth, the difference is input capacitance — 1.2 pF against 3.9 pF — because capacitance is what dominates loading at high frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup> Accessory choice also enters the specification: long leads plugged directly into an active probe head are quoted at around 500 MHz, against a rigid probe tip with offset [[ground]] for full performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1368" title="EEVblog #1368 - Active Oscilloscope Probes COMPARED (Part 2)">1368</a></sup>

High-[[voltage]] differential probes sit lower. A benchmark HVP70 is a 70 MHz part;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> the Micsig MDP700 family spans 100, 150, and 200 MHz across the MDP700, MDP701, and MDP702.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup> Differential probes built around a [[differential-amplifier|differential amplifier]] architecture are inherently slower than isolated optical probes, which behave like active probes and can reach a gigahertz or beyond.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup>

Beyond roughly 100 MHz, differential probe bandwidth is largely academic because the connection dominates. With leads flying loose, a 300 megahertz high-voltage differential probe has little point: "the probing solution is everything".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Seventy megahertz is more than enough once probing is factored in, and even a 20 MHz differential probe is decently useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Where genuine high-frequency differential measurement is required, a fibre-optic differential probe is the better answer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup> Bandwidth also carries a noise penalty — the lower-bandwidth 70 MHz probe is measurably quieter than the 100 MHz one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

Probe bandwidth is nonetheless the thing that decides whether fast switching content is visible at all. A 1 MHz switching node extends into tens and even hundreds of megahertz through its harmonics, so common-mode rejection and bandwidth must both be assessed at those harmonic frequencies rather than at the fundamental.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1557" title="EEVblog 1557 - Micsig SigOFIT Optical Fibre Probe + GaN Experiment">1557</a></sup> Manufacturer datasheets frequently stop short of that: a 70 MHz probe whose [[common-mode-rejection-ratio|CMRR]] is specified only to 10 MHz hides a sharp degradation above it, measuring −21 dB at 70 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1521" title="EEVblog 1521 - CMRR Full Edit">1521</a></sup>

## Matching probe to instrument

A probe only needs to outrun the scope in front of it. The Rigol DS1054Z ships four RP2200 switchable probes rated 150 MHz with a nominal 17 pF input capacitance in times 10 — ordinary parts, but ample for a 50 MHz instrument, so probe performance is not the limit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Under controlled comparison on a 100 MHz scope, a supplied 150 MHz probe and better probes produce essentially the same result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-707" title="EEVblog #707 - Rigol Oscilloscope Probe Performance">707</a></sup> Similarly, on a 500 MHz scope a 500 MHz, 11 pF passive probe makes probe bandwidth largely irrelevant to the outcome.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup>

Physical probing fixtures carry their own bandwidth. A hands-free PCBite holder is specified at 3 dB down at 200 MHz with 14 to 18 pF of input capacitance, adequate for general-purpose use, while its SP10 passive 1:1 hands-free probe falls back to the usual times 1 territory of about 10 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup>

## Setting and verifying probe bandwidth

Some oscilloscopes allow the probe's bandwidth to be entered independently of the channel [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]]. On the R&S MXO4 a user-defined probe entry accepts an arbitrary probe bandwidth — one channel at 700 MHz alongside another at 20 MHz, and settings down to 850 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> The same facility on a Keysight MSO4 allows a probe bandwidth to be dialled in and taken down into the kilohertz region, acting as front-end filtering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> The setting is not cosmetic in measurement workflows: changing modes can reset the probe definition, requiring the user-defined bandwidth to be re-entered at the probe's actual figure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1631" title="EEVblog 1631 - $230 Micsig MDP700 HV Differential Probe Review">1631</a></sup>

Verification is done against the datasheet and, where possible, by measurement. A [[frequency-response|frequency response]] analyser sweeping the probe in times 1 mode from 100 kHz to 100 MHz yields the true −3 dB point and phase response directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EWR4RQPzB6U" title="x1 Oscilloscope Probe SHOOTOUT - Rigol vs Siglent">•</a></sup> A fast-edge pulse generator serves the same purpose from the time domain, verifying bandwidth and step response of the whole probing system [[via]] the 0.35 rise-time relationship for a Gaussian response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-433" title="EEVblog #433 - Mailbag">433</a></sup> Datasheets are not always complete — the sheet supplied in the box with one probe omitted the times 1 bandwidth entirely, which appeared only in the manufacturer's series datasheet.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup> Checking the specific probe's rated bandwidth before trusting a measurement is the standing precaution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>
