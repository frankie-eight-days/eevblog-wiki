A bridge rectifier is an arrangement of four diodes that converts an AC input into a pulsating DC output, steering [[current]] through the load in the same direction regardless of input polarity. It is the standard front-end rectification stage in both linear and switch-mode power supplies, and it appears throughout electronic equipment in roles ranging from mains conversion to [[multimeter]] [[input-protection|input protection]]. Functionally, "all the diode bridge rectifier is doing is current steering where the current needs to go."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup>

## Operation and characteristics

In a full-wave bridge, two diodes conduct on each half-cycle, so the current path always includes two [[diode]] drops in series. Each drop is nominally about 0.6 V, rising toward 1 V at higher currents, so a bridge can waste around 2 V of the available headroom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> Because the output polarity is fixed regardless of how the AC (or unpolarised DC) input is connected, equipment fitted with a bridge on its input can be hooked up either way around with no concern for polarity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup>

Full-wave rectification doubles the [[ripple]] [[frequency]] seen by the following filter [[capacitor]]: 50 Hz or 60 Hz mains produces 100 Hz or 120 Hz ripple, whereas a half-wave rectifier leaves ripple at the mains frequency itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> The DC produced from rectified mains relates to the AC figure by the 0.707 factor: 350 V DC corresponds to roughly 245 V AC, which permits a rectified-DC-fed supply to be substituted directly for mains in some equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1657" title="EEVblog 1657 - DUMPSTER Samsung 75&quot; QLED One Connect TV">1657</a></sup>

At power-up, the discharged bulk capacitor acts as a [[short-circuit|short circuit]], and inrush current is limited only by wiring [[resistance]] and the equivalent DC resistance of the bridge itself; this is why such supplies commonly use slow-blow rather than fast-blow fuses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup>

## Role in power supplies

The canonical offline [[power-supply|power supply]] front end runs mains through a [[fuse]], common-mode choke, X- and Y-class suppression capacitors and MOVs, then into the bridge rectifier, whose output feeds the high-[[voltage]] bulk capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-533" title="EEVblog #533 - LED Fluoro Tube Teardown">533</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup> That bulk capacitor must be rated for the rectified peak — 400 V or 450 V parts are typical on 240 V mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dLlhoUHlnjE" title="LED Flicker 3 - More Electric Boogaloo">•</a></sup> In supplies without [[power-factor-correction|power factor correction]], the bridge feeds the bulk storage capacitor directly, and the resulting short conduction angle is precisely the condition a PFC stage is inserted to correct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup>

Linear supplies follow the same pattern at lower voltage: [[transformer-tap|transformer tap]], bridge, filter cap, then a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] such as a 7805, [[lm317|LM317]] or LM723 with pass [[transistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-C_tXWwcyZlc" title="Dumpster HP3488A Switch Teardown + Silverbrook STORY">•</a></sup> Multi-channel instruments typically give each isolated channel its own transformer tap, bridge rectifier and filter, with separate smaller bridges for control circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-621" title="EEVblog #621- Stanford Research SR650 Repair - Part 2">621</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup> A centre-tapped arrangement with two series filter capacitors produces a split rail — each capacitor sees only half the total voltage, giving, for example, ±24 V from a 48 V overall rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

In a crude capacitive-dropper supply, the simplest possible regulated mains supply is a series capacitor or [[resistor]] feeding a diode bridge and a [[zener-diode|Zener diode]]; the bridge alone does not regulate — a regulation element is required.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1482" title="EEVblog 1482 - Mains Capacitor Zener Regulator Circuit">1482</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> Undersized dropper capacitors starve the bridge of voltage, a failure that leaves a 24 V [[relay]] seeing only 9–13 V and unable to pull in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup>

## Power dissipation and thermal design

Bridge rectifiers are power semiconductors and their thermal treatment is a visible marker of design quality. Heavier units are bolted to dedicated heatsinks, sometimes with temperature monitoring.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-30" title="EEVblog #30 - Jaycar Bench Lab Power Supply Review">30</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup> Some designs mount several bridges — main and auxiliary — on a single shared [[heat-sink|heatsink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> [[airflow|Airflow]] matters: placing the bridge directly in the fan stream, ahead of the main heatsink, is regarded as sound thermal layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup> Cheaper practice omits the heatsink entirely, relying on heat-shrink sleeving and extra lead length for a little dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-462" title="EEVblog #462 - Video Editing PC Assembly">462</a></sup> A lab power supply bridge measured peaking over 90 °C under full load is cause for concern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup>

Small ceramic capacitors placed directly across each diode inside the bridge are a recognised technique for suppressing higher-frequency transient [[noise]] generated by the diodes, even though the fundamental is at mains frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1156" title="EEVblog #1156 - Focal Professional CMS40 Studio Monitor Teardown">1156</a></sup> High-voltage isolation slots routed into the [[pcb|PCB]] around the bridge are standard practice on mains-connected boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup>

## Multimeter input protection

A bridge rectifier is a core element of multimeter current-input protection. Connected across the [[current-shunt-resistor|current shunt resistor]], it clamps the fault voltage to a few diode drops so that a gross overload — voltage applied to a current jack — blows the [[hrc-fuse|HRC fuse]] rather than destroying the precision shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup> The bridge configuration is used precisely because the applied fault may be of either polarity, or AC; the clamp works either way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> In the Fluke 27, the bridge plus four additional series diodes gives a 3.6 V nominal clamp, the extra diodes raising the clamp voltage to suit a higher [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]]; where burden voltage is under two diode drops (about 1.2 V), the bridge can simply be shorted out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Variants using four, five or six discrete diodes in bridge configuration serve the same purpose, and a surface-mount DF10S-type package rated at 1,000 V is typical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

The protection diodes need be neither fast nor high power — ordinary 1N4007 parts suffice — because the energy only has to be withstood for the time the fuse takes to open: "Fuses don't blow instantly. They take seconds to blow."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The 10 A range is normally excluded from bridge protection, since its heavy shunt and direct current path take care of themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> A meter lacking a PTC, MOVs, HRC fuses and a diode bridge on its inputs is, as engineering judgment, not well designed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>

## Other applications

- **Average-responding RMS meters** use what is effectively a full-wave bridge to produce the absolute value of the input — flipping the negative half up — before the averaging and [[calibration]] stages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup>
- **Transient clamping**: bridges across balanced audio-amplifier inputs clamp extreme transients to the supply rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>
- **Earth-leakage breakers**: one implementation places the trip [[solenoid]] in series with the AC side of a bridge, with the thyristor across the bridge output, so that tripping shorts the bridge and removes its own power, latching everything off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup>
- **Solenoid drive**: automatic transfer switches power their changeover solenoids through individual diode bridges wired essentially across the mains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1500" title="EEVblog 1500 - Automatic Transfer Switch REVERSE ENGINEERED">1500</a></sup>
- **Energy harvesting**: integrated converters for piezoelectric sources embed a full-wave bridge to rectify the AC element output, followed by a buck converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup>
- **Synchronous rectification**: a controller driving four MOSFETs in the bridge arrangement replaces the lossy diodes; "the low on-resistance of the MOSFETs eliminates the loss," recovering the roughly 2 V a diode bridge would otherwise drop, with parts such as the LT4320/LT4220 handling up to 72 V peak at 60 Hz, or 600 Hz for the LT4320.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup>
- **[[led|LED]] retrofit tubes** exist in a type carrying a bridge rectifier off each pin; these are hazardous to install live and the fitting should be powered down first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-362" title="EEVblog #362 - LED Tube Lighting Install &amp; Theory">362</a></sup>

Cost-reduced products are frequently identified by what is missing: fake [[usb|USB]] chargers substitute a single half-wave rectifier for a full bridge,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-388" title="EEVblog #388 - Fake Apple USB Charger Teardown">388</a></sup> and cheap mains lamps use a capacitive divider into a bridge with no constant-current control at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1278" title="EEVblog #1278 - Mailbag">1278</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1191" title="EEVblog #1191 - Digital Energy Savers BUSTED!">1191</a></sup>

## Testing and failure modes

A bridge is checked in or out of circuit with a [[diode-test|diode test]]: from the negative output terminal to each AC input, and from each AC input to the positive output terminal, each junction should read one diode drop, about half a volt; the output terminals should also be checked for a short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1726" title="EEVblog 1726 - REPAIR of an EV Charger (Unusual Twist!)">1726</a></sup> Bridges usually fail open, which presents plainly as absent [[output-voltage|output voltage]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> A rarer and harder-to-find mode is intermittent thermal failure, in which the bridge drops the rectified rail under load once warm and recovers on cooling — repeatable, and capable of dragging down every derived rail in a multi-tap linear supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> In vintage equipment the apparent culprit is often not the bridge at all but decades-old dry [[solder]] joints with inadequate flow-through on its pins, cured by reflowing or replacement as a matter of course; pads on 35-year-old boards may lift during the work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup>

Where a bridge feeds an under-voltage symptom, the bridge itself is rarely the root cause: a starved dropper capacitor or collapsed upstream rail is more likely, and the rail voltage at the bridge output should be measured first — Dave Jones's own stated [[troubleshooting]] rule is "Thou shall measure voltages," offered after he replaced a heater's bridge rectifier unnecessarily before finding the real fault in the dropper capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1481" title="EEVblog 1481 - Dodgy Dangerous Heater REPAIR">1481</a></sup> When measuring [[capacitance]] in circuit, a test signal of 0.1 V RMS rather than 0.5 V RMS is used specifically so that bridge rectifiers and other active junctions in the surrounding circuit are never turned on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup>
