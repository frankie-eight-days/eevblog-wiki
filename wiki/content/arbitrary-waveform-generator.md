An arbitrary waveform generator (AWG, or "arb gen") is a signal source that plays back a user-defined sequence of samples through a digital-to-analog converter, rather than synthesising only the fixed shapes a conventional [[function-generator|function generator]] can produce.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> The distinction is a hard one: a plain DDS generator cannot do arbitrary [[waveform]] capability at all, because the shape is baked into the synthesis engine rather than held in memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> Because the waveform is just data, an arb gen can reproduce a captured signal, a mathematically defined curve, a physiological waveform, or a static DC level, which makes it one of the more broadly useful boxes on a bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup>

## How the signal is produced

Modern arbitrary waveform generators do not generate the signal in the analog domain the way older instruments did; they clock samples out of memory into a DAC, typically a 16-bit part, with even inexpensive units using 14- or 16-bit converters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> The depth of that [[sample-memory|sample memory]] sets how much waveform detail is available: an isolated [[oscilloscope]] design used a 4K arbitrary buffer, which is not large but still supports a useful range of shapes, and it was implemented not in the [[fpga|FPGA]] but in an Analog Devices part with an arbitrary waveform generator built into it, giving a 0 to 65 MHz range at very low distortion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup> A scope-internal arb gen of the same era offered 10 bits of vertical resolution, 100 megasamples per second and 8K of sample memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup>

Physically the output chain is simple and repetitive across designs: a DAC, an LC reconstruction filter under a shield can, a set of relays for range and coupling switching, and a 50 Ω buffer driver into the BNC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup> One 14-bit scope used two Burr-Brown DACs driven by a dedicated Spartan-6 FPGA, with a substantial [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] spent on relays and analog conditioning for the arb output alone — no fewer than eight relays serving the arb gen and pass/fail outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1004" title="EEVblog #1004 - Owon XDS3202A 14bit Oscilloscope Teardown">1004</a></sup> Elsewhere the whole function is folded into a custom [[asic|ASIC]], as with the ADG 395C that drives a 50 MHz arbitrary waveform generator and most likely the pattern generator output alongside it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qE-SwtYQM6E" title="Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope Pattern Generator">•</a></sup>

## Standalone instruments

Dedicated arb gens span an enormous range. Mid-range bench units include an 80 MHz, 500 megasample per second function/arbitrary waveform generator,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> a two-channel 160 MHz, 500 megasample per second model with two separate outputs plus two sync outputs and a hardware [[frequency-counter|frequency counter]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-323" title="EEVblog #323 - Rigol Scratch&#x27;n&#x27;Sniff">323</a></sup> and a 120 MHz, 1.2 gigasample per second [[touchscreen]] model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> At the top of the mainstream range sits a four-channel, 500 MHz, 2.5 gigasample per second generator with a 10-inch touchscreen.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fM4bbKVw2og" title="Uni-T 500MHz 4CH ARB Signal Generator Unboxing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1625" title="EEVblog 1625 - Electronex: Scope Wars: The Rise of Uni-T">1625</a></sup> Beyond that are laboratory instruments running at 24 gigasamples per second and weighing around 20 kilograms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-358" title="EEVblog #358 - Electronex 2012">358</a></sup>

A related class combines several sources in one chassis. The Agilent 81160A is a pulse function arbitrary [[noise]] generator — a pulse generator, function generator, arbitrary waveform generator and noise generator in one two-channel, 500 MHz instrument with gold BNC outputs — costing around $20,000, with an optional 660 Mbit pattern generator adding roughly another six thousand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-309" title="EEVblog #309 - Mailbag">309</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup> Modular [[usb|USB]] instruments follow the same idea in a stackable die-cast form factor, pairing an AWG with a scope and a 26.5 GHz VNA in matching enclosures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u22duJf4n1Q" title="Keysight P9336A USB AWG Teardown">•</a></sup>

## Arb gens built into oscilloscopes

Integrating a waveform generator into a scope is a well-established practice, but the integrated version is almost always a reduced one. Most scope-internal generators are limited to about 20 or 25 MHz of [[bandwidth]], which becomes a real constraint when the internal [[frequency-response|frequency response]] analyser is driven from the internal source — the sweep can only go as high as the generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LbqnHtNPt9Y" title="Siglent Oscilloscope - Function Gen USB Connection Test">•</a></sup> A 100 MHz built-in generator is unusual enough to be worth noting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-LbqnHtNPt9Y" title="Siglent Oscilloscope - Function Gen USB Connection Test">•</a></sup> Some designs improve on the norm: 50 MHz built in rather than 25 and integrated rather than supplied as an external box,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup> or dual 100 MHz arb gens on a dedicated board identifiable by its relays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup>

Even where the shape memory is present, the surrounding feature set is often thin. A 50 MHz integrated generator with sine, square, ramp, pulse, noise, DC and arb — with an audible [[relay]] click on switching to DC — had no modulation capability at all, which is a significant omission given that modulation is one of the main reasons to reach for a generator during scope work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> Another integrated generator offered AM and FM modulation but no FSK.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> A [[firmware]]-added arb capability that could store a captured waveform and replay it repetitively, automatically inheriting the captured amplitude and setting a 200 Hz repetition rate, still lacked single-shot output and was not a full-featured generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup> The engineering conclusion is consistent: an internal arb gen is a genuine convenience and worth having, but a serious high-performance arb still means a dedicated instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup>

Where the arb gen is absent it is usually a deliberate cost decision. Scopes have shipped with unpopulated cutouts for the two extra BNCs an arb output would need,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1503" title="EEVblog 1503 - Rigol HDO4000 12bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1503</a></sup> with the hardware fitted but the function locked behind a software licence,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup> or with the capability physically absent as one of the features dropped to reach a $699 price point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup> At least one 500 MHz scope offered no arb gen even as an option, making it a deliberately barebones bandwidth-for-price product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> Others sell the function as an external doohickey that plugs into the scope over USB and integrates with its software — a $268 option for 25 MHz and 125 megasamples per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup>

## Waveform libraries and editing

Beyond sine, square, ramp, pulse, DC and noise, the standard built-in set typically includes sinc, exponential rise and a cardiac or ECG waveform, plus a range of built-in math functions such as exponentials, window functions and trigonometric shapes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-972" title="EEVblog #972 - Operating Chips Outside Their Spec">972</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup> Waveforms can generally be uploaded over USB or LAN, or loaded from a text or CSV file.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> More capable software front ends allow the waveform to be defined by an equation edited directly on the graph, or assembled from multiple segments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> At the small end of the scale, an LCR tweezer's [[signal-generator|signal generator]] accepted an arbitrary waveform of up to 100 points edited as a text file on a PC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup>

Providing an arb gen with a file loader but no built-in waveform library is a design failure, particularly in an educational lab environment where a built-in set is exactly what users expect from an arbitrary function generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup>

## Using an arb gen as a precision DC source

Because the output is a DAC, an arbitrary waveform generator can serve as a fine-resolution DC [[voltage-source|voltage source]] when no calibrator or spare supply is available, and most units have a DC option that goes unnoticed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> With a 14- or 16-bit converter, even a no-name unit bought for around $100 can produce precisely stepped small voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup> The one caveat is the 50 Ω series output [[resistance]], which must be accounted for if the output feeds an external [[voltage-divider|voltage divider]], or the resulting voltages will be wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1560" title="EEVblog 1560 - TIP: Use Your Arb Gen as a High Resolution DC Voltage Source!">1560</a></sup>

## Cost structure

The gap between the parts in an AWG and its retail price is large but explicable. A bill-of-materials analysis of a $5,800 four-channel generator put the all-in cost to design, develop, test and assemble each unit at well over five or six hundred dollars, into $800 and possibly $1,000 — not $80, and not $2,000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Non-recurring engineering dominates the difference: assuming around ten employees for a year or two, at least $500,000 in labour and company overhead before management costs, and plausibly a million or several million.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> On top of that sit packaging and accessories, dealer margins that are certainly not five percent, and headroom for the manufacturer's own profit and for later discounting, all of which is captured by a roughly 2.5× cost multiplier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> The same arithmetic explains why an arb option on a mainstream scope carried an $1,100 list price when a standalone dual-channel 20 MHz generator with arbitrary capability could be had for well under a thousand dollars — the option is priced against the platform, not the parts, and at one point amounted to five dollars' worth of components sold for a thousand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup>
