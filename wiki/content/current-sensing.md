Current sensing is the measurement of [[current]] flowing in a circuit, almost always by converting it into a [[voltage]] that ordinary analogue circuitry can handle. The dominant technique passes the current through a low-value [[resistor]] — a shunt — and measures the voltage developed across it, which is then amplified and fed to a [[comparator]], a control loop, or an ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup> The alternative family of techniques senses the [[magnetic-field]] the current produces, using a [[current-transformer]] or a Hall effect device, and buys galvanic isolation at the cost of sensitivity and DC response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-769" title="EEVblog #769 - Mailbag">769</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup> Current sensing is the foundation of constant-current regulation, [[current-limiting]], overload protection, energy metering and power profiling, and it appears in some form in nearly every piece of powered equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup>

## The shunt resistor

The shunt is placed in series with the current path and the differential voltage across it is tapped off by two traces running to an amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-946" title="EEVblog #946 - Apple (Raspberry) Pi Cluster - PART 2">946</a></sup> The signal is inherently differential and floating at whatever potential the current path happens to sit at, so a differential amplifier across the sense resistor is the natural front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Shunt values in practice run from a few milliohms in high-current paths — 8 mΩ on a 100 W [[usb]]-C supply, 10 mΩ in a hot air station and in an audio amplifier's supply rail — up to 1 ohm or more where the current is small and the volt drop can be afforded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-OvGdE5hC1Ro" title="Bakon BK212 Soldering Station - URGH">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-530" title="EEVblog #530 - Electrophoresis Power Supply Teardown">530</a></sup>

Two constraints pull against each other. A larger [[resistance]] gives a larger, easier signal, but the shunt dissipates I²R and inserts a [[voltage-drop]] into the very path being measured. Sizing therefore starts from the dissipation budget: for a 100 A metering application with a 2 W ceiling, rearranging P = I²R gives the shunt resistance as the allowable power divided by the square of the maximum current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> Physical package size follows the same logic — a motor current sense resistor is built in an older, larger style of surface-mount package rather than an 0805 or 1206 precisely because it must tolerate more dissipation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup>

Where a single part cannot carry the power, resistors are paralleled. A 40 A switchmode supply uses twelve 0.1 ohm resistors in parallel as its output shunt, tapped off to the front panel current meter and to further circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-272" title="EEVblog #272 - Manson 9400 40A 3-15V Switchmode PSU Teardown">272</a></sup> A source measure unit parallels four 10 ohm power resistors to make a 2.5 ohm shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> An amplifier board uses two 20 mΩ resistors in parallel to form a 10 mΩ shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup> [[battery]] packs commonly parallel two 10 milliohm resistors to reach 5 milliohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RasOXxxEhCk" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL Investigation - Part 1">•</a></sup>

## Kelvin connection and sense taps

At low shunt values the resistance of the copper reaching the shunt becomes comparable to the shunt itself, so the sense connection must be separated from the current-carrying connection. This is a four-terminal, or Kelvin, arrangement: heavy conductors carry the current in and out, and separate light sense conductors tap the voltage at the resistor body.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> A four-terminal shunt in a 7.5-digit [[multimeter]] takes the main current on heavy heat-shrunk wires and brings one sense wire off separately, the second sense connection being stitched down to the [[ground-plane]] so the measurement is referred to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> The sense terminals are deliberately isolated from the surrounding ground pour on the top layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

The same discipline applies at the board level. On a multimeter's current path, the sense points are taken directly at each end of the 1 ohm resistor so that the resistor alone, and not the trace, forms the sense element; on the 100 ohm range the trace resistance is a negligible fraction of the total and the precaution is unnecessary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Where [[pcb]] copper itself must carry heavy current, the traces are flooded with solder to lower their resistance, though the improvement is modest once the copper is already wide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Lk2KRc6Bm4I" title="Mystery Dumpster Diving Teardown - 2020-07-11">•</a></sup> Power supplies with remote sensing run the sense wires alongside the main drive for both positive and negative outputs, snaking them the length of the chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup>

## High side and low side

A low-side shunt sits between the load and ground and is easy to amplify because the signal sits near ground potential, but it lifts the load's local ground above system ground by the drop across the shunt — around 0.1 V for a 1 amp design — which can make sharing grounds with an ADC awkward and may force a deliberate split between analogue and power grounds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Low-side sensing is nonetheless widespread: a large portable battery system uses it for the main pack, and a USB-C supply places one shunt on the low side to ground alongside the positive-side connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1499" title="EEVblog 1499 - EcoFlow Delta Pro 3.6kWh Portable Battery TEARDOWN!">1499</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup>

High-side sensing avoids disturbing the load's ground reference but requires an amplifier whose common-mode input range extends to the supply rail, typically a small dedicated part in a SOT-23 package sitting next to the shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> For low-side work, a general-purpose [[op-amp]] whose common-mode input range includes ground is adequate for non-critical shunt measurement to a modest accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1436" title="EEVBlog 1436 - The TOP 5 Jellybean OPAMP&#x27;s">1436</a></sup>

## Amplification, gain and ranging

The shunt voltage is small and almost always needs gain. Where a discrete instrumentation stage is built from op amps, the gain-setting resistors must be matched: matching a pair of 10k resistors by hand on a multimeter to within a couple of least significant digits gives far better common-mode rejection than the 1% tolerance they are marked at.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> Gain also sets resolution — raising a microcurrent amplifier's gain from 200 to 500 makes one least significant bit of a 12-bit converter equal exactly one microamp, a more usable figure than 2.5 microamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

Dedicated current sense amplifiers remove much of this work. A zero-drift precision current sense IC such as the LTC6102 is used for MCU power measurement, with two devices fitted so that two current ranges can be covered.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup> Switching regulator controllers frequently integrate the function, bringing the shunt's differential voltage straight to dedicated current sense pins; a single-chip constant-current constant-voltage buck controller works this way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup> [[power-factor]] correction controllers carry both a current sense input and a separate zero current detect input, since the control scheme must know when current reaches zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-273" title="EEVblog #273 - Power Factor Correction with the MC34262">273</a></sup> Some microcontrollers provide the front end directly, with differential ADC input pairs and a programmable gain amplifier, which suits high-side or low-side current monitoring without external amplification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87" title="EEVblog #87 - Let&#x27;s hop on the Electronics Design Merry-Go-Round">87</a></sup> Ranging can also be done by varying amplifier gain under digital control, as in a three-bit R-2R ladder DAC whose output sets the gain of an op amp in a combined current sense and temperature sense circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-501" title="EEVblog #501 - Sinclair C5 Electric Car Teardown &amp; Test Drive">501</a></sup>

Resolution differences between product variants often come from the sensing chain rather than the shunt: an [[electronic-load]]'s A and non-A models, differing by a factor of ten in current resolution, appear to share the same current sense resistor, with the difference lying in the sense network or in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup>

## Magnetic and isolated sensing

Where the current path cannot be broken or must stay isolated, the field around the conductor is sensed instead. A Hall effect current sensor sits over a wide trace and drives an op amp for gain; at 100 mV per amp it is intended for high-current applications and cannot resolve milliamps or microamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-769" title="EEVblog #769 - Mailbag">769</a></sup> A clip-on toroidal probe for an [[oscilloscope]] contains the sensor and its amplifier and delivers a nominal 1 V per amp, effectively turning the scope into a clamp meter for [[waveform]] work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1059" title="EEVblog #1059 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Waveform Measurement">1059</a></sup>

Current transformers are the standard approach for mains metering. Direction and routing matter: if both the active and neutral conductors pass through the same core, their fields cancel and no useful signal results, so a [[transformer]] intended to measure a load's consumption must enclose only one of them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup> Equipment sometimes fits more than one transformer to obtain more than one measurement — one enclosing a single conductor for consumption measurement, feeding circuitry that routes back to the control board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1164" title="EEVblog #1164 - Xbox Engineering Baptism Of Fire">1164</a></sup> Cost reduction shows up as omitted sensing: an EV charger measures current only on the neutral side and omits the core balance [[relay]] found in more complete designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1643" title="EEVblog 1643 - DON&#x27;T BUY this Cheap 7kW 32A EV EVSE Charger">1643</a></sup> Because a transformer measures whatever net current passes through its window, redirecting a second generation source through a consumption transformer changes what the meter reports without touching the production measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-k2_mJtAeaog" title="SNEAKY Enphase Solar Monitoring Modification">•</a></sup>

Fully isolated shunt modules occupy the ground between the two families. A part specified at 0.1% nominal accuracy and 300 kHz [[bandwidth]], costing around 69 dollars, provides a differential output that can feed any sensor system and eliminates the design work associated with remote current sensing — expensive for a million-unit product, cheap for a one-off system build.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1410" title="EEVblog 1410 - Mailbag">1410</a></sup>

## Regulation, limiting and protection

Constant-current operation is built directly on a shunt. With a series pass [[transistor]], the voltage drop across the shunt sense resistor is used to hold the current at a fixed value; when the control circuitry switches modes, the same pass element operates as a [[linear-regulator|linear voltage regulator]], which is why lithium-ion charger ICs are typically linear types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-176" title="EEVblog #176 - Lithium Ion/Polymer Battery Charging Tutorial">176</a></sup> Bench supplies use the same structure, with the output current sense resistor tapping off to an amplifier and thence to the series pass element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup> Older regulator chips include [[current-limit]] sensing internally, working against an external current sense resistor with the pass transistor mounted off-board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup>

The topology of the output stage has consequences. Placing the sense resistor in the output path for constant-current control while also fitting a large output [[capacitance]] is a poor combination: the [[capacitor]] charges quickly on a voltage step, and when the current limit engages the charge stored in it can be dumped into the load in excess of the set limit. Output capacitance should be minimised in a constant-current supply for both reasons.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup>

Short-circuit protection in linear amplifiers uses the same principle without any amplifier at all: output current flows through 1 ohm emitter resistors, and when the voltage sensed across them rises far enough it turns on protection transistors that steal drive away from the pre-drivers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup> Switching designs monitor current for overload and respond by reducing the commanded output to limit transient currents, alongside the protection inherent in the analogue design of the bridge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1170" title="EEVblog #1170 - TRUE Mystery Teardown! (not even Dave knows)">1170</a></sup> High-voltage supplies typically tap a 1 ohm resistor in series with the main switching element for the same purpose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-530" title="EEVblog #530 - Electrophoresis Power Supply Teardown">530</a></sup>

Motor and stepper drives sense current to control it rather than merely to limit it. A stepper driver's current setting is checked by measuring a reference voltage at test points on the board — 0.6 V for one motor type, a different value for another — and an incorrect setting shows up as a stepped pattern in printed output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-274" title="EEVblog #274 - Makerbot Tweaking &amp; First Print">274</a></sup> A pump motor's shunt lets the [[firmware]] monitor how much current the motor is drawing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup>

## Instrument current inputs

In multimeters the [[current-shunt]] is the range element. Typical implementations use 100 ohms, 1 ohm and 10 milliohms for successively higher ranges, and these values set the [[burden-voltage]] the meter inserts into the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> The 10 A input has its own dedicated shunt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup> Input jack construction can itself be part of the sensing scheme: split current jacks are used to detect which terminal a lead has been inserted into and switch ranges automatically.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup> The current sense jack circuit also carries its own protection and input resistor network — 5 megohm input resistors in one design — sitting behind MOVs and PTC devices.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>

Source measure units separate the two measurement paths physically, with a voltage sense channel on one part of the board and a current sense channel on another, each terminating in its own high-end ADC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Beyond the measurement path itself, such instruments carry additional current sensing throughout for self-test purposes rather than for the specified output current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>

## Diagnostic and analytical uses

A shunt plus an oscilloscope is a general-purpose window into a device's behaviour. A resistor in series with the power line, with a scope across it, is the most basic tool for power line analysis; a 10 ohm resistor in series with a battery serves for probing an electronic lock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> Inserting a resistor into a USB [[power-rail]] similarly yields a usable signal — at 100 mV per division a device drawing about 40 milliamps produces a clear trace, and many devices tolerate the added drop well enough to keep working while being profiled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1006" title="EEVblog #1006 - Trezor Bitcoin Hardware Wallet Teardown">1006</a></sup>

Sensing current also serves as simple presence detection: a resistor in a probe's supply line acts as a shunt, and the resulting voltage change indicates that a probe has been plugged in and is drawing current, with different probe types drawing different amounts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup> Automated test fixtures fit a current sense resistor and a small current sense amplifier so that board current becomes one of the measured parameters of the test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-552" title="EEVblog #552 - DFM Automated PCB Panel Testing">552</a></sup>

## Identifying current sensing in hardware

A low-value power resistor with two fine traces leaving it toward a small amplifier is the standard visual signature of a shunt, and the arrangement recurs across power supplies, single-board computer expansion rails, battery packs and monitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-861" title="EEVblog #861 - Rigol DP832 PSU FAIL &amp; REPAIR">861</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-946" title="EEVblog #946 - Apple (Raspberry) Pi Cluster - PART 2">946</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-RasOXxxEhCk" title="Jackery Battery Bank FAIL Investigation - Part 1">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1215" title="EEVblog #1215 - $18,000 4K Monitor Teardown From 2001!">1215</a></sup> Large power resistors on heat sinks alongside pass transistors indicate [[current-measurement]] rather than dissipation duty in an electronic load, since a genuine load would not need such resistors for measuring current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup> Even a fitted zero ohm jumper in a footprint sized for a large power resistor suggests a current sense provision left unpopulated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup> In schematics the same elements appear as a resistor with a tap into the control loop — a [[potentiometer]] forming part of the current sense circuit, for instance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup>
