The 5 volt rail is the nominal supply that powers digital logic in the great majority of electronic equipment built from the TTL era through to the present. In older instruments and computers it is typically the highest-[[current]] rail in the system and often the only one the digital section needs, which makes it both the busiest node on the board and the first thing worth measuring when a machine will not boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> Its conventional tolerance is plus or minus 5%, a window running from 4.75 V to 5.25 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup>

## Tolerance and where to sit inside it

The 5% window is the working definition of a healthy rail: 5.12 V is unremarkable, 4.97 V is fine, and 4.93 V on a running machine is enough to rule the supply out as a first-order suspect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Gbn_51IoJiM" title="Amiga 2000 Teardown &amp; Power Up">•</a></sup> Readings outside the window are not automatically fatal — 4.68 V measured on a working [[analog-oscilloscope|analogue oscilloscope]] is out of spec but not worth agonising over — though they mark the supply as degraded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup> Values that sit inside the window but low, such as 4.79 V on a standby rail, are technically in spec yet suspicious enough to follow up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-378" title="EEVblog #378 - Dumpster Diving Teardown Repair">378</a></sup> The same suspicion applied to a receiver measuring 4.8 V eventually pointed at a specific failed 22 nF [[capacitor]], C405.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-379" title="EEVBlog #379 - Yamaha RX-V557 Receiver Fix">379</a></sup> A reading well above nominal is just as diagnostic: 5.68 V on what should be a regular 5 V supply is clearly not a normal rail, and 6 V where 5 V is expected suggests the internal supply itself has drifted out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-297" title="EEVblog #297 - Canon LANC Bus Reverse Engineering">297</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup>

Because the tolerance is symmetrical but the losses in a system are not, the standard practice on high-current 5 V systems is to trim deliberately to the high side rather than to exactly 5.000 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup> Old boards populated with hundreds of chips would be set at 5.25 V at the regulator, so that circuitry near the [[connector]] sat at the top of the tolerance band and circuitry at the far end of the board — after the drop across cables, connectors and traces — still landed above 4.75 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-905" title="EEVblog #905 - REPAIR: HP85 Vintage Computer">905</a></sup> Equipment found trimmed to 5.25 V in the field is doing exactly this.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup> Trimming precisely to 5 V, by contrast, spends the entire margin on IR drop.

## Current, and the shape of the supply around it

The 5 V rail's current demand is what distinguishes it physically from the other rails. An Amiga 500 draws about 1 A idle and just over 2 A while reading a floppy, against 0.7 A on the plus 12 V rail and effectively nothing on minus 12 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> A LeCroy 9384C [[oscilloscope]] supply is rated at 14 A on the 5 V rail, and — importantly for load testing — derives its 3.3 V rail from it, so both must be loaded together to verify capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> By contrast a rubidium [[frequency]] standard draws only about 90 mA on 5 V against 0.77 A on its 15 V rail, since the 5 V there serves only control logic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-235" title="EEVblog #235 - Rubidium Frequency Standard">235</a></sup>

That current shows up in construction. In a well-built supply the transistors and power diodes on one side of the [[heat-sink|heatsink]] belong to the main 5 V rail, while the smaller plus 12, minus 12 and minus 5 rails share the other side with less current capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup> On the board, the 5 V connection is given multiple parallel pins or wires — four wires on the highest-current supply of a scope acquisition board — shorted together at the connector and fanned into an internal plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Whole boards may carry nothing but 5 V: a six-layer digital board with one big 5 V digital plane and no other supply avoids the split-plane and routing-layer problems that a modern multi-rail [[fpga|FPGA]] design forces on the layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup> A dedicated [[ground-plane|ground plane]] and a dedicated 5 V plane on a six-layer board, with the remaining layers left for routing, is the classic arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup>

## Regulation

The rail is generated by whatever suits the current: a 78L05 with series [[diode]] protection for a handful of milliamps of instrument logic, a switching regulator such as a TPS54302 rated 3 A where the rail feeds a whole product, or a discrete [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] with a series pass [[transistor]] and an [[output-current|output current]]-sense [[resistor]] in older instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> At the bottom of the scale, some designs do not regulate at all: one vintage portable computer supply used a proper linear regulator for its 12 V rail but produced 5 V from nothing more than a 5 W [[zener-diode|zener diode]], an approach that stands out as poor next to the effort spent elsewhere in the same supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup>

Where the 5 V rail feeds most of the system, its integrity is a single point of failure for the product. When a 5 V regulator supplies the entire logic board, thermal shutdown of that one part resets the whole instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> Where 5 V additionally supports [[usb|USB]] charging and most of the peripheral circuitry, verifying it is the first job of any repair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1672" title="EEVblog 1672 - JBL REPAIR: You&#x27;ll never guess the fault!">1672</a></sup>

## Ripple, noise and bypassing

Manufacturers specify [[ripple]] on the digital 5 V rail, and the specification is meaningful: a Tektronix 2465B allows 150 mV peak to peak, and a unit measuring 350 mV is far enough out to explain misbehaviour by itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> A Raspberry Pi PoE hat produced close to 400 mV peak to peak on its 5 V output at full power, and at very light load the same converter's regulation broke down entirely, creeping up to 5.8 V below a minimum load of roughly 0.1 W.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1122" title="EEVblog #1122 - Raspberry Pi 3 PoE Hat FAIL Investigation">1122</a></sup>

The worst-case demonstration of what bypassing does is a [[crystal-oscillator|crystal oscillator]] driving a 50 ohm load with no [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] at all, where the 5 V rail swings up 2.5 V and down 2.5 V on every transition because there is no local reservoir to supply the gulp of high-frequency current the output stage demands.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Removing the load leaves the rail still poor but without the large transitions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Ordinary bulk decoupling is simply a large electrolytic between [[ground]] and the 5 V rail, frequently present on the board but absent from the published circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-827" title="EEVblog #827 - Mailbag">827</a></sup>

Rail [[noise]] on this scale is not visible at normal vertical settings. Confirming that a marginal 5 V regulator was misbehaving required [[ac-coupling|AC coupling]] and 100 mV per division; on a 5 V scale, and even inside a couple-hundred-millivolt trigger window, nothing could be captured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> The regulator was still regulating at 5 V; only the AC component had changed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> For serious rail measurement a dedicated [[power-rail|power rail]] probe into a 50 ohm input is the appropriate instrument, and while conveniently adjacent ground and 5 V pins are good enough to probe, wiring directly to the point of interest is better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup>

## Diagnosis

Measuring the 5 V rail is the standard opening move on a dead or misbehaving machine, ahead of consulting the [[service-manual|service manual]] for the unlabelled test points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup> A good 5 V rail with lit LEDs and a running self-test establishes that the digital section is alive and shifts suspicion elsewhere — to the [[crt|CRT]], the video output, or another rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-695" title="EEVblog #695 - Mailbag Tektronix 2465 Analog Oscilloscopes">695</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-536" title="EEVblog #536 - HP35670A DSA Repair">536</a></sup> A rail at 1.47 V ends the investigation immediately and points at the [[power-supply|power supply]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup> The inverse case is equally informative: an instrument with plus 5, minus 5 and plus and minus 15 V all present but its 2 V rail dead should still have brought up its main [[processor]], and did not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

A shorted 5 V rail is diagnosed by [[resistance]] rather than [[voltage]], and the technique is to hunt for the lowest reading across the rail while working across the board. On an ArduCopter board a resettable polyswitch [[fuse]] running hot flagged the short; probing gave 0.22 Ω at the chip pins but 0.08 Ω at one particular capacitor, with a suspect tantalum reading 0.11 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> The [[schematic]]'s three tantalums across the 5 V rail were lifted and proved innocent — a textbook case of being [[led]] up the garden path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> Sharp probes matter, since oxide on [[solder]] joints makes low-ohms readings fluctuate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> The same method on a programmer board found the 5 V rail flatly shorted, and a healthy rail afterwards measured 11 Ω in both directions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup> Measuring current instead of resistance also localises the fault: an intercom drawing 197 mA against a 200 mA bench limit showed the 5 V rail itself was fine and the problem lay elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

Hiccup behaviour is its own signature. A 5 V rail captured on a scope starting up to 4.5 V, shutting back down and repeating every second or two indicates the supply is going into [[overcurrent-protection|overcurrent protection]], which in one repair appeared only after capacitors had been changed — the original fault having been large ripple on an otherwise accurate rail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup>

## Interdependence between rails

The 5 V rail rarely fails in isolation, because supplies are often referenced to each other rather than independently. In one instrument every rail's [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] was tied to the 15 V rail, including the 5 V regulator's, so a fault dragging the 15 V rail down dragged the 5 V and the negative 15 V down with it — with all rails dropping by exactly the same percentage, which is itself the tell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The coupling ran the other way too: an overload on the 5 V rail could cause the other channels to lose regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> In a portable PC supply the minus 5 V rail was derived from the minus 12 V output, so a dead minus 12 V guaranteed a dead minus 5 V and both had to be traced back to the same cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> A failure that puts excessive current through a DC-DC converter can take out the 5 V output and, through it, everything downstream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-E1IqcGcZKHE" title="RPi CM4 Compute Module FAILURE Part 2: Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup>

## Use in circuit

Beyond bulk logic supply, the 5 V rail serves as the reference for ordinary interfacing. A pull-up resistor tied to the 5 V rail at one end can be pulled low through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] to emulate a button press without any risk to the target.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-liWWY5cSs4Q" title="Microchip - Atmel Collaboration">•</a></sup> A control panel read a button ladder as an analogue voltage between the 5 V rail and an auxiliary input through a built-in ADC, so a dicky contact produced voltages all over the place and inconsistent decoding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> A digital input specified with a 0.5 V minimum low and a high threshold above 1 V can simply be tied to the 5 V rail when the fixed state is wanted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1246" title="EEVblog #1246 - Dumpster TV to Retro RGB Monitor Conversion">1246</a></sup> Development boards commonly expose both 3.3 V and 5 V for [[breadboard]] work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-888" title="EEVblog #888 - Mailbag">888</a></sup> When powering a prototype's 5 V rail from a bench supply, [[overvoltage-protection|overvoltage protection]] set to around 5.1 V is the guard against the knob being moved or misset to 12 V and destroying expensive hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup>
