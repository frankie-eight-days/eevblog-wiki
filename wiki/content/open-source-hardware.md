Open source hardware is the practice of publishing the complete design information for a physical product — schematics, [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]], [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]], and [[firmware]] — under a licence that lets anyone study, modify, manufacture and sell it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup> It is not the same as free hardware: a physical object still costs money to produce, and what is given away is the information needed to make it, so that anyone who does not want to buy the finished article can build their own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup> The concept long predates the name, but the formal community definition — a document of twelve points — was ratified comparatively recently, and a design only qualifies as open hardware if it meets that definition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup>

## Definition and licensing

The defining constraint is commercial freedom. A design carrying a non-commercial clause is not open source hardware, because the whole point is that a third party may take the original design files and produce and sell a copy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-326" title="EEVblog #326 - Makerbot Replicator Teardown">326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-333" title="EEVblog #333 - Unwritten Rules of Open Source Hardware">333</a></sup> Restricting competitors is a legitimate choice for a designer to make, but a product carrying that restriction should not be described as open hardware, because those who support the formal definition treat the term as load-bearing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup>

Licences in actual use span the Creative Commons family, including CC-BY-SA and Creative Commons more generally, the GPL, and the CERN Open Hardware Licence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-313" title="EEVblog #313 - Bus Pirate LCD Debugging">313</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-294" title="EEVblog #294 - Mailbag">294</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1079" title="EEVblog #1079 - Mailbag">1079</a></sup> Design files are commonly distributed through a public repository rather than a project website.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src--xX2kK-RmQU" title="Hack A Day Prize Interview">•</a></sup>

A separate question is whether a design expressed in a proprietary file format is open at all. A design produced in a closed tool can be read only by someone who has bought that tool, so the position taken by the open EDA projects is that hardware is not truly open unless the file formats — and preferably the tools themselves — are open as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog #121 - gEDA Interview with DJ Delorie">121</a></sup> The practical form of this complaint is the project that publishes only native Eagle [[schematic]] and board files with no PDF rendering, which forces anyone who wants merely to read the schematic to install a specific CAD package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-688" title="EEVblog #688 - How To Rework Solder SMD Chips - BTTF Time Circuits Repair!">688</a></sup> Releasing an easily viewable schematic is treated as a minimum courtesy for an open hardware release.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> The same failure mode appears at the manufacturing end: a repository containing schematic and board files but no Gerbers is only partly usable, since the recipient must re-derive the manufacturing outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup>

Free entry-level tools and the movement are mutually dependent. The open source hardware world is built around free software tools and, frequently, around their limitations — the [[arduino|Arduino]] was designed around the restrictions of the free version of Eagle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-527" title="EEVblog #527 - Altium Entry Level PCB Tool Rant">527</a></sup> A vendor with no free tier is therefore absent from the segment entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-527" title="EEVblog #527 - Altium Entry Level PCB Tool Rant">527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup> Altium's CircuitMaker was released free of layer, size and complexity limits and without a commercial-use clause, on a cloud-and-community model that requires projects to be public — a structure aimed squarely at open source hardware work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-754" title="EEVblog #754 - Altium Circuit Maker First Impressions">754</a></sup>

## Community norms

Beyond the licence there is a body of unwritten convention that predates the movement and traces back to the magazine-project era.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-333" title="EEVblog #333 - Unwritten Rules of Open Source Hardware">333</a></sup> The first is: "Don't clone. Innovate."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-333" title="EEVblog #333 - Unwritten Rules of Open Source Hardware">333</a></sup> Taking someone's design files and producing an exact duplicate is legally permitted — that permission is precisely what the absence of a non-commercial clause buys — but it contributes nothing back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-333" title="EEVblog #333 - Unwritten Rules of Open Source Hardware">333</a></sup> These conventions are norms rather than rules; they are not enforceable and nobody is obliged to follow them, but observing them makes for better standing in the community than going strictly by the legal licence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-336" title="EEVblog #336 - Drive Time Rant">336</a></sup>

Reciprocity is the other half. Contributors who give to the community tend to receive time back in kind — someone who cannot produce a decent enclosure may find another contributor offering to design the CAD files for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup> A derivative work made from an open source design is expected to be re-released as open source hardware in turn.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup>

## The gear logo problem

The community gear logo is applied to products regardless of whether they meet the definition — sometimes innocently, sometimes as a marketing move that trades on the name without the substance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup> The logo is itself open, so its use cannot be policed except socially, and nothing about it tells a prospective user which files are actually available; determining that requires visiting the project's download page and reading the licence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup>

The all-or-nothing structure produces a perverse outcome: a company willing to open part of its hardware risks being shunned for not opening enough, which discourages it from opening anything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup> The proposed remedy is a graded logo — retain the gear, but signal how much of the design is being released, so that a project may open as little or as much as it wants provided it identifies what.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1581" title="EEVblog 1581 - Tesla Roadster now OPEN SOURCE?">1581</a></sup> Partial openness is common in practice: products exist whose software and enclosure files are open while the hardware is not,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> whose design files are promised but withheld until the first production batch has shipped,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup> and whose schematics are scheduled for release only once the model is obsolete, as a right-to-repair measure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>

Claims of openness also fail on inspection. Instruments promoted as open source have turned out to publish an SDK and application source while no hardware schematic exists anywhere on the vendor's site.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #858 - Red Pitaya Extended Cut">2</a></sup> Others advertise open design files that are available only as a preview.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1621" title="EEVblog 1621 - Mailbag: RadiaCode Radiation Monitor + LattePanda Mu Compute Module">1621</a></sup>

## Verifiability and trust

Where a device's security depends on its contents, open hardware changes what a user can check. A hardware wallet whose hardware and firmware are both open can be independently vetted and, in principle, built from scratch, which removes dependence on the supply chain and the associated risk of interception between manufacturer and buyer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1062" title="EEVblog #1062 - Trezor Model T Hardware Wallet Review">1062</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup> The trade-off is concrete rather than ideological: a closed competitor may use a genuine secure element supplied under NDA, with physical and electrical side-channel protection, and so be technically more resistant to a determined attacker, while offering no way to examine the code or the hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1062" title="EEVblog #1062 - Trezor Model T Hardware Wallet Review">1062</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup> Building from open files does not eliminate trust either — it relocates it to whoever assembles the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1374" title="EEVblog #1374 - DIY Trezor Crypto Hardware Wallet - Part 1">1374</a></sup>

The same reasoning applies to silicon documentation. A single-board computer built on a part whose full [[datasheet]] is published is open in a way that one built on a part requiring an NDA is not, whatever the rest of the board's status.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-883" title="EEVblog #883 - Orange Pi One vs Raspberry Pi 2">883</a></sup>

[[reverse-engineering|Reverse engineering]] is central to the ethos: being able to take a thing apart and work out how it functions is the point, so terms of use that forbid decompilation, reverse engineering, or the creation of derivative works are directly contrary to it, as are clauses that prohibit building competing services from a forked platform.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1721" title="EEVblog 1721 - RIP Arduino (New T&amp;C Deep Dive)">1721</a></sup>

## Commercial limits

Open hardware and large-scale manufacturing sit uneasily together. A complex machine gains little from full openness, because nobody is going to fabricate their own sheet metal and build one from the design files — the practical audience for the released files is the tinkerers and hackers who want to modify, repair, or extend a unit they bought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-356" title="EEVblog #356 - Makerbot Replicator 2 Announcement">356</a></sup> As a product line matures, it becomes cheaper and simpler to sell a finished assembly than to keep a kit alive, and the kit tends to die of that arithmetic rather than of any decision of principle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-198" title="Makerbot Venture Capital Funding - EEVblog #198">198</a></sup> Venture funding accelerates the same trajectory, since an eventual sale or float generally ends the open-source character of the company.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-198" title="Makerbot Venture Capital Funding - EEVblog #198">198</a></sup>

Openness nonetheless has commercial value at smaller scale. Publishing a design generates interest, establishes the designer as the authority on that product, and leads to further orders and work — a cheaper route to attention than secrecy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-106" title="EEVblog #106 - Top 5 Tips To Bring Your Product to Market (and why Patents are a BAD idea)">106</a></sup> For an individual, a public body of contributed open hardware work functions as a portfolio that establishes a reputation before an interview begins; the advice for a student is to take one project far enough to be worth pointing at, and specifically to finish it to a polished standard — laid out properly and housed in a real enclosure — rather than leaving it a prototype.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--xX2kK-RmQU" title="Hack A Day Prize Interview">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-KJEmBtvZrPU" title="eevBLAB #78 - Engineering Student Job Advice">•</a></sup>

## Antecedents

The practice is continuous with hobbyist electronics magazines, which published projects with full circuits and one-to-one [[pcb|PCB]] artwork.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1326" title="EEVblog #1326 - How Engineering Minds Think Alike">1326</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src--xX2kK-RmQU" title="Hack A Day Prize Interview">•</a></sup> A reader could photocopy the artwork from the page, or download the layout from the publisher's bulletin board over a dial-up modem, then expose, etch and drill the board at home.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1326" title="EEVblog #1326 - How Engineering Minds Think Alike">1326</a></sup> Designs published this way could be freely rebuilt, improved, republished in a later issue, or sold as kits by third parties, which is functionally what an open source hardware licence permits today.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--xX2kK-RmQU" title="Hack A Day Prize Interview">•</a></sup> Designers sometimes sold pre-programmed logic devices for such projects while still publishing the programmable logic files for free download, so that buying the part was a convenience rather than a requirement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1326" title="EEVblog #1326 - How Engineering Minds Think Alike">1326</a></sup> The kit industry saw a revival alongside the modern movement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--xX2kK-RmQU" title="Hack A Day Prize Interview">•</a></sup>

## Representative projects

Open source hardware appears across the full range of instruments and gadgets. Test and measurement examples include a constant-[[current]] dummy load distributed with Gerbers, BOM and schematic,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-304" title="EEVblog #304 - Mailbag">304</a></sup> a low-jitter 10 MHz reference clock published with full jitter analysis,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-899" title="EEVblog #899 - Extreme Mailbag!">899</a></sup> a [[battery]]-life measurement instrument that models the effect of changing chemistry, [[cutoff-voltage|cutoff voltage]], or a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] for a [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]],<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup> a [[usb|USB]] [[oscilloscope]] released with all design files whose hardware is sound but whose user interface lacks basics such as a 20 MHz [[bandwidth-limit|bandwidth limit]] — an obvious target for community work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup>

Instrumentation aside, the corpus covers an open source hardware [[multimeter]] concept,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> the Ultramicron pocket dosimeter, a data-logging Geiger counter with an internal battery that continues logging while switched off,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-776" title="EEVblog #776 - World&#x27;s Smallest Digital Dosimeter">776</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-775" title="EEVblog #775 - Mailbag">775</a></sup> a 4-axis desktop robotic arm with all hardware designs and control algorithms released,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-586" title="EEVblog #586 - Open Source Hardware uARM 4-Axis Desktop Robotic Arm Kickstarter">586</a></sup> a TTL RISC kit computer shipped with full schematics,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1080" title="EEVblog #1080 - Gigatron TTL RISC Kit Computer Review">1080</a></sup> open-back DSP headphones,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1569" title="EEVblog 1569 - A Very Ploopy Open Source Mailbag">1569</a></sup> the Bus Pirate [[lcd|LCD]] adapter,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-313" title="EEVblog #313 - Bus Pirate LCD Debugging">313</a></sup> an open screen adapter driving a generic LCD from DisplayPort,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-592" title="EEVblog #592 - Mailbag">592</a></sup> and a 3D-printer control board combining GPL and CERN licensed sections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup>

Bitcoin mining hardware is a case where openness drives an entire product ecosystem: an open source [[asic|ASIC]] miner design has been manufactured in many variants by many companies, differing in stands, cooling arrangements and form factor, all derived from the same released files.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0h7lKgIFFXA" title="BitAxe Ultra $80 Bitcoin ASIC Miner REVIEW">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4ANGcEJbrTE" title="$260 NerdQaxe++ Open Source Solo Bitcoin Miner REVIEW">•</a></sup>

A worked example of building open tooling for a closed part is the Padauk three-cent [[microcontroller]], whose vendor programmer costs on the order of a hundred dollars. The device was reverse engineered by the community, and full open source hardware and software now exist for programming and emulating it, including VHDL emulator implementations and an open C compiler toolchain based on SDCC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (1 of 5): 3 Cent Micro - Open Source Programmer">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9gYaB1AGmT8" title="Padauk 3 Cent Micro Programmer &amp; ICE Unboxing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1141" title="EEVblog #1141 - Padauk 3 CENT Micro - Programmer">1141</a></sup>
