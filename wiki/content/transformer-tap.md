A transformer tap is a connection brought out from an intermediate point on a [[transformer]] winding, so that one transformer can supply several different voltages rather than a single fixed one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1301" title="EEVblog #1301 - Arcade Machine Repair">1301</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Taps appear on both sides of the magnetics: primary taps let one design be wired for different mains voltages, while secondary taps let a downstream circuit select the lowest usable input [[voltage]] for the job.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-569" title="EEVblog #569 - Tour of an Analog TV Transmission Facility">569</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> In linear equipment the secondary tap is the single most important thermal design element, because everything above the tap voltage is burned as heat in the pass element.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup>

## Primary taps and mains voltage selection

Multiple primary taps are the standard way of making one product worldwide-compatible: the tapping is changed for the local mains, and only the transformer itself differs between 50 Hz and 60 Hz markets.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-569" title="EEVblog #569 - Tour of an Analog TV Transmission Facility">569</a></sup> Consumer gear often exposes this as a slide-type voltage selector on the case, which is mechanically a three-pin tap selector on the transformer primary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1756" title="EEVblog 1756 - Sharp 1980&#x27;s Boombox Bodge Repair">1756</a></sup> Where no external selector exists, the tap change may be an internal jumper, or may not be provided at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup>

The HP/Agilent E3610A illustrates the alternative approach. The mains wiring runs from the IEC input through the front-panel switch and straight into the transformer with no [[solder]] tabs, no internal jumpers, and no accessible taps, so there is no way to convert the standard unit to 240 V operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup> A separate transformer is used for each market, with the 240 V version sold as an option rather than a tap change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup> Building one tapped transformer instead of two part numbers is the better engineering choice, and its absence here is a design failing rather than a necessity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup>

## Secondary taps in linear power supplies

A [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] drops the difference between its rectified input and its output across the pass [[transistor]], so a single high-voltage secondary would dissipate enormous power at low output settings. Tapped secondaries solve this: the supply switches to a lower tap as the set voltage falls, keeping the headroom — and the dissipation — bounded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> A linear bench supply of any range therefore needs taps as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup>

Tap switching is normally done with relays, audible as a click as the [[output-voltage|output voltage]] crosses a boundary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gqzZHbEfWDU" title="KoradFollowup Charles">•</a></sup> The Rigol DP832 instead switches its secondary taps with triacs and triac drivers, an unusual substitution for the conventional [[relay]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> A toroidal-transformer supply with three relays visible inside can be expected to carry three taps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-TQcV3ftPLgA" title="Matrix PSU Unboxings &amp; Teardown">•</a></sup>

Measured tap structures are modest in number. The Siglent SPD3303X has three taps, switching at roughly 8 V, 16 V and 24 V, and will only change tap with the output enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> The Atten PPS3205T-3S transformer is labelled with 0 V, 7.6 V, 19 V and 34 V windings, each rated 5 A, corresponding to the tap transitions observable from the front panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup>

## Worst-case dissipation and tap boundaries

Because dissipation is set by the gap between the tap voltage and the output, the hottest operating point of a tapped linear supply is just *above* a tap transition, where the supply has selected a higher tap but is delivering only slightly more voltage than the tap below.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> Testing therefore targets those boundaries rather than the extremes of the range. On the Atten, 24 V at 5 A was chosen precisely because it sits near the bottom of the highest tap, giving maximum drop across the pass transistor; dropping to 23 V moved the unit one tap down onto the high side of that tap and reduced the [[heat-sink|heat sink]] load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> The full test point was 24 V at 5 A plus 6 V at 3 A, about 138 W total.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup>

The other worst case is a short applied while the supply is set low, so the pass element drops nearly the whole tap voltage into a near-zero output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> Driving the SPD3303X this way with an [[electronic-load|electronic load]] in constant-[[resistance]] mode at 1 Ω made the supply oscillate between constant voltage and constant [[current]] and hunt across its tap boundaries at the same time, taking the power transistor to 100 °C — within margin, but not a condition to leave running indefinitely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup>

Tap coverage also sets real current limits that specifications may not state. The Atten delivered 5 A at 20 V, where the relay had selected a lower tap, but could not do so at 30 V on the top tap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> Stepping from 5 V to 30 V under a 10 Ω load, a 90 W step, took the supply through two tap changes before it settled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup>

Tap voltage is also a fixed constraint on modification. Extra current might be obtainable from an Agilent 6643A by changing a jumper, but the voltage range is determined by transformer tap ratios, and with no spare or alternative taps on the transformer the supply is tailored to the magnetics fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-667" title="EEVblog #667 - Agilent 6643A Power Supply Binding Post Hack">667</a></sup> Similarly, the thermal problems of the Rigol DP832's [[lm317|LM317]] were not addressed by re-tapping: the transformer and its tap were left unchanged in the revised design, so the voltage reaching the regulator after the [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] stayed as it was.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> Mains that sits high — 248 V is common in some locations, and over 245 V nominal most of the time — pushes that rectified rail higher still.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> Out of the box the DP832 was set to its 230 V tap.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup>

## Auxiliary and multiple-output taps

Beyond the main power path, separate low-current taps commonly feed housekeeping rails so that logic and analogue supplies do not share the main rectifier. The Atten has a dedicated tap for the DAC/ADC board's ±15 V regulators and another for the front-panel logic board, each with its own bridge rectifier, filtering and on-board regulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> The SPD3303X's third channel is fed from its own yellow-wire tap and its own discrete-diode bridge, which does not need to be heat-sunk because that channel's power output is lower.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> An Onkyo receiver derives its negative VP rail from a separate tap measuring 39 V AC on a dedicated transformer tap board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1394" title="EEVblog 1394 - Onkyo Receiver Repair - Part 1">1394</a></sup>

Taps also carry heavy current in non-supply equipment: the JBC CD-2BB [[soldering]] station routes its transformer taps through heavy [[via-stitching|via stitching]] and thick copper to handle the current of a roughly 150 W iron.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup>

## Other tapped configurations

A centre or extra tap is what converts a half-wave arrangement into a full-wave one. A Cockcroft-Walton multiplier can be made full-wave by mirroring and duplicating the ladder and taking the extra tap from the transformer exactly as a conventional linear supply does, which doubles the [[ripple]] [[frequency]] and reduces [[capacitor]] sag.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup>

In inverters, an H-bridge on each side of the transformer tap allows that side to be pulled to [[ground]] or driven high, so either side of the winding can be switched, alternated, or disconnected — the same topology used for motor drives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1620" title="EEVblog 1620 - Deye Solar Hybrid Inverter EXTREME TEARDOWN">1620</a></sup>

Constant-voltage 100 V line audio systems use the same tapping idea on the speaker transformers rather than the source: individual 4-inch speakers tapped for half a watt or one watt allow roughly 300 units to be reticulated onto a single 150 W amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>

## Taps in fault diagnosis

Because taps are the boundary between the mains side and the rectified rails, they are a natural probe point. Measuring AC on a tap distinguishes a dead transformer or open winding from a downstream fault: on one repair the tap was confirmed live once the mains relay was forced on, drawing 42 W, which localised the fault to the [[processor]] failing to recognise the powered-on state rather than to the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-378" title="EEVblog #378 - Dumpster Diving Teardown Repair">378</a></sup> Where a rail shows excess ripple, a failing tap is possible but ranks well below a degraded reservoir capacitor or an interconnect problem in likelihood; a 35-year-old 5300 µF capacitor going bad would produce a large amount of excess ripple, which is the more expected signature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Tap wiring is also useful for orientation when [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]] an unfamiliar board, identifying which rails enter the secondary side and where the current shunts sit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup>

Taps are visible in teardowns as the incoming secondary wiring. An electrophoresis supply's primary tap is a six-wire bundle of three brown and three black wires arranged as three separate pairs, and its 250 V tap measured 258 V RMS with the output ramping — the tap voltage itself fixed and low-[[impedance]], with the rising output being the DC produced by the downstream converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-530" title="EEVblog #530 - Electrophoresis Power Supply Teardown">530</a></sup> Poorly executed tap connections are a workmanship flag in their own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-922" title="EEVblog #922 - Mailbag">922</a></sup>
