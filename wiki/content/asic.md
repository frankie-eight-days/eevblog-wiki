An ASIC — an application-specific integrated circuit — is a chip designed to perform one job for one product or one product family, rather than a general-purpose part bought from a catalogue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> It sits at one end of a spectrum whose other end is the [[fpga|FPGA]], a universal chip that can be configured to implement virtually any digital function the designer can imagine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-496" title="EEVblog #496 - What Is An FPGA?">496</a></sup> The reason to build one is that fixed silicon does in hardware, at full speed and with no [[processor]] involvement, work that a CPU would have to do serially and slowly — and the reason not to is money, because the design effort must be paid for before a single unit ships.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup>

## The economics: NRE and volume

The dominant cost of an ASIC is non-recurring engineering, the up-front design cost that must be absorbed before the per-unit price becomes attractive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> Once that NRE has been paid off, the ASIC becomes relatively cheap — cheaper than buying an ADC from Texas Instruments or Analog Devices and an FPGA from Xilinx and bolting them together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup> That amortisation is what allows capability developed for a flagship product to migrate downward into low-end models at a fraction of the original price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>

The corollary is a volume threshold. Rolling your own ASIC is not justified for a product selling in the hundreds or low thousands of units; a logic analyser family shipping at that scale is served better by an FPGA, which can be made to do anything required without the silicon spin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> Gate arrays occupy the middle [[ground]]: a semi-customised array lets a manufacturer implement custom logic without spinning a full ASIC, which is cheaper and simpler, and is the sensible choice for specialised industrial equipment where price is no object.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-969" title="EEVblog #969 - Vintage $80k NLE Video Editor Teardown">969</a></sup> Sinclair's uncommitted logic array in the ZX81 is the same idea applied to a consumer product, and it is what let the machine shed the large chip count of its predecessor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-872" title="EEVblog #872 - Mailbag">872</a></sup> A four-ASIC processor split across separate devices, plus two more for the display, points at gate-array logic capacity limits rather than a true custom design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup>

There is also a semi-custom path short of a full design. A large enough customer can approach a chip vendor and ask for an existing part to be modified — a feature added, a feature removed to cut power — with the vendor free to sell the variant to others, provided the quantity is there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1229" title="EEVblog #1229 - Mailbag">1229</a></sup>

## Development time and the teams behind it

ASIC programmes run on multi-year cycles. The Keysight MegaZoom 4 chipset took over five years to develop, and was a complete architectural redesign from the ground up rather than an evolution of MegaZoom 3.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032  Part 4 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Matt Holcomb is credited with the MegaZoom 4 ASIC that underpins the 2000 and 3000 X-Series scopes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-150" title="EEVblog #150 - Agilent Easter Egg">150</a></sup> Because the investment is so large, the timing of a successor is deliberately paced to extract full value from it before market shifts force a change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032  Part 4 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Scope manufacturers maintain a standing ASIC team that moves on to the next device while the [[current]] product ships.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

The labour is largely VHDL and Verilog: system architects and HDL engineers who may spend years of a career on one piece of silicon for one application.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WQRdEtqLabE" title="eevBLAB #58 - Engineers Are Underappreciated">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1079" title="EEVblog #1079 - Mailbag">1079</a></sup> In regulated fields the timeline stretches further still — for an implantable device, designing the ASIC is only part of the burden alongside qualifying every single part that goes into the product, with five to eight years from concept to market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1027" title="EEVblog #1027 -  Implantable NeuroStimulator Teardown">1027</a></sup>

## The oscilloscope acquisition ASIC

The clearest demonstration of what an ASIC buys is the digital storage [[oscilloscope]]. In the traditional architecture the ADC feeds an FPGA or custom chip, which funnels data through dual-port or external memory to a CPU, which then updates the display — and the CPU becomes the bottleneck.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup> A full acquisition ASIC breaks that chain by plotting [[waveform|waveforms]] directly onto the [[lcd|LCD]], relegating the CPU to a secondary role handling Ethernet, [[usb|USB]], files and the operating system, and reaching the display only indirectly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup> A GUI controller block inside the same chip handles cursors and graphical overlays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup>

The performance consequence is large. The MegaZoom 4 ASIC delivers one million waveform updates per second straight to the display, a figure that previously required instruments costing ten to twenty thousand dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup> The same silicon supports 5 GSa/s in the higher-[[bandwidth]] models.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup> Rohde & Schwarz's IXP ASIC in the MXO4 is responsible for four and a half million waveform updates per second along with the digital trigger system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup>

Because the processing is in hardware, enabling features costs little or nothing in update rate. Serial decoding on the InfiniiVision runs in the ASIC in real time with no performance penalty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> Math functions likewise execute in direct hardware rather than software, which is why scopes that compute math on the main processor slow down when those functions are switched on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> A scope without acquisition ASIC hardware collapses when digital channels, [[fft|FFT]] or serial decode are enabled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> FFT is a good example of the threshold: a 128 k-point transform demands enough processing that it is typically done in an FPGA or ASIC, and grinds to a halt on an ordinary processor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup>

Hardware acceleration is not unlimited. On the MXO4, turning on peak detect drops the update rate from about 47,000 per second to 60.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> Even a dedicated ASIC will slow drastically once enough extra processing is stacked on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup> Dead time is ultimately an architectural and UI-prioritisation decision as much as a silicon one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup>

## Channel partitioning

Acquisition ASICs are commonly built to serve two channels each, and instruments are populated with one per channel pair.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> This creates an exploitable asymmetry: with only two channels in use, selecting one from each pair — channels one and three, or two and four — preserves full [[sample-rate|sample rate]] and [[memory-depth|memory depth]], whereas two channels sharing a chip force a halving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> The four-channel InfiniiVision 3000 X carries four ASICs, two of them the MegaZoom 4 waveform devices, against one set on the 2000 X board — roughly double the acquisition capability, consistent with the 3000's one million waveforms per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-144" title="EEVblog #144 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">144</a></sup> Tektronix's MDO4000 shows the same six-device, two-channel-group split, and the same halving of update performance past two active channels.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix MDO4000 Oscilloscope Teardown - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> Where a single acquisition chip is spread across all four channels, no combination of channels escapes the penalty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup>

## In-house silicon as a competitive position

Building the acquisition chipset in-house marks the transition from integrator to principal. Agilent's InfiniiVision ASIC was designed in-house and manufactured at the company's own plant in Malaysia.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> Rigol developed its own front-end ASIC and acquisition chip, the Phoenix chipset, first appearing in the MSO5000 and MSO7000 and later carried into the DHO series, where the same front-end ASIC appears in a two-channel instrument at around $299.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1218" title="EEVblog #1218 - 1GHz Oscilloscope Spec Shootout">1218</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> Uni-T likewise moved to its own ASICs for its 7000 series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1625" title="EEVblog 1625 - Electronex: Scope Wars: The Rise of Uni-T">1625</a></sup> Rolling one's own analogue front end is part of the same shift: an 800 MHz flat front end in essentially a single chip is now within reach of the larger manufacturers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup> Tektronix uses a custom acquisition ASIC on its higher-end models while lower-tier instruments use off-the-shelf National Semiconductor converters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> The MDO3000's ADC hybrid, developed in-house, achieves better than 5 GHz input bandwidth, enough to sample RF directly for a 3 GHz spectrum analyser function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

## The cost of fixed function

The flip side of hardware acceleration is inflexibility. An FPGA-based scope can in principle have its acquisition behaviour changed by a [[firmware]] or gateware update, because the fabric is field-reconfigurable; an ASIC-based one cannot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> Fixed silicon can therefore constrain a product's feature set, limiting what a later model can add even when the underlying capability plausibly exists in the chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-383" title="EEVBlog #383 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Review">383</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> A memory-depth ceiling baked into the acquisition ASIC — four megapoints, for instance — persists across product revisions until a new ASIC is developed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> The same conservatism keeps a successful device in service a long time: the MegaZoom 4 was still the basis of shipping instruments roughly thirteen years after its introduction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup>

## ASICs in embedded and consumer products

Outside instrumentation, a single large ASIC frequently constitutes the entire product. A portable keyboard reduces to one big ASIC and little else on a double-sided board, with everything remaining built single-sided down to a price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1727" title="EEVblog 1727 - REPAIR: Casio CT-X700 Keyboard">1727</a></sup> The Amstrad NC100's main LSI runs continuously from a 12.2 MHz ceramic resonator with the LCD controller built in, and everything hooks into it — the Z80 CPU does comparatively little.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-385" title="EEVblog #385 - Amstrad NC100 Notepad Repair">385</a></sup> A budget mobile phone reduces to a main ASIC plus perhaps external memory and an RF chipset on a single board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-514" title="EEVblog #514 - $5 Mobile Phone Teardown - LG800G">514</a></sup> Simple handheld games and calculators are typically one chip-on-board ASIC, occasionally two when a separate LCD controller is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-633" title="EEVblog #633 - Mailbag">633</a></sup> Fibre network terminals show the same pattern of one large ASIC controlling everything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-913" title="EEVblog #913 - Mailbag">913</a></sup>

Manufacturers also respin ASICs for model variants: the HP 41CV integrated five times the register count of the C model into the same custom quad flat pack rather than adding external memory chips.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-582" title="EEVblog #582 - HP41CV Calculator Teardown">582</a></sup> Long-lived meter ASICs stay in production for decades — Fluke was still manufacturing the classic 27 ASIC in 2005.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup> A custom ASIC may sit alongside a standard [[microcontroller]], handling one specific function such as non-contact [[voltage]] detection next to an MSP430.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup>

Some ASICs are DSP arrays. Yamaha's YSS910 devices, marketed as the first 44-bit DSPs, appear ten to a board, each with its own local memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup> Sony rolled its own silicon extensively for video effects processing, where a very specific task justified custom parts with obscure part numbers and no public documentation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-752" title="EEVblog #752 - Original Sony Walkman TPS-L2 Teardown">752</a></sup> Purpose-built devices such as Qualcomm's CSR8670, a [[bluetooth|Bluetooth]] application-specific chip with a DSP core running at around 80 MHz, occupy the same niche from the catalogue side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1150" title="EEVblog #1150 - Electrostatic Speaker Teardown">1150</a></sup> Cryptocurrency mining hardware is the extreme case of quantity justifying custom silicon: 40 ASICs per blade drawing 140 W total, with a Cortex-M3 handling only control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup> Hardware integration in a power meter runs at one megasample per second for both voltage and current, so no transient is missed while the front panel remains responsive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>

## Identifying and diagnosing custom silicon

A large unmarked [[bga|BGA]], a chip-on-board blob, or a house-branded part is not by itself evidence of an ASIC. A branded chip may simply be an off-the-shelf microcontroller ordered in enough quantity to be custom-marked, which is the likely explanation on a low-volume consumer appliance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-330" title="EEVblog #330 - Medela Swing Teardown &amp; Repair">330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> A blobbed die could equally be a commodity ARM part with flash bought in die form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-afErPINq8qc" title="Mini Arcade Game Console Repair OOPSIE!">•</a></sup> Lasered-off markings usually indicate commercial chips being hidden to slow [[reverse-engineering|reverse engineering]] rather than genuine custom silicon.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup> Conversely, a design may contain no custom devices at all — the TRS-80 Model 100 was built entirely from off-the-shelf parts on one double-sided board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup> Supply rails offer a weak clue: a 1.2 V core rail suggests a modern high-density custom device, and 1.8 V core supplies are common on television main ASICs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1145" title="EEVBlog #1145 - Dumpster Diving 4K TV!">1145</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup> Process nodes set a hard floor on operation — a typical ASIC begins to fail below about 0.8 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1383" title="EEVblog 1383 - Human Battery Energy Harvesting BUSTED">1383</a></sup>

For repair, an ASIC is usually the end of the road. Custom silicon is unobtainable and undocumented, so a failure inside it is terminal. A LeCroy 9384C drawing eleven amps against a six-amp expectation, with roughly 0.1 ohm across the 3.3 V rail and no hot spot findable with a $12,000 [[thermal-camera|thermal camera]], turned out to have all the excess dissipated across its four acquisition ASICs at near-uniform temperature — four identically dead devices, and an unrepairable instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Removing one ASIC dropped the current from about ten amps to 8.8, a proportional decrease consistent with the shared-failure theory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup> Thermal imaging is the main diagnostic tool here, with the caveat that shiny surfaces such as BNC connectors reflect heat and produce false hot spots.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup> Baselines matter: an ASIC at 55 °C is unremarkable, while a main chip die at around 100 °C is normal for some designs and requires forced [[airflow]] to stay alive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Where the fault is between a main ASIC and its associated BGA memory, freeze spray may fail to provoke an intermittent even when a dry joint is present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-781" title="EEVblog #781 - Samsung LCD TV Part 2">781</a></sup>
