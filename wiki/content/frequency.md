Frequency is the number of cycles a repeating signal completes in one second, measured in hertz: a light that stays on for half a second and off for half a second completes one cycle per second and is therefore blinking at 1 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> It is the inverse of the time period, and the conversion between the two — t equals one over f — is one of the most fundamental relationships in all of electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> Frequency matters far beyond describing a [[waveform]], because almost every property of a real component and almost every failure mode of a real circuit is frequency-dependent: reactance, [[impedance]], noise, radiated interference and [[measurement-accuracy]] all move as frequency moves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup>

## Period, angular frequency and the time domain

Because period and frequency are reciprocal, the two are interchangeable on the bench. A 1 kHz waveform has a period of essentially exactly 1 ms, and that period must be known before cursor-based phase calculations can be performed on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup> A pulse train at 3.9 Hz can equally be described by its mark and space times in milliseconds, with the instrument deriving the hertz reading from them; lengthening the space lowers the frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1681" title="EEVblog 1681 - Mailbag: Nice DIY CC Load,  Amazing Proto Boards, Ian Johnston">1681</a></sup> Cursor deltas convert directly: a heartbeat interval measured as roughly 980 millihertz is slightly faster than 1 Hz, and so slightly above 60 beats per minute.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup>

In AC analysis frequency usually appears in angular form. Omega is simply shorthand for 2 pi f, in units of radians per second, and carries the frequency component inside every phasor expression.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1469" title="EEVblog 1469 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 2 - Phasors">1469</a></sup> This is why scientific calculators carry degrees, radians and gradients modes at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup>

## Reactance and impedance

Inductive reactance is omega L — 2 pi times the frequency times the [[inductance]] in henries — expressed in ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> It behaves like [[resistance]] in the sense that a larger value impedes [[current]] more, so for a given [[inductor]], rising frequency raises the AC resistance and further impedes current flow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> [[capacitance]] inverts the same relationship: capacitive reactance is one over 2 pi times frequency times capacitance, with frequency in hertz and capacitance in farads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1660" title="EEVblog 1660 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 4: Resistors, Capacitors, Inductors">1660</a></sup> At 1 GHz a one picofarad input capacitance is already a significant load, which is why probe input capacitance dominates high-frequency probing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>

The impedance of a lumped component changes with every hertz of frequency change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> The characteristic impedance of a distributed-element transmission line does not — it is broadly flat with frequency apart from a roll-off at the extreme, and conflating the two terms is an error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup>

## Measurement frequency and component measurement

Because component values are measured by exciting them, the measurement frequency is part of the result. An [[lcr-meter]] displays impedance and phase angle at a chosen frequency, selectable from 100 Hz upwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> Stray capacitance on a solderless [[breadboard]] reads about half a picofarad at 100 Hz, where it is barely measurable and down in the noise, just over 2 pF at 1 kHz, and 2.25 pF at 10 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup> Raising the frequency also buys resolution: a handheld LCR meter offering only 0.1 pF resolution at 120 Hz gains a digit at 1 kHz, reaching 10 fF, and reaches 1 fF resolution at 10 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup>

The opposite rule applies to in-circuit measurement. Large capacitors — the values realistically measurable in circuit — should be measured at the lowest frequency available, 100 Hz or 120 Hz, because raising the frequency actually makes the situation worse for large values, collapsing the signal level until nothing useful remains.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Small values in circuit, such as 100 nF surrounded by active silicon, cannot be measured accurately at any frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> Frequency dependence is also one of the layers of complexity behind ceramic [[capacitor]] behaviour, alongside [[voltage]] dependency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup>

## Measuring frequency

A digital [[oscilloscope]] measures frequency automatically alongside peak-to-peak voltage, average value and rise and fall time, and can hold a stopped waveform for analysis after the signal has been disconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> These live in the horizontal measurement group, together with period, rise and fall time, width and duty cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup>

A [[frequency-counter]] works by gating: the time between a reset pulse and the following clock-disable pulse sets a multiplier applied to the counted value, so a one-second gate yields a direct reading in hertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> Multimeters commonly include a frequency function, in some cases alongside simultaneous voltage and current display.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> A simple AC-coupled [[comparator]] front end with a logic output can extend such measurements to signals of up to 50 V RMS and frequencies up to 80 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-809" title="EEVblog #809 - Mailbag">809</a></sup>

Aliasing is the characteristic frequency-measurement failure. A digital scope without anti-alias protection, fed a 10 MHz signal and run at a slow [[time-base]], will display and even measure an entirely fictitious low frequency such as 1 Hz or 8.16 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-F0HQJIPcDYs" title="Oscilloscope Anti Alias?">•</a></sup> The same behaviour shows up when a scope's own generator is swept to its 5 MHz maximum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> An analog scope in XY mode gives a frequency comparison instead: two 1 kHz sine waves produce a stationary circle, and shifting one source by 1 Hz makes the circle rotate once per second in a direction set by which is higher.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup>

## Frequency as a diagnostic signature

The frequency of an unwanted signal usually identifies its source. A studio monitor amplifier oscillating at 61.7 Hz and 40 V peak to peak is destroying its own driver; after partial repair the same fault reappeared at 350 Hz with a visibly drifting period, showing the oscillation was not resonant but condition-dependent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1072" title="EEVblog #1072 - KRK Rokit 6 Studio Monitor Speaker Repair">1072</a></sup> Piezoelectric singing in a 10 uF multilayer ceramic capacitor is not at a fixed frequency either — the interval between successive peaks visibly varies — but the electrical frequency correlates with the audible sound, which is what identifies the mechanism.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup>

Harmonic relationships confirm identifications. A peak at 36.85 MHz found near a 12.28 MHz crystal is the third harmonic of the fundamental, itself measured at 12.23 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup> A signal on a [[vacuum-fluorescent-display]] controller that a scope refused to read directly became a clean 12 MHz once the input was AC coupled, and that number identified the node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717" title="EEVblog #717 - How To Hack Vacuum Fluorescent Displays">717</a></sup> The beep of an electronic safe lock was captured by spectrum analysis at 4.072 kHz, allowing the buzzer tone to be compared against a suspected duplicate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup>

Mechanical resonance obeys the same logic. Sweeping a shaker table across a mounted [[pcb]] found violent response around 95 to 98 Hz and a second node at about 111 Hz within a couple of hertz, with nothing happening at all at 120 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup>

## High-frequency content and interference

Repetition rate and edge rate are separate things, and the edge is what causes trouble. Digital signals at only 6 MHz still contain high frequency content in their transitions, and this remains true even for a 1 Hz signal — the long ground lead of a probe is an inductor that rings on the edge regardless of how slow the repetition is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> A [[sine-wave]] is the exception, because it contains no fast transition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

Susceptibility follows harmonic content. A [[multimeter]] disturbed by an injected square wave at 5 MHz was reacting to harmonics extending up into the RF region; reducing the fundamental frequency progressively reduced the effect, while at 10 MHz and 10 V peak to peak the disturbance was severe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup> Coupling was loose enough that moving a hand near the leads changed the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup>

Frequency also sets physical dimensions and behaviour. On a satellite phone, the shorter helical [[antenna]] is the receiver and the longer one the transmitter, because the transmit frequency is lower and therefore the wavelength longer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-721" title="EEVblog #721 - Globalstar Satellite Phone Teardown">721</a></sup> In a terminated coaxial run, sweeping upwards brings the reflected signal out of phase with the incident one, with a distinct minimum at the input at about 47 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> At around 1 MHz and 40,000 volts, a handheld Tesla coil's current largely travels across the skin rather than through the body, and it will punch through insulators such as wood over two or three centimetres.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yQ7_A4Cr9ak" title="Physics in the Pub - Electricity">•</a></sup> 5G operates typically at 25 GHz and above against roughly 5 GHz or lower for 4G, but transmission and reception power levels are not correspondingly higher, and microwatt-level energy produces negligible heating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4vHx-UyIM9M" title="eevBLAB #74 - 5G Causes EVERYTHING!">•</a></sup>

Frequency-selective faults point at the analog front end: an [[oscilloscope-channel]] that reproduces a 1 kHz sine wave's amplitude and low frequency shape correctly while mangling its high frequency content has a [[bandwidth]]-limited defect, not a gain defect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> Front-end multilayer ceramic capacitors are also microphonic, coupling both a relatively high-frequency tap and lower-frequency thumps from the bench into the trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - A Shocking Oscilloscope Problem!">983</a></sup>

## Noise and frequency

Op-amp input noise voltage is specified as a density, in nanovolts per root hertz, and plotted against frequency on dual logarithmic axes because the relationship is not linear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> Measured on a spectrum analyser, a bench instrument's [[noise-floor]] came out at about 31 nV per root hertz at 1 kHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup> Low-frequency spans are slow to acquire: a 1 kHz span takes about a quarter of a second per record length before RMS averaging even begins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup>

## Generated and mains frequencies

Mains equipment is normally specified over a range rather than at a point — a vintage instrument rated for 45 to 62 hertz handles both 50 Hz and 60 Hz supplies without issue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-553" title="EEVblog #553 - Mailbag">553</a></sup> A variable frequency converter can synthesise mains from below 50 Hz up to 440 Hz, and its output voltage regulation degrades noticeably as frequency is raised, with the internal [[switching-frequency]] audible from outside the case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-449" title="EEVblog #449 - Absopulse VFC500 Variable Frequency Converter Teardown">449</a></sup> Mains ripple injection signalling uses tones from 167 hertz up to 2 kilohertz, selected on a DIP switch.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1283" title="EEVblog #1283- What is Mains Ripple Injection?">1283</a></sup> An AC voltage standard set to 5.00000 V at 60 Hz sits in the middle of its most accurate range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup> A tuning-fork wristwatch achieves its smooth sweeping second hand from a fork driven by two coils in an oscillating circuit at around 50 hertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-911" title="EEVblog #911 - 2016 Sydney Maker Faire Part 2">911</a></sup>

Where frequency genuinely does not matter, it should not be chased. A 555-based [[led]] dimmer wanders between about 310 Hz and 733 Hz as its duty cycle is adjusted, which is irrelevant because both extremes are far above any frequency at which flicker would be visible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> Duty cycle control from 0.3% to over 99% was achieved despite that drift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup>

## Frequency in pseudoscience

Frequency is a favoured word in unfounded product claims, used without any defined quantity. Wellness marketing has asserted that unprocessed vegetables and fruit carry a beneficial frequency entering the body while canned food registers zero hertz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1290" title="EEVblog #1290 - Hapbee: Anatomy of a Snake Oil Product">1290</a></sup> A supposedly anomalous sphere was presented as emitting strange frequencies of around one to two hertz on unexplained measuring gear, where readings drifted and were selectively accepted or rejected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uI62sAN5JzU" title="The Mysterious Buga Sphere Transmits LF Waves! (Alien Origin?)">•</a></sup> Free-energy crowdfunding campaigns similarly wrap conventional mains figures — 50 to 60 hertz at 110 to 380 volts — around claims of perpetual motion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HfqpuhYiR3o" title="eevBLAB #60 - Kickstarter Free Energy SCAMS!">•</a></sup>
