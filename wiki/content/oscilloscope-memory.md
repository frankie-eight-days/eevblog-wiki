Acquisition memory is the buffer a [[digital-oscilloscope|digital oscilloscope]] fills with samples during a capture, and its depth sets how much time a scope can record without giving up [[sample-rate|sample rate]]. It is one of the fundamental performance requirements of a scope, ranked alongside [[bandwidth]] and sample rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> Depth determines whether a long event can be captured whole and then zoomed into for detail, or whether the scope must throw away timing resolution to cover the same window.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

## Memory depth, sample rate and time base

Sample rate, [[memory-depth|memory depth]] and captured time span are locked together: the record length divided by the sample rate is the time the scope can see. A scope that cannot hold enough points is forced to drop its sample rate as the [[time-base|time base]] is slowed, simply because it does not have the memory to sustain the maximum rate over a longer window; with 400 megapoints available, a scope could hold 5 gigasamples per second all the way out to 1 millisecond per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> The same arithmetic worked in the opposite direction explains headline specifications — 8 gigasamples per second alongside 100 megapoints of memory is deliberate overkill, since roughly five times oversampling is all that is needed, and the full sample rate is available on the base 70 MHz model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup>

Peak figures are almost always single-channel figures. Memory is shared across the acquisition system, so enabling more channels divides it: a scope quoting 1.25 gigasamples per second and 25 megapoints maximum halves both on two channels and drops to 312 on three, with no further penalty for the fourth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> Older low-cost instruments showed the constraint more crudely still — one scope that advertised 1 gigasample per second could only deliver 16k points at that rate, never its full one or two megabytes, while a competitor at the same price held 2 meg of memory at the full gigasample rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup> Display modes can also consume it: turning on digital phosphor mode on a Tektronix TDS540D drastically reduces the memory available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1515" title="EEVblog 1515 - Dumpster Tektronix TDS540D 500MHz Oscilloscope LCD Upgrade">1515</a></sup>

## Memory versus waveform update rate

Deep memory and a fast [[waveform-update-rate|waveform update rate]] pull against each other, and vendors have made the trade in both directions. The Agilent 2000/3000 X series shipped with only 100 kilobytes of memory at a time when low-end scopes were moving to 1 or 2 megabytes, and that choice bought an update rate of 50,000 [[waveform|waveforms]] per second in a $1,200 instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> The trade is not free at slow sweep speeds: a scope slows down at slower time bases because it must physically take the time to fill the memory, so a headline million waveforms per second means nothing when the time base is slow and the whole buffer has to be filled.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Measured on one instrument, the update rate fell to 22.7 kHz at 2 microseconds per division and 1.8 kHz at 50 microseconds per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup>

## Architectural ceilings

Memory depth is often fixed by the acquisition [[asic|ASIC]] rather than by the price of [[ram|RAM]]. The Keysight 3000T is limited to 4 megapoints across every model from 100 MHz upwards because that is the maximum the MegaZoom 4 ASIC supports, and that ceiling is the single major limitation of an otherwise leading instrument — at a time when $300 scopes carried double or triple that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup> Segmented memory and a very fast update rate compensate to a degree, which makes raw depth less critical on that architecture, but it remains a genuine fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup>

## Manual and automatic memory management

Some scopes require the record length to be chosen before the capture is taken. The GW Instek GDS-1000B offers 10,000, 100k and up to 10 megapoints with no automatic memory management, so the setting must be committed to in advance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> Keysight instruments instead allocate memory automatically according to the time base, which makes the amount actually in use at any moment opaque to the operator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup>

## Segmented memory and waveform history

Segmented or record/replay modes spend a large buffer on many short captures rather than one long one, capturing bursts of interest and skipping the dead time between them. On the Rigol DS2000, the waveform replay function grabs the full 7-megapoint working record repeatedly, filling the 56-megapoint total memory with successive snapshots — which is why the scope appears to be using only 7 megapoints at a given time base.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-369" title="EEVblog #369 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Playing Around">369</a></sup> Siglent's history mode behaves the same way: the full 200 meg is genuinely used, but as roughly 1,987 frames of 100,000 points each rather than one continuous record.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1312" title="EEVblog #1312 - Siglent Oscilloscopes CRIPPLING History Mode!">1312</a></sup> The Rigol DS1054Z has the feature buried in the utility menu under the name record, and it lacks the dedicated front-panel segmented memory control of the DS2000 series, but it extends the already deep memory to capture far more data than a conventional acquisition allows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup> The Rigol 2000 series combines huge memory, an analog-style persistence display and a strong segmented memory implementation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> On the Keysight 3000T, segmented memory became standard across the range after previously being a paid option.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-701" title="EEVblog #701 - Keysight 3000T Oscilloscope Review">701</a></sup>

The most usable form of this is automatic history: the scope continuously builds waveform history in the background while free-running and consumes all its memory doing so, so pressing stop leaves a scrollable backlog — 8,500 frames on the R&S MXO4 — without the operator having to have configured anything in advance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HN_eeI12qZw" title="The R&amp;S MXO4 is too powerful for its own good (and Dave&#x27;s sanity)">•</a></sup>

## What deep memory is for on the bench

Long serial protocol captures are the canonical case: decoding a long packet requires the deepest memory available, so that the whole transaction is captured at a sample rate fine enough to resolve individual bit edges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> On a 56-megapoint scope, working at 14 megapoints and escalating to the full 56 when needed is the normal practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-762" title="EEVblog #762 - How Secure Are Electronic Safe Locks?">762</a></sup> Timing measurements improve directly with depth — a phase measurement quantised to 0.08 microsecond steps at 10K points memory gains resolution when the full 10 megapoints is selected and the capture repeated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup>

Deep memory also underpins the zoom-in workflow: set the time base, trigger and maximum memory depth, take a single-shot capture, then zoom into the stored record at leisure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> The asymmetry is that a scope can zoom in but not out — data outside the pre-set window was never acquired.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup> Memory only helps where the acquisition is the limiting factor; a scope with 14 megapoints standard can have an [[fft|FFT]] implementation that remains almost a toy despite excelling in the time domain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-845" title="EEVblog #845 - Oscilloscope FFT Comparison">845</a></sup>

Getting the data off the instrument is a separate bottleneck. A four-channel design sampling at 500 megasamples per second with two bytes per sample produces 4 gigabytes per second, which cannot cross a [[usb|USB]] link, so the samples must be buffered locally — 64 megasamples of storage per channel in one isolated scope design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup>

## Historical depths

Early digital storage scopes were memory-starved to the point of near-uselessness. A digital scope of that era was essentially an analog scope with storage bolted on and a couple of kilobytes of memory behind it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-809" title="EEVblog #809 - Mailbag">809</a></sup> A widely admired scope of that generation held 2.5K of [[sample-memory|sample memory]], which is pathetic by modern standards, and old HP 54600-series digital scopes only a couple of kilobytes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Digital-storage-only models from that generation are worth avoiding on the second-hand market for exactly this reason, whereas the analog scopes of the same period are not similarly compromised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1022" title="EEVblog #1022 - How To Find A $50 Oscilloscope On Ebay - REDUX">1022</a></sup> Digital scopes from the 20-megasample era typically had one or two kilobytes at best.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1716" title="EEVblog 1716 - University Dumpster Diving: Kikusui Oscilloscope">1716</a></sup> Later 1990s instruments improved but not dramatically: a GW Instek GDS 820C carried 125k, and an Agilent InfiniiVision of that vintage around 64K per channel — adequate for high-bandwidth analysis even so.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1536" title="EEVblog 1536 - How to find a $50 Oscilloscope on Ebay (Part 3)">1536</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup> A fully optioned Tektronix TDS540D reached 8 megapoints, which keeps it a genuinely powerful 500 MHz four-channel instrument today.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1185" title="EEVblog #1185 - Dumpster Tek 500MHz TDS540D Scope">1185</a></sup>

## Depths in current instruments

Contemporary depths span several orders of magnitude by price class. Around $25, a pocket instrument may carry 1k of sample memory, a specification that would have been novel 25 years ago.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup> At $80, 50k to 64k is enough for the purposes of such an instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1540" title="EEVblog 1540 - The $80 Zotek ZT-702S Osciloscope Multimeter REVIEW">1540</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup> A $140 tablet scope offers 240k points, which is a fair amount for the price, though its usefulness is undermined when the user interface allows zooming into only about two screens' worth of the captured record and refuses to scroll through the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1317" title="EEVblog #1317 - $140 2CH 100MHz Fnirsi Tablet Oscilloscope Review">1317</a></sup> Mainstream bench scopes run far deeper: 14 megapoints standard on Siglent's SDS1104X-E, 25 megapoints on the Rigol DHO800, 40 megapoints on the OWON XDS3202A, 100 megapoints on the Rigol MSO5000, 250 megapoints on the Siglent 5000X, 400 megapoints on the R&S MXO4, and up to 500 megapoints on the Rigol MSO7000 and HDO4000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1320" title="EEVblog #1320 - Premature Oscilloscope Triggering">1320</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-CMoBGGqojqs" title="Rigol MSO7000 Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1501" title="EEVblog 1501 - Rigol HDO4000 Low Noise 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">1501</a></sup> Depth on its own does not redeem an instrument — a 200 MHz two-channel scope at 1 gigasample per second with 40 megapoints is not worth $1,300 unless its high-resolution converter is the reason for buying it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ByUiOk00K0U" title="OWON 14bit XDS3202A Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impressions">•</a></sup>

Because the display is a rendering of what sits in the acquisition buffer, a stopped scope showing data that predates the [[current]] settings is correct behaviour rather than a fault: the trace should match the memory contents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>
