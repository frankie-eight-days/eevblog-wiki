A multimeter is a hand-held or bench instrument that measures multiple electrical quantities — at minimum [[voltage]], [[current]], and [[resistance]], hence the name "multi".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> It is fundamentally a [[voltage-measurement]] device: even [[current-measurement]] is performed by passing the current through an internal [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] and measuring the resulting [[voltage-drop]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-UJjMt2-k99c" title="What Is An Oscilloscope?">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup> It is the primary first-line diagnostic instrument in electronics, but every measurement it makes disturbs the circuit under test to some degree, and most of the practical knowledge around multimeters concerns understanding and controlling that disturbance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## Architecture

A typical handheld multimeter is built around a dedicated multimeter chipset that integrates the processor, analog front end, and ADC, with an input [[voltage-divider]], multiplexers, and shunt resistors ahead of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Analog multiplexer chips such as the 74HC4051 are extremely common inside multimeters for range and signal switching because they cost only cents each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup> A change of chipset can be recognised externally by a change in range layout, such as AC and DC volts moving onto a single dial position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5v8qX9PrjnA" title="Brymen BM257 vs BM235 Multimeter IR Serial Protocol">•</a></sup> Single-chipset designs are typically bare-bones and lack closed-case software [[calibration]], since the internal processor has no [[eeprom]] to store calibration constants.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

The current-measurement front end typically consists of a shared milliamps/microamps jack and a separate amps jack, each feeding a high rupture capacity (HRC) [[fuse]] and then a shunt resistor referenced to the common terminal — in amps mode a multimeter is essentially a [[current-shunt]] input to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> HRC fuses in quality meters are rated at 1,000 V so they can safely interrupt high-energy faults; some cheap meters use HRC fuses with inadequate voltage ratings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> The voltage and [[resistance-measurement]] circuitry is effectively an entirely separate circuit from the current front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup>

## Counts, digits, resolution, and accuracy

Multimeter displays are specified either in digits or counts, and the two are interchangeable. A 3½-digit meter displays ±1999, where the "half" means the most significant digit can only be 0 or 1; this is a 2,000-count meter, while a 4½-digit meter is a 20,000-count meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup> Marketing terms such as "three and three quarter digits" have no real standard behind them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup> Count and digit figures describe resolution only — they have nothing whatsoever to do with accuracy, and conflating a "10,000 count resolution" with "10,000 count accuracy" is a basic error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup>

The basic DC volts range is the most accurate function on any multimeter — more accurate than resistance, current, or [[capacitance]] — and sometimes the millivolt range is better still.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> A good meter will have ohms accuracy close to its DC volts accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> DC current accuracy is hardest to achieve because it depends on the shunt resistor, and accuracy figures are typically worst on the 10 A range and best on the microamps range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> This is also why low-current measurement instruments feed their output into the multimeter's voltage range rather than its current range: the voltage range is the most accurate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup>

## Burden voltage

Every multimeter measures current by dropping a voltage across an internal shunt resistor, and that drop — the [[burden-voltage]] — is subtracted from the circuit under test. It is the single largest hidden error in current measurement, affecting every multimeter on the market.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> A good meter such as the Fluke 87 exhibits 1.8 mV of burden per milliamp, so measuring 200 mA drops 360 mV — enough to push a 5 V circuit out of specification, let alone a 3.3 V or 1.2 V circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-i069j6S80OU" title="uCurrent">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup> Burden voltage can reach several hundred millivolts to a volt at full-scale reading, and it changes with both the current being measured and the range selected: the microamps range has a much higher internal shunt resistance than the milliamps range, so switching ranges changes the measured current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> Inserting the meter adds the shunt resistance in series with the circuit, so a 1 V source across 100 Ω no longer delivers 10 mA once the shunt is included.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup>

The practical mitigations are: switch to the amps jack, whose much lower shunt resistance minimises circuit disturbance at the cost of resolution (on the order of 0.1 mA on a typical handheld); or measure the voltage across an existing [[series-resistor]] in the circuit instead of breaking into it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-324" title="EEVblog #324 - DC-DC Converter Testing - USB Supply Part 4">324</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Burden voltage can be measured directly by connecting a second multimeter across the current terminals of the first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1604" title="EEVblog 1604 - BEWARE! Multimeter Burden Voltage">1604</a></sup> Dedicated current-to-voltage adapters such as the µCurrent exist specifically to solve this problem, amplifying the drop across a very small shunt (e.g. 10 mΩ) so the meter reads it on its accurate voltage range with minimal circuit impact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog #2 - Burden Voltage, HP Multimeter review">2</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-72" title="EEVblog #72 - Let&#x27;s Design a Product">72</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1318" title="EEVblog #1318 - What&#x27;s State-of-the-Art in µCurrent Opamps?">1318</a></sup> Redesigning the conventional front end to lower burden voltage across both shunt and fuse is achievable at little or no additional cost, but most manufacturers do not bother.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-931" title="EEVblog #931 - Designing A Better Multimeter PART 2">931</a></sup>

## Input impedance and circuit loading

On voltage ranges, a digital multimeter presents approximately 10 MΩ input resistance — around 11.1 MΩ on some designs — on almost every range, AC or DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> In high-[[impedance]] circuits this load forms a divider with the circuit and corrupts the reading: as an order-of-magnitude rule, a meter impedance one order above the circuit impedance causes roughly 10 % error, and two orders roughly 1 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> The effect is easily demonstrated by placing a second 10 MΩ meter in parallel with the first, which visibly drops the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup> The same load explains why a 9 V [[battery]]'s measured terminal voltage sags under even the light load of a meter, an effect of the battery's internal ionic resistance rather than temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup> As a rule of thumb, the meter's impedance should be at least an order of magnitude above what is being measured, or the disturbance becomes too great.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup> Precision meters offer a high-input-impedance mode (gigaohms, effectively infinite), but usually only on the millivolt and lower voltage ranges; handhelds may only offer it on the millivolt range, and some bench meters drop back to 10 MΩ above the 10 V range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-584" title="EEVblog #584 - What Effect Does Your Multimeter Input Impedance Have?">584</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

A related trap is measuring voltage "behind" a rectifying or blocking element: leakage through a [[diode]] combined with the meter's 10 MΩ input resistance can produce a reading of several volts where zero is expected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## Resistance measurement

An ohms range works by sourcing a known current through the unknown resistance and measuring the voltage drop across it, so anything else in parallel with the [[resistor]] — or any external voltage fed into the nodes — invalidates the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> Measuring resistance in-circuit, even on an unpowered board, gives wrong values whenever other components provide parallel paths, and measuring on a live circuit can inject currents and voltages that scramble the reading entirely; manufacturer manuals themselves warn that ohms mode on a live circuit gives incorrect readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> The open-circuit voltage a meter sources on its ohms ranges is unspecified and varies with range and battery: one meter produced 2.8 V on its 60 MΩ range but 7.3 V on its 600 Ω range from a 9 V battery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup>

Some meters offer a low-ohms range that outputs less than 0.6 V, below the threshold needed to turn on semiconductor junctions, allowing in-circuit resistance checks without diode conduction corrupting the result; the feature was very common on 1980s multimeters and appears by default on some modern ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> Very low resistance measurement requires milliohm resolution for tasks such as tracing shorted power rails — a healthy 3.3 V rail will not read 0.1 Ω in both directions with no capacitive charge-up, and chasing such a short needs at least a 4½-digit and preferably a 5½-digit meter or an [[lcr-meter]] with good low-ohms capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

Diode test ranges have their own ceiling: a meter that tests to only 15 V cannot forward-bias high-voltage devices such as a 36 V zener or diodes with forward voltages up to 52 V at rated current, since the range only pushes a couple of milliamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1395" title="EEVblog 1395 - Onkyo Repair SUCCESS">1395</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup>

## Safety and input protection

Measurement-category (CAT) ratings define the energy a meter can safely survive. In a high-energy test at roughly 4 kV — about half the 8 kV used for CAT III qualification — a properly CAT III rated meter survived and failed safe, while non-compliant meters smoked, burned, and appeared ready to catch fire.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-84" title="EEVblog #84 - High Energy Multimeter Destruction">84</a></sup> Well-designed ohms-range protection will survive 240 V mains applied directly to it, though recovery to an accurate reading may take many minutes; surviving 240 V on the ohms range is a standard benchmark of [[input-protection]] quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-252" title="EEVblog #252 - Multimeter Ohms Overload">252</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> Metal-oxide varistors inside the meter clamp overvoltage transients extremely quickly, limiting applied surges to around 1.8 kV even from multi-kilovolt sources.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup>

Fusing practice has subtleties: meters with a 600 mA range marked "fused" are commonly shipped with a 400 mA fuse rather than 600 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1377" title="EEVblog 1377 - The Amazing UNPREDICTABILITY of Fuses!">1377</a></sup> Replacement fuses must be selected against the average time-versus-current curve — HRC fuses broadly share the same ballpark curves, and a substitute should be checked against them, e.g. blowing in about 20 ms at 5 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup> Properly certified meters avoid internal rechargeable batteries with [[usb]] ports, because exposed external metal would fail third-party safety certification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-D2PANd9Hu3U" title="Zotek/Zoyi Test Equipment MAILBAG BONANZA UNBOXING!">•</a></sup>

The low-impedance (LoZ) voltage mode, designed to eliminate ghost voltages, doubles as a safe discharge tool for charged mains filter capacitors, which can hold 300–400 V for tens of minutes where no bleed resistor is fitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1655" title="EEVblog 1655 - How to Discharge Capacitors SAFELY using a Multimeter!">1655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-816" title="EEVblog #816 - Chinon Film Camera Teardown">816</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1388" title="EEVblog 1388 -  Dumpster Diving 4K TV Murphy&#x27;s &quot;Repair&quot;">1388</a></sup> Conversely, the amps mode serves as a fuse-protected shorting probe — useful for pulling reset lines low or discharging nodes — presenting only the fuse resistance plus the shunt, on the order of a couple of ohms, while giving a direct readout of any current flowing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1702" title="EEVblog 1702 : A Most Interesting REPAIR">1702</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Monitor Speaker Repair (FULL)">1322</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

## Additional functions and lesser-known capabilities

- **Capacitance and [[inductance]].** Capacitance mode is common but only accurate to a couple of percent at best. Because multimeters excite the component at a range-dependent test [[frequency]] rather than a fixed one, the capacitance range can be pressed into service to indicate inductance values — a little-known trick that gives usable indications for reasonably sized inductors, provided range-dependent frequency and unit conversions are handled carefully. A proper LCR meter remains far superior, offering selectable test frequencies (100 Hz to 100 kHz) plus ESR, Q, and dissipation factor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup>
- **Temperature.** Temperature-capable meters almost universally use K-type thermocouples, and more advanced models include an internal sensor reporting the meter's own temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1392" title="EEVblog 1392 - No temp probe? No problem!">1392</a></sup>
- **True RMS and AC behaviour.** AC voltage ranges physically AC-couple the input, stripping [[dc-offset]], and are [[bandwidth]]-limited (a handheld may require the signal to be reduced to 1 kHz); "AC+DC" mode is distinct from true RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1223" title="EEVblog #1223 - Oscilloscope Standard Deviation Noise Measurement">1223</a></sup> Budget meters are typically average-responding rather than true RMS.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup>
- **4–20 mA process loops.** Any meter's current range reads a 4–20 mA loop directly, but a few meters add a dedicated mode displaying the loop as 0–100 %.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-DX_f0Cg6pHo" title="How a 4-20mA Process Control Current Loop Rotational Sensor Works">•</a></sup>
- **[[data-logging]] and dual measurement.** Logging meters allow watt-hour and amp-hour [[battery-capacity]] measurements by recording voltage and current to the cut-off point; specialised meters measure voltage and current simultaneously and integrate energy directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-141" title="EEVblog #141 - AA Alkaline Battery Capacity Measurement">141</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

Fundamental limitations remain: a multimeter cannot display time-varying signals, which is the entire reason oscilloscopes exist, and its update rate cannot track rapidly changing voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup> Very short current pulses are [[oscilloscope]] territory; meters respond to them with sluggish, inconsistent decay behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cBNOxg8Px8M" title="Playing with short current pulses on multimeters">•</a></sup> A meter measuring DC voltage is not measuring power, and a regular ohms range cannot measure loudspeaker impedance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 - WAVJA Solar Balls, 60x Better Than A Solar Panel? - Part 1">1633</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - FREE Your Sonos Speaker! (HACK)">1519</a></sup>

## Calibration and traceability

Calibration is fundamentally about measurement confidence and uncertainty — a lab expresses a confidence level (e.g. three sigma) that an instrument is within spec, built on the demonstrated long-term stability of its standards; nothing is ever 100 % certain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> At the top of the chain, 8½-digit meters such as the HP/Agilent/Keysight 3458A serve as transfer standards in calibration facilities, and dedicated boxes of precision resistors such as the Fluke 5450A served as resistance transfer standards for calibrating multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1330" title="EEVblog #1330 - Mailbag with GUEST LABS!">1330</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup>

Formal paid calibration is not always warranted. A practical alternative is to cross-reference two or three meters against each other and against inexpensive voltage-reference chips or off-the-shelf ~$50 reference standards, continually verifying volts, ohms, and amps; a meter known to meet spec from the factory anchors the group, and agreement among several meters gives confidence none has drifted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-374" title="EEVblog #374 - DIY Multimeter Calibration">374</a></sup> Some workflows mandate two meters for any critical measurement in case one is out of spec, with cross-checks typically following the manufacturer's recommended interval of around 12 months.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Certain professional meters add a self-check mode so the instrument can be verified immediately before a critical measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4UY3uI6tl1E" title="eevBLAB 121 - NEW Fluke 87VI Multimeter! MAYBE?">•</a></sup> Out-of-the-box spread across a batch of identical meters is small enough that batch comparison against a reference is meaningful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup> Instruments should also be respected outside their specified environment: consumer meters run at 2 °C — well outside spec — will drift from their reference readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-101" title="EEVblog #101 - Hacking your own Peltier LAB Thermal Chamber">101</a></sup>

## Bench practice and selection

Anyone serious about electronics needs at least two multimeters: voltage and current frequently must be measured simultaneously, and two meters can be compared against each other to detect drift.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26" title="EEVblog #26 - Multimeter Tutorial - Counts, Accuracy, Resolution &amp; Calibration">26</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> Dave Jones's standing bench rule is that a good lab needs four multimeters, because characterising a [[power-supply]] or converter requires input voltage, input current, output voltage, and output current at the same time; multiple meters also allow hands-off monitoring of several rails at once, which matters for safety around high voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-67" title="EEVblog #67 - Hacking the Princeton Tec EOS LED Headlamp with a Cree XPG">67</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup>

On selection: as a rough rule, over about $100 buys a decently engineered, safety-compliant meter, while under $50 is largely poor-quality territory; sticking to established brands — Fluke, Keysight/Agilent, Gossen Metrawatt, Brymen, Extech, Amprobe, BK Precision, Hioki, Yokogawa — is the safe path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> For electronics work specifically, a microamps range is essential — milliamps alone is insufficient — along with volts, ohms, capacitance, continuity, and reasonable DC current accuracy.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup> Electrical-trade meters differ from electronics meters in lacking microamp ranges and low capacitance ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> Price and capability decouple sharply at the low end: a $20-class meter outperformed the $42 Fluke 101, which omits current measurement entirely, while a $100-class Brymen offers independent UL listing and CAT IV rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1574" title="EEVblog 1574 - REVIEW: Is the $42 Fluke 101 Multimeter Any Good?">1574</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> At the bench end, second-hand 6½-digit meters can be found from roughly $200–500, and the 6½-digit class reveals effects — like battery ionic resistance under tiny loads — invisible to lesser instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1184" title="EEVblog #1184 - Ebay $200 6.5 Digit Meter Search">1184</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-515" title="EEVblog #515 - Battery Ionic Resistance Investigation">515</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1680" title="EEVblog 1680 - Free Energy from the Earth&#x27;s Magnetic Field?">1680</a></sup> Metrology-grade handhelds have reached 7½ digits (Prema 6047, Keithley DMM7510), and Gossen produced what is believed to be the only 6½-digit handheld.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1680" title="EEVblog 1680 - Free Energy from the Earth&#x27;s Magnetic Field?">1680</a></sup>
