A ceramic capacitor stores charge between plates separated by a ceramic dielectric, and it is by a wide margin the most common [[capacitor]] type in use, applied to almost every job a small capacitor can do.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 2of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Ceramics and impedance)">33</a></sup> Ceramic dielectrics reach relative permittivities of several thousand, far above the values available from plastic film, which is what allows large [[capacitance]] in a very small package.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup> Steady advances in ceramic materials have progressively displaced other technologies, to the point that the migration away from tantalum and electrolytic parts is close to complete for small and mid-range values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 2of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Ceramics and impedance)">33</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup>

## Classes and dielectrics

The EIA divides ceramic capacitors into two basic classes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 2of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Ceramics and impedance)">33</a></sup> Class 1 covers the NP0 and C0G types and a few more obscure variants; their defining property is that their capacitance does not change with temperature, and once a part has been measured it stays where it was measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 2of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Ceramics and impedance)">33</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> Class 1 parts are confined to low values, typically 1 nF and below.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup>

Class 2 dielectrics — X7R, Y5V and similar — buy their much larger capacitance at the cost of stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> Modern class 2 parts reach hundreds of nanofarads, microfarads, and even hundreds of microfarads in small surface-mount packages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> A Y5V part may carry an initial tolerance of −20% / +80% before any operating condition is applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup>

## Voltage and temperature dependency

Class 2 ceramics lose capacitance as DC bias is applied across them, and the effect is large enough to invalidate simple design assumptions. A 10 µF 16 V Y5V part measuring 8.65 µF on an [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] behaved as 7.8 µF in an RC charging test at 1 V, and its charging characteristic changed shape entirely when the applied [[voltage]] was raised to 10 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> X7R parts are markedly better behaved than Y5V, but still not stable, and the dependency varies between different case sizes within the same dielectric family and the same manufacturer's range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> The practical consequence is that class 2 ceramics are unsuitable for RC timing, where the capacitor value directly sets the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-626" title="EEVblog #626 - Ceramic Capacitor Voltage Dependency">626</a></sup> Precision integrators use plastic film types rather than ordinary ceramics for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup> Where a design does specify ceramics for accuracy-sensitive analog work, the usual discipline is to require C0G or NP0 dielectric and a voltage rating far in excess of the working voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-354" title="EEVblog #354 - CityRail PA Amp Teardown">354</a></sup>

## Impedance and bypassing

A ceramic capacitor's [[impedance]] against [[frequency]] has a characteristic V shape: falling capacitively, reaching a minimum at self-resonance, then rising again as parasitic [[inductance]] dominates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> Value and package set where that minimum sits. A 10 µF 1206 part resonates around 1 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> A 10 nF part in an 0805 package is above 150 Ω at 100 kHz and resonates up near 40–50 MHz, while a larger value in the same package sits above 15 Ω at 100 kHz with resonance around 5–6 MHz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> The broad conclusion is that a large value is good at low frequencies and useless at high ones, and vice versa.

Because no single part covers the whole band, the traditional practice was to parallel several values — a 100 nF with a 1 µF, or a 10 nF with a 1 µF — so that their resonant dips overlap into a broad low-impedance region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup> Modern high-capacitance ceramics reduce the need for that stacking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup> Package geometry, not just capacitance, determines how wide the useful window is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wjMIsM4sDw8" title="Unboxing WOW! The coolest bit of kit in my lab.">•</a></sup>

In practice a ceramic bypass placed close to the load is dramatically more effective than the same part placed further away, and the improvement is measurable as reduced high-frequency [[ripple]] as the capacitor is physically moved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> A 0.1 µF ceramic and a 0.47 µF film capacitor perform about equally well in the same position.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> Density of bypassing tracks how hard a device is working: on one instrument mainboard a secondary [[asic|ASIC]] carried a set of heavy-duty ceramic bypass capacitors while the main processing ASIC was served by ordinary 100 nF parts distributed around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup>

## Regulator output capacitance

Low-dropout regulators depend on [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]] for loop stability, and ceramics are the usual choice. A typical LDO specifies a minimum of 2.2 µF ceramic on the output, and is then guaranteed stable across its load range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> Removing the output capacitance entirely makes such a regulator misbehave immediately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> The specified minimum has margin in it — 0.47 µF was still stable at about a quarter of an amp — but designing below the recommended value is poor practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> For an unspecified jellybean LDO, roughly 1 µF of ceramic across the output is the normal starting point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

Output capacitance also sets load transient response. With 2.2 µF of ceramic, a 200 mA load step produced about 50 mV of output deviation; raising the value to 10 µF reduced the excursion but lengthened the recovery.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup> More output capacitance generally improves transient response, but it is not free: a very large bulk capacitance on a supply output creates its own stability problems.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-222" title="EEVblog #222 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 2">222</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup>

Ceramics also appear as small local additions in signal circuits, where they are not always benign. Adding 0.1 µF ceramic bypasses from each supply rail to a [[virtual-ground|virtual ground]] can cure an op-amp [[oscillation]] caused by a capacitive load, but it also places a capacitive load on the amplifier output through the [[series-resistor|series resistor]], and is not a guaranteed fix.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup> Bypassing the divider on the input side instead — a 0.1 µF ceramic across one of the 200 kΩ divider resistors — is the technically better way to solve the same problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup>

## Failure by short circuit

The dominant failure mode of a [[multilayer-ceramic-capacitor|multilayer ceramic capacitor]] is a short, not an open.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1077" title="EEVblog #1077 - Dumpster PC Repair">1077</a></sup> A capacitor that fails open rarely ruins the day; one that fails short usually does, because ceramics are typically fitted directly across power rails, and a low-impedance supply can dump enormous [[current]] into the fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> The result is not merely a blown [[fuse]]: the part can catch fire and vaporise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> One documented case was a large ceramic across a 0–50 V bench supply output that burned away completely, leaving carbonised board underneath — most likely a part rated at only 25 V, or a defective one, since it failed only when the output was wound up towards full scale.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup> A mains alarm panel caught fire from a shorted ceramic, and a commercial bench [[power-supply|power supply]] likewise, the latter fixed by relocating the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1505" title="EEVblog 1505 - 120W Home Phantom Power? Audit Time!">1505</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1265" title="EEVblog #1265 - $53 360W Lab Bench PSU!">1265</a></sup> Ceramics failing short are also a routine cause of blown fuses in consumer equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1077" title="EEVblog #1077 - Dumpster PC Repair">1077</a></sup>

Three mitigations are used. Derating: for a 0–50 V output, the correct search filter is multilayer ceramics rated above 50 V — 63 V, 80 V, 100 V, or another preferred value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup> Redundancy: two capacitors in series, so that if one breaks down the other still stands off the voltage and the circuit merely loses half its capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1035" title="EEVblog #1035 - Flaming DIY Power Supply!">1035</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> Construction: parts with soft terminations, or dielectrics and terminations designed to fail open or to resist cracking, are available, are more expensive, and are used routinely in automotive work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> Instrument manufacturers who fit ceramic output capacitors on precision supplies generally use the properly terminated types for exactly this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1174" title="EEVblog #1174 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz PSU Teardowns">1174</a></sup> Series redundancy is rarely done in practice, but it is the available answer where reliability matters most.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1710" title="EEVblog 1710 - Mailbag: Tennismatic, Breadboards, Books &amp; Boost Converters">1710</a></sup>

## Mechanical fragility and piezoelectric behaviour

Ceramic capacitors crack very easily — a chip capacitor can be levered off a board with a small flat-bladed screwdriver simply by cracking it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1089" title="EEVblog #1089 - XTAL Oscillator Teardown">1089</a></sup> The same brittleness produces micro-cracks in service, which are the usual precursor to a short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1293" title="EEVblog #1293 - New Rohde and Schwarz NGP800 800W PSU">1293</a></sup> The mechanism is not always obvious: in a high-current [[switch-mode-power-supply|switching converter]], the physical vibration of the ceramic capacitors at the [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]] travelled through the rigid [[pcb|PCB]] and set up out-of-plane mechanical resonances that cracked nearby MEMS oscillators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1504" title="EEVblog 1504 - The COOL thing you MISSED at Tesla AI Day 2022">1504</a></sup> Vibration is also why space-qualified assemblies favour ceramics but pay close attention to how tall components stand off the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup>

Class 2 ceramics are piezoelectric, so mechanical shock generates a voltage. Tapping an [[oscilloscope-probe|oscilloscope probe]] or its compensation network produces a visible response on the screen, traceable to the ceramic compensation capacitors inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-162" title="EEVblog #162 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect on an Oscilloscope">162</a></sup> The effect is exploited deliberately in some transducers: hydrophone elements are effectively two-plate ceramic capacitors that bend under sound pressure, and are known in the industry as benders.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1722" title="EEVblog 1722 - Manufacturing Hardware War STORY">1722</a></sup>

## High-voltage and isolation applications

High-voltage ceramics are common where the alternative is bulky. Old equipment used 3 kV disc ceramics with insulating sleeving over the leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1169" title="EEVblog #1169 - TI 1972 Computer Interfacing">1169</a></sup> A high-voltage differential probe carries 6.8 pF 1 kV ceramics for frequency compensation, straddling the [[isolation-slot|isolation slot]] with the rest of the high-voltage input network, some of them heat-shrunk — plausibly for explosion containment, as is done with input MOVs in multimeters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> Scope-meter front ends likewise bridge isolation slots with ceramic capacitors to couple signals across a barrier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-430" title="EEVblog #430 - Fluke 91 Scopemeter Teardown">430</a></sup> Where a specific capacitance is needed at high voltage and only ceramics offer the required stability, the parts are stacked in series for voltage and in parallel for value; one source-measure unit did this across a ±245 V network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>

Voltage rating alone does not make a part safe across a safety barrier. A counterfeit [[usb|USB]] charger used an ordinary 1 kV 470 pF ceramic between [[transformer]] primary and secondary where a Y-class safety-rated capacitor is required for type approval — inadequate, though still better than the competing unit that had no capacitor there at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-388" title="EEVblog #388 - Fake Apple USB Charger Teardown">388</a></sup>

## Packages, values and displacement of other types

Surface-mount ceramics are made in the standard chip sizes — 1206, 0805, 0603, 0402 and smaller — with 0805 a convenient general-purpose size for hand work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> Through-hole disc ceramics remain in older designs and in kit-building, where their lack of polarity is one of their conveniences.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup>

The available capacitance has grown enormously. Where 1 µF or 10 µF was once an extremely large part and electrolytics were mandatory above that, a 100 µF ceramic is now unremarkable, though such parts remain expensive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup> That progression is what has eroded the case for tantalum, which is costly, uses a scarce material, and in older or cheaper forms is prone to failing violently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 2of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Ceramics and impedance)">33</a></sup> Some manufacturers apply a blanket rule against tantalums; one 4-channel [[oscilloscope]] design contained exactly one, a 470 µF part where ceramic options on that board could not reach the value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-148" title="EEVblog #148 - Agilent 3000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Teardown">148</a></sup> Very high value ceramic surface-mount parts are even used as small energy stores in energy-harvesting designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup>

Ceramics substitute directly for electrolytics in [[ac-coupling|AC coupling]], high-frequency bypassing and general filtering, and there is no reason to prefer an electrolytic in those roles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-827" title="EEVblog #827 - Mailbag">827</a></sup> They are the natural choice in charge-pump voltage doublers and inverters, where the parts already present in an SMD design — 0.47 µF or 1 µF ceramics — do the job that would otherwise call for larger and more expensive electrolytics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> They also appear across the individual diodes of bridge rectifiers, four 1206 parts in one studio-monitor supply, to suppress high-frequency switching transients from the diodes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1156" title="EEVblog #1156 - Focal Professional CMS40 Studio Monitor Teardown">1156</a></sup> Adding 100 nF of ceramic across a switching supply output brought its high-frequency output excursions down to roughly 200 mV peak to peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

## In fault-finding

Because the failure mode is a short, a ceramic capacitor is a standing suspect whenever a board shows a dead rail or a blown fuse — especially on boards carrying no electrolytics at all, where nothing else is a likely candidate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1145" title="EEVBlog #1145 - Dumpster Diving 4K TV!">1145</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup> The difficulty is that a rail may carry many of them: one repair turned up seven further ceramics on a 3.3 V rail after the first suspects were removed, at which point unsoldering candidates one at a time gives way to injecting current from a high-current supply to find the short by other means.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup> Ceramics are not always guilty — a shorted electrolytic near a [[heat-sink|heatsink]] is often more likely — and the odds should govern the order of investigation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> Where a suspect ceramic has only surface contamination, the dielectric elements are internal to the body, so cleaning the outside and refitting the part is reasonable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1564" title="EEVblog 1564 - Agilent/Keysight U1273AX Multimeter Repair">1564</a></sup>
