The range switch is the rotary selector on the front of a [[multimeter]] that chooses the measurement function and range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> In almost all modern handheld meters it is not a self-contained component but a mechanism formed from the instrument itself: wiper contacts carried on a rotating carrier, bearing directly onto gold- or nickel-plated pads etched into the main [[pcb|PCB]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> Because it sits in the signal path between the input jacks and the measurement front end, it is simultaneously the meter's principal user interface, one of its highest-wear mechanical parts, and a component that must survive high-[[voltage]] overload.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup>

## Construction

The switch is a multi-gang assembly: a single rotation moves many contacts at once across a large area, in some cases spanning around two inches of PCB surface, with contact pressure varying across that span.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Bh3gcBr0DKs" title="How to do Lifecycle Testing">•</a></sup> Contacts are commonly arranged in concentric rings on both the top and bottom sides of the board, so a single position can make several connections simultaneously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup> Better implementations use dual-wipe contacts — two wiping fingers per pad, often on two layers — which tolerates debris and wear far better than a single point of contact.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1097" title="EEVblog #1097 - Mailbag">1097</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-991" title="EEVblog #991 - Mailbag">991</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dv0B_WqL7w4" title="UniT 210E Current Clamp Teardown + Brymen Comparison">•</a></sup>

Manufacturers reuse one physical switch body across an entire model line, populating only the contacts a given model's set of ranges and functions requires; unused slots are visible in the moulding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup> On a bench instrument the same arrangement appears with a shaft running back from the front panel to a switch assembly on the main board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

Detenting is what separates a good switch from a poor one, and it is done in the plastics rather than electrically. Typical schemes are small sprung nubs riding into indents in a moulded ring,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup> leaf springs either side engaging large notches,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-173" title="EEVblog #173 - Gossen Metrahit Energy Multimeter Teardown">173</a></sup> or a ball-bearing mechanism, which is what the best-feeling analog meters use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-634" title="EEVblog #634 - Analog Multimeter Teardowns">634</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-46" title="EEVblog #46 - Gossen Metrawatt Xtra Multimeter Review">46</a></sup> A spongy, indistinct action is a design failure in the plastic geometry, not a materials problem — the angles on the plastics and the plastic springs simply have not been worked out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup> The characteristic complaint about a bad switch is that there is no tactile confirmation of position at all: "There's no positive indent."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup>

Two structural variants dominate. In the Fluke arrangement the click mechanism lives in the top of the case, and the knob carries pressure contacts down onto the PCB, with the whole stack held by a clip-and-ring retention arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup> The alternative, used by Brymen among others, puts the click mechanism inside a plastic moulding on the PCB itself and relies on the enclosure screws to maintain pressure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> The Fluke pattern came first and has been very widely copied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup> Within Fluke's own range the details differ: the 17B uses a two-leaf dual-contact spring arrangement with grease on gold-plated pads, while the 87V uses a four-leaf terminal system and adds a metal post in the centre.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup> The 87V switch design proved stable enough to survive multiple PCB revisions with the contacts unchanged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-185" title="EEVblog #185 - Fluke 87V Multimeter GSM Fix!">185</a></sup>

Plating is a real cost lever. Gold over nickel is the desirable finish and is specified on better meters as a deliberate expense;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Iwy8UVVQNkA" title="Sanwa PM300 Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> a nickel-only finish, distinguishable by its silvery cast under the right light, appears even on otherwise well-built meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> Cheaper meters use plain brass contacts, which is tolerable at their price if the switch at least provides multiple contacts per range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-91" title="EEVblog #91 - $50 Multimeter Shootout - Extech EX330, Amprobe AM220, Elenco, Vichy VC99, GS Pro-50">91</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup>

## Role in the measurement circuit

In a multimeter [[schematic]] the range switch is drawn split across the whole diagram, with its physical PCB contacts appearing wherever they are needed rather than as a single symbol.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> Its function is to reconfigure the analog front end. On a [[current]] range, closing a pair of contacts shorts out one [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] so that a smaller one — 1 ohm, for example — becomes the active shunt, converting the measured current into a voltage the ADC can read.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup> The same mechanism allows ranges to be redefined at design time: routing a 5,000 µA range into a 1 ohm shunt instead of a larger one yields 5 mV instead of 500 mV, at which point the range is simply relabelled 5 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-929" title="EEVblog #929 - Designing A Better Multimeter">929</a></sup> Selecting temperature routes the input into the instrumentation amplifier with cold-junction compensation applied.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

The switch also carries functions beyond ranging. On some meters it is the power switch, so that a single dirty contact prevents the instrument from turning on at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup> Where a design replaces mechanical selection with relays and pushbuttons, the switch position that used to do the job directly is replaced by an audible [[relay]] changeover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup> Meters that omit the rotary switch entirely in favour of front-panel buttons lose a genuine advantage: a rotary switch has an unambiguous off position that can be seen and felt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

Very early and Soviet-era designs took the opposite approach, dispensing with switched ranges in favour of a separate banana jack for every range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-930" title="EEVBlog #930 - Mailbag">930</a></sup> Others accreted the switch to the point that it dominates the instrument, with the wiring bodged across the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-482" title="EEVblog #482 - Retro Iskra Multimeter Teardown">482</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1471" title="EEVblog 1471 - Mailbag">1471</a></sup>

## Overload and failure

The range switch is one of the weakest points in a meter's [[input-protection|input protection]]. On the microamp and milliamp ranges the current does not flow through the high-[[current-shunt|current shunt]] but through the range switch itself, so a gross overload applied to the low-current jack can destroy the switch as well as the [[fuse]] — the PCB traces forming the contacts are simply not able to carry it if the design has not accounted for the case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup>

High-voltage overload attacks the switch differently. Clearances between adjacent range contacts on the PCB can be small enough to break down, and arcing across a range switch has been demonstrated at around 5 kV.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-832" title="EEVblog #832 - Keysight U1282A Multimeter Teardown">832</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup> In overload testing where peaks of roughly 3,000 volts were reached, the arcing was expected in the range contacts rather than in the front-end components.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1016" title="EEVblog #1016 - Crude Multimeter High Voltage Overload Testing">1016</a></sup> A field failure on the [[121gw|121GW]] took the form of a track-to-track breakdown between range switch traces with the switch in the volts position, on a front end comprising a nominal 1.5 k PTC, a 1 k high-voltage protection [[resistor]], and two 575 V MOVs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-YPUJipe8Loo" title="121GW Meter 1210V Test">•</a></sup>

Mechanical failure is usually intermittent rather than absolute, and presents as a fault that comes and goes with handling — a wandering reading, a mode that changes on its own, a meter that will not start.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-777" title="EEVblog #777 - Keithley 177 Microvolt DMM Repair">777</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d5CxW3VBoZU" title="BM235 Firmware Quirk">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup> Tolerance stack-up is a plausible cause: on the 121GW, boards produced slightly under nominal thickness reduced contact pressure and produced range switch problems that required a fix in production.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup> A switch removed from its case will also wobble badly, because the case is part of the mechanism and provides the containment the design assumes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qmcSEJehqZM" title="Energizer Battery Leak Fluke 3000 Multimeter &quot;Repair&quot;">•</a></sup>

## Endurance

Range switches are frequently compared unfavourably with rotary encoders on cycle count, which misreads the problem: a multimeter switch is a large multi-gang assembly with contacts sweeping over a wide area under varying pressure, not a small sealed detent. "They're vastly different beasts."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Bh3gcBr0DKs" title="How to do Lifecycle Testing">•</a></sup> For context, several hundred [[rotary-encoder|rotary encoder]] types are rated below 100,000 cycles, and Brymen are reported to specify their meters to 25,000 — so 50,000 cycles is a substantial figure for a range switch, not a poor one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Bh3gcBr0DKs" title="How to do Lifecycle Testing">•</a></sup>

Endurance testing can be automated with a stepper-driven jig turning a 3D-printed knob adapter, counting a full sweep from one off position to the other and back as a single rotation, with a motor release to reposition the shaft without driving past the end stops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QSmiMlWEpy0" title="121GW Range Switch Cycling Test Jig">•</a></sup> Such a rig ran a 121GW-family switch through 50,000 cycles overnight, after which the contacts still functioned, with only a trace of contact intermittency and a 10 k [[resistance]] standard still reading correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QSmiMlWEpy0" title="121GW Range Switch Cycling Test Jig">•</a></sup> A later run reached 51,000 cycles with visible wear concentrated on the outer contact ring, and [[contact-resistance|contact resistance]] measured at a steady 0.9 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup> A significant practical limitation is that the board itself tolerates only so many removals and reinsertions for inspection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup>

Doing the test rigorously is considerably harder than spinning a knob. Every one of the multiple contacts on every range must be measured individually, which means cutting traces so that nothing is measured in parallel; the jig must itself be qualified to represent a human finger; the meter should be rotated through different orientations and moved during cycling; and materials analysis of the contacts should accompany the electrical measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Bh3gcBr0DKs" title="How to do Lifecycle Testing">•</a></sup>

Mechanism changes can be assessed on the same rig. Adding a 0.5 mm shim to a 121GW switch preserved the indents but made the action noticeably less clicky, altering the character of the feel rather than degrading it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u2-ot2vWLxI" title="121GW Range Switch Cycle Testing">•</a></sup>

## Ergonomics

A meter is used either on its tilt bail or flat on the bench, and the ability to change range one-handed without the instrument sliding across the desk is an underrated and frequently failed test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Rubber holsters and moulded rubber dimples on the base solve it; smooth plastic bottoms do not, and some meters can be spun bodily by the knob.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> A very stiff switch passes the test almost by accident, because the pressure needed to turn it also holds the meter down, but that stiffness is itself a defect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> At the opposite extreme, a switch light enough to change position under an incidental twist of the case — jumping from ohms to volts — is a serious fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1083" title="EEVblog #1083 - Pocket Multimeter Shootout">1083</a></sup>

Physical placement matters for survival as well as use. A recessed knob is better protected than a raised one, which offers easier access at the cost of sticking out further,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> and a switch left proud of the holster is exposed to a direct impact if the meter lands face down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> One holster design allows the meter to be stored back-to-front specifically to shield the switch, though the knob can still protrude far enough to take the impact anyway.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup>

Audible feedback on switching, fitted to several meters, is consistently treated as a defect rather than a feature: it renders an otherwise good instrument unsuitable for a quiet office or lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Sealed meters fare surprisingly well against contamination — a switch packed with grit still powered up and operated after the meter was run over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-151" title="EEVblog #151 - Extech Multimeter Canyon Trip">151</a></sup>

## Servicing

On many designs the range switch must be removed before the main PCB can be extracted, since the contacts are on the board underside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9u8TEJdAVdQ" title="Returned Faulty BM235 Switch Investigation">•</a></sup> Reassembly carries the risk of refitting the knob at the wrong index, which silently mislabels every position; well-executed designs are keyed so that this cannot happen, for instance by a notch on the switch that lines up with a matching notch in the case to guarantee contact alignment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1447" title="EEVblog 1447 - EXPENSIVE Fluke vs CHEAPER Brymen - Teardown">1447</a></sup> Where no such keying exists, the position must be noted before disassembly and the switch returned to a known setting such as off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1576" title="EEVblog 1576 - Tektronix DMM916 Multimeter Teardown &amp; (Easy) Repair">1576</a></sup> Grease is normal on the contact pads of good switches and should not be mistaken for melted or damaged plastic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-344" title="EEVblog #344 - Fluke 17B Multimeter Teardown">344</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup>
