An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) turns a continuous [[voltage]] into a numeric code that a [[processor]] can act on, and it is the boundary at which every measuring instrument stops being an analog circuit and becomes a computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup> Its resolution and its input range jointly set the smallest change an instrument can resolve, so the ADC — together with the [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] feeding it — is usually the component that defines what a meter, scope or data logger is actually capable of.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qmcSEJehqZM" title="Energizer Battery Leak Fluke 3000 Multimeter &quot;Repair&quot;">•</a></sup> Once a signal has been converted, essentially anything can be done to it in software, which is why so much circuitry that used to be analog has migrated to the digital side of the converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup>

## Resolution, reference and volts per bit

Resolution is set by the bit count and the reference voltage together. A 12-bit converter has 2 to the power of 12, or 4,096, steps; against a 2.5 V reference that works out to roughly 610 microvolts per bit, an awkward number that forces scaling arithmetic on every reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> Choosing a 2.048 V reference instead makes the values come out spot on with no fudging, and the same trick can be pushed further upstream: setting a [[current]]-sense amplifier gain of 500 rather than 200 yields exactly one microamp per bit on a 12-bit converter, which is a nicer number to work with than 2.5 microamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-238" title="EEVblog #238 - Power Supply Design Part 7">238</a></sup> A 1 V per amp current-sense output against a 2.048 V reference gives a usable range of 0 to 2.048 amps at 500 microamps per bit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup>

The bit count needed follows from the required resolution at full scale. Resolving 0.1 watt in a domestic energy meter that must cover well beyond 2,400 watts implies about 24,000 counts, so at least a 15-bit and realistically a 16-bit converter per channel is required; a 12-bit converter is only adequate if sub-watt resolution is abandoned.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> The reverse case is equally common: matching a 10-bit DAC needs only a 10-bit ADC, and paying for 12-bit measurement of a quantity a 10-bit output cannot control is waste.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-259" title="EEVblog #259 - PSU Rev C Schematic - Part 12">259</a></sup>

Resolution is also what the last digits of a reading are made of. When a measured value sits low in the range, the error becomes a greater percentage purely from the ADC count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup> At the other end, a converter can be better than the displayed digits: a bench [[multimeter]] whose main 6½-digit display is static may still be producing more resolution internally, with the processor calculating a floating-point result from the converter output that can be extracted over LAN, GPIB or a CSV dump.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

## Converters inside microcontrollers

Integrated ADCs are cheap, multi-channel and adequate for a great many jobs. Microcontrollers routinely carry 8- to 12-bit converters with a dozen or more multiplexed input channels, used for [[battery-voltage|battery voltage]] during charging, sensor reading, and general housekeeping.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-444" title="EEVblog #444 - Car Lane Guidance Camera Teardown">444</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-595" title="EEVblog #595 - World&#x27;s Worst Shittiest Camcorder: Teardown">595</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup> Built-in converters are, however, generally not as good as dedicated parts, and pushing a [[microcontroller]]'s ADC to 12 bits is the point at which an external converter usually wins — which is why most of them stop at 10 bits, since that is about all they are good for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1413" title="EEVblog 1413 - Oscilloscope Current Probe TEARDOWN + DEMO">1413</a></sup> Even where 12 bits are claimed, the effective number of bits is the question that matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup>

Selecting one is a real constraint problem: price, performance, availability, package type and [[power-consumption|power consumption]] all have to be met at once, and dozens of candidates may need to be evaluated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-302" title="EEVblog #302 - Electronics Beginner Advice">302</a></sup> Peripheral conflicts bite as well — on some parts almost all the ADC pins are multiplexed onto the [[lcd-segment|LCD segment]] drivers, which can rule a device out entirely when three analog channels are needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-248" title="EEVblog #248 - LCD Enabled Microcontroller Selection">248</a></sup> And a converter sharing a die with switching loads is exposed to them: [[pwm|PWM]]-driven display current in the same chip has to be managed so it does not interfere with conversions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

Before integration, an ADC was a separate and expensive chip. The first reprogrammable microcontrollers had no converter at all, and a design without one had to square the analog signal up with a [[comparator]] and feed the microcontroller a digital edge instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-713" title="EEVblog #713 - Voice Recognition - 1980&#x27;s Style">713</a></sup> Where a converter exists, it can replace hardware elsewhere: an entire keypad can be read on a single ADC pin by giving each key a different printed [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] and mapping the resulting voltage in software.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1282" title="EEVblog #1282 - Design Your Own Membrane Keypad! (µSupply Part 20)">1282</a></sup> The same technique reads front-panel key matrices and rotary range switches, and its characteristic failure mode is a dirty contact producing a voltage that wanders and decodes as the wrong key.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1602" title="EEVblog 1602 - Sony Amplifier REPAIR ADVENTURE">1602</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1592" title="EEVblog 1592 - Fluke 287 Multimeter Teardown">1592</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup>

Getting samples out of the converter efficiently is a [[firmware]] problem in its own right. On STM32 parts, setting the WAIT bit in the CFGR1 register stops the converter advancing through its channel sequence until the data register has been read, so a slow CPU or late interrupt no longer loses samples — an easy fix to miss in the user guide.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eUA2TRX1NTc" title="STM32 ARM Development - DMA &amp; ADC Discussion">•</a></sup> Better still is to ignore the ADC interrupts entirely and let DMA move each completed sample into memory without occupying the CPU, using circular mode so the transfer restarts itself; this both minimises latency between channels and removes the per-sample overhead, and the transfer size can be set to 16 bits so buffers need not be padded out to 32.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eUA2TRX1NTc" title="STM32 ARM Development - DMA &amp; ADC Discussion">•</a></sup>

## Driving the converter

An ADC input is not an arbitrary load. A successive-approximation converter cannot be fed from an arbitrarily high [[input-impedance|input impedance]], so voltage buffers ahead of it are not optional decoration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-232" title="EEVblog #232 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 5">232</a></sup> [[datasheet|Datasheet]] limits make this explicit: an analog source [[resistance]] of 500 ohms with 2 microamps maximum input leakage means the source must be low [[impedance]] to keep errors down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-642" title="EEVblog #642 - TI Connected Launchpad">642</a></sup> A peak detector is a case in point — the storage [[capacitor]] must be followed by an op-amp voltage follower presenting very high impedance to the cap and a low-impedance source to the converter, or the act of measuring droops the value being measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> Signal conditioning should also fill the range: an ECG front end scales and level-shifts the signal so that the 10-bit [[dynamic-range|dynamic range]] is used to the full, and a microphone preamp gain is chosen with the following converter's dynamic range in mind.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-611" title="EEVblog #611 - Electret Microphone Design">611</a></sup> Layout deserves the same care, and a good converter datasheet gives layout guidance directly or in an application note.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup>

## Oscilloscope acquisition

In a [[digital-oscilloscope|digital oscilloscope]] the ADC sits immediately behind the front end, fed as a [[differential-pair|differential pair]] from the variable-gain amplifier, with length-matched serpentine tracks carrying the sample clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> Eight bits has long been the norm, 256 levels, which becomes visible as individual quantisation steps as soon as a small signal is expanded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> Ten-bit and then 12-bit converters displaced it, and 14-bit true converters followed; a 14-bit part is not the same thing as a 12-bit converter running in a high-resolution mode that reports up to 18 bits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - A Shocking Oscilloscope Problem!">983</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup> Building a converter is not the same discipline as building digital logic — the process technology is different, and the parts that do it best are sometimes designed in-house rather than bought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1638" title="EEVblog 1638 - NEW Keysight Megazoom V ASIC 1GHz HD3 Oscilloscope!">1638</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-983" title="EEVblog #983 - A Shocking Oscilloscope Problem!">983</a></sup>

Channel count is bought with converters. A scope with two dual-channel ADCs runs 1 GSa/s with two channels on and halves to 500 MSa/s with four; deleting one converter in a cheaper model halves the [[sample-rate|sample rate]] again, and models that share a single ADC between two channels lose rate when both are on but keep it if channels one and three are used instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1042" title="EEVblog #1042 - Siglent&#x27;s $499 SDS1104X-E 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1042</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1355" title="EEVblog #1355 - NEW Siglent SDS1104X-U $399 4CH Oscilloscope Teardown">1355</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1503" title="EEVblog 1503 - Rigol HDO4000 12bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1503</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup> The reverse trick is interleaving: feeding the same analog signal into both halves of a dual converter with a deliberate skew doubles the effective clock rate in single-channel mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> Taken to an extreme, five dual 40 MSa/s converter chips — ten ADCs — staggered at 100 MHz each produce 1 GSa/s, with the parts running well above their marked speed grade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-19" title="EEVblog #19 - Rigol caught with their pants down!">19</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup> Converters at this rate dissipate accordingly; they can be the hottest device on the board, sitting around 74 to 75 °C while the applications processor stays near 55 °C, and 5 GSa/s parts require serious heatsinking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> [[sample-memory|Sample memory]] must also be sized for the word width — a 12-bit ADC needs two bytes per sample, so a given [[memory-depth|memory depth]] costs twice the bytes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup>

## Using the converter's range

Because vertical resolution is finite, an [[oscilloscope]] measurement is only as good as the fraction of the converter's range the [[waveform]] occupies. A signal squeezed into a few divisions wastes converter range and degrades both resolution and the accuracy of automated measurements; using the vernier to bring the waveform just inside full scale gives the measurement more bits to work with and produces a smaller standard deviation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1228" title="EEVblog #1228 - Do Digital Scopes Have REAL Verniers?">1228</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1226" title="EEVblog #1226 - Get Better Accuracy On Your Oscilloscope">1226</a></sup> On scopes with a genuine variable-gain amplifier this is real hardware gain rather than a software trick, and some designs instead apply digital gain inside the ADC chip itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1228" title="EEVblog #1228 - Do Digital Scopes Have REAL Verniers?">1228</a></sup> [[dc-offset|DC offset]] serves the same end from the other direction: shifting a DC-coupled rail down into the converter's window is what lets a [[power-rail|power rail]] probe resolve small [[ripple]] on a large standing voltage, and a 12-bit converter then allows a genuine zoom into that detail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1735" title="EEVblog 1735 - Power Rail Probing &amp; Oscilloscope DC Offset EXPLAINED">1735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup> Overrange is unrecoverable — once the front-end amplifier or ADC buffer clips, expanding the vertical scale afterwards cannot recover data that was never captured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-793" title="EEVblog #793 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1002 Oscilloscope Hands-On">793</a></sup> The full-scale converter range can be found empirically by increasing amplitude until clipping appears, though the result may reflect where the input amplifier clips rather than the converter itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>

Two further effects are easy to misread. The trigger path is a separate analog system from the acquisition path, so a scope can genuinely trigger on an event that never appears on screen because the converter and the trigger circuitry are not seeing the same thing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1320" title="EEVblog #1320 - Premature Oscilloscope Triggering">1320</a></sup> Modern designs remove the discrepancy by triggering in the digital domain on the converter output, which also allows a one-button 50 percent [[trigger-level|trigger level]] anywhere within the capture window.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-685" title="EEVblog #685 - What Is Oscilloscope AC Trigger Coupling?">685</a></sup> Separately, what reaches the converter is the [[bandwidth]]-limited signal, not the original: a 200 MHz [[square-wave|square wave]] into a 200 MHz front end arrives at the ADC with its harmonics rolled off, closer to a [[sine-wave|sine wave]], which is precisely the condition under which sin x over x interpolation is a mathematically valid reconstruction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1213" title="EEVblog #1213 - The Oscilloscope Interpolation Trap!">1213</a></sup>

## Buying resolution with processing

Bits can be manufactured after the fact. High-resolution acquisition mode trades sample rate for vertical resolution, taking an 8-bit converter to nine, ten, twelve or even fourteen effective bits, and it is far more forgiving of artefacts than mathematical averaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> In a spectrum analyser the same principle appears as processing gain: digital down-conversion and discrete Fourier transforms over a narrow [[resolution-bandwidth|resolution bandwidth]] lower the [[noise-floor|noise floor]] by roughly 57 dB, so an 8-bit converter that would ordinarily manage about 50 dB of dynamic range delivers a noise floor near 107 dB.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> This is what makes it economic to bolt a spectrum analyser onto an oscilloscope: the RF front end feeds the converters already present for the analog channels, and the added hardware is a VCO, some attenuation, switching and amplification for a few tens of dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

The all-digital IF follows the same logic. Rather than an analog log amplifier, envelope detector and video filter, the signal after the IF stage is sampled directly with a high-resolution, high-sample-rate converter and everything downstream is done in software inside an [[fpga|FPGA]] or DSP.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup> That converter need not be fast — the mixer and resolution-bandwidth filter deliver something in the order of tens of megahertz — but it must be precise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-391" title="EEVblog #391 - Rigol DSA815 Spectrum Analyser Teardown">391</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> Direct sampling of RF is spreading upward as converters improve, currently reaching single-digit gigahertz, beyond which parts cannot simply be bought and proprietary custom [[asic|ASIC]] converters are developed instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032  Part 4 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> A 300 MHz IF sampled directly into FPGAs is how a deep space communications complex handles its receive chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1547" title="EEVblog 1547 (Part 3) - Tour of the NASA Canberra Deep Space Communications Complex">1547</a></sup>

## Precision instrumentation

Precision measurement uses a different class of converter entirely. In a 6½-digit bench multimeter the ADC is not an off-the-shelf chip at all — fast 8- and 10-bit parts cannot be cascaded to reach that performance — and the converter is instead built from a multi-slope architecture whose integration capacitor and switching sit outside the logic chip.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-427" title="EEVblog #427 - HP 3478A Multimeter Teardown">427</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> Older instruments show the ancestry plainly: a matched pair implementing a 4½-digit dual-slope converter and display driver, or a timer IC operating the conversion against a voltage reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-406" title="EEVblog #406 - Keithley 480 Picoammeter Teardown &amp; Calibration">406</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-482" title="EEVblog #482 - Retro Iskra Multimeter Teardown">482</a></sup> Distinctive external sampling capacitors on an analog board are enough to identify an integrating converter without tracing it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup> A 17-bit converter is what allows a [[soldering]] station to hold temperature accurately without [[calibration]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup>

Integration time is a measurement parameter in these instruments, not a post-processing one. Setting the aperture to one mains cycle — 20 ms at 50 Hz — averages mains-[[frequency]] [[noise]] out inside the converter's own sampling window rather than afterwards, buying accuracy and 50/60 Hz rejection at the cost of measurement speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Calibration belongs to the same group of components: what determines a meter's calibration is the voltage reference, the analog-to-digital converter, and the hybrid resistor divider network, so replacing a microcontroller containing an integrated sigma-delta converter can shift the readings while a [[firmware-update|firmware update]] alone cannot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qmcSEJehqZM" title="Energizer Battery Leak Fluke 3000 Multimeter &quot;Repair&quot;">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0Oo2J66FLZA" title="BM786 Firmware Update Test">•</a></sup>

Because it sits at the end of the input chain, the converter is also what the [[input-protection|input protection]] network exists to defend, and protection topologies vary considerably between manufacturers according to how the ADC and its dividers work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Susceptibility shows up the same way: RF conducted into the input jacks can flood the ADC to the point where the reading is meaningless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

## High channel counts and data acquisition

Where many channels must be digitised at once, the converter defines the system architecture. A towed seismic array ran up to 10,000 simultaneously sampled real-time channels of 24-bit conversion, with converters roughly every 30 m across 10 km of cable, the data carried over an E1-protocol ATM fibre optic network and power distributed at 500 V DC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-40" title="EEVblog #40 - Dilbert and the world of micro managed Engineering">40</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-131" title="EEVblog #131 - Live Show #7">131</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> Multi-channel converter cards for particle physics experiments follow the same pattern, 64 channels to a card with the analog section at the front and the digital interface at the back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-994" title="EEVblog #994 - CERN/Fermilab Particle Accelerator Boards!">994</a></sup> At laboratory scale, general-purpose data acquisition hardware combines converters and DACs on one board, with 16-bit conversion at 100 kSa/s or 24-bit conversion for load cells, RTDs and thermocouples.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup>

Cheaper multi-channel front ends multiplex instead of duplicating. Cell balance voltages in a [[battery]] charger are divided down, passed through an analog multiplexer whose channel the microcontroller selects, and fed to a single converter [[via]] a low-pass filter; an [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]] multiplexes its voltage and current measurements into one ADC and computes the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> A charger can also poll sense lines continuously through the converter to detect when a cell has been plugged in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

[[usb|USB]] and modular instruments have made 14-bit converters commonplace: 14-bit conversion at 100 to 125 MSa/s appears across several such products, with resolution stated bluntly enough to matter — 340 microvolts per bit means that a 100 microvolt per division setting shows nothing at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1552" title="EEVblog 1552 - Digilent Analog Discovery 3">1552</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-858" title="EEVblog #858 - Red Pitaya">858</a></sup> Higher-end units offer selectable resolution from 8 to 16 bits, trading sample rate for bits, up to 1 GSa/s in 8-bit single-channel mode at around half a watt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-521" title="EEVblog #521 - Picoscope 5000 USB Oscilloscope Teardown">521</a></sup> Some combine a 16-bit low-speed converter with an 18-bit high-speed converter and blend the two so a swept measurement crosses the handover without a discontinuity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1624" title="EEVblog 1624 - Electronex: Liquid Instruments Moku Oscilloscope">1624</a></sup> These parts are not cheap, and high-speed converters and DACs alongside an FPGA are a large part of what such hardware costs to build.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1596" title="EEVblog 1596 - NEW Digilent Analog Discovery Pro ADP2230 TEARDOWN">1596</a></sup> Their availability is historically contingent: the volumes generated by the cell phone industry are what made fast converters and FPGAs cheap enough that building a digital scope became a realistic proposition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-207" title="CleverScope USB Oscilloscope - EEVblog #207 (2 of 3)">207</a></sup>

At the limits, converter noise rather than converter bits sets the outcome. A 14-bit front end with two LSBs of channel noise gives 200 mV of noise at an 800 V full scale, and holding that performance across the entire frequency range is the hard part of the design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1119" title="EEVBlog #1119 - Designing a 1kV Isolated Oscilloscope">1119</a></sup>
