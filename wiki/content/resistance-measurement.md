Resistance measurement is the determination of the opposition a component or circuit path presents to direct [[current]], expressed in ohms. It is one of the three basic functions every [[multimeter]] provides alongside [[voltage]] and current, and the ohmmeter function is present even on pocket instruments that omit a current range entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Its practical importance extends well beyond checking a [[resistor]]'s value: [[resistance]] is the primary diagnostic quantity used to locate short circuits, verify continuity of conductors, identify unmarked components, and confirm that a board is safe to work on.

## Principle of operation

An ohmmeter does not measure resistance directly. It forces a known test current through the resistance under test and measures the resulting [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]], deriving the value from Ohm's law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> [[reverse-engineering|Reverse engineering]] of the Brymen BM2257 front end shows this concretely: in ohms mode the instrument must both drive a signal out across the input terminals and read the voltage back from those same terminals, with the drive path routed through the same [[input-protection|input protection]] and clamping network used by the voltage ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup>

Because the test current must flow through the resistance and return to the meter, anything else that injects current into the same node corrupts the result. If external voltages reach the measurement point through a low [[impedance]] path, both the test current and the measured drop are disturbed and the displayed value is wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup>

Handheld meters obtain the test current from the internal [[battery]]. Older analog designs frequently carried two batteries of different voltages for this reason, the higher voltage cell serving the higher resistance ranges while the lower voltage cell supplied the larger currents needed on the low ohms ranges.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup>

## Test lead and contact resistance

The resistance of the test leads is in series with the measurement and is not negligible at low values. Typical leads measure in the region of 0.05 to 0.4 ohms; a set measured 0.29 ohms in one case and 0.37 ohms in another.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1043" title="EEVblog #1043 - Mailbag">1043</a></sup> Any measurement below roughly ten ohms therefore requires the lead resistance be nulled first, using the relative or delta function found on most meters, which is one of that function's principal uses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-133" title="EEVblog #133 - Dodgy Digikey Components">133</a></sup> With the leads shorted and nulled, an IET decade resistance box set to all zeros read 0.361 ohms, essentially the box's own residual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-211" title="EEVblog #211 - IET Decade Resistance Substitution Box">211</a></sup> The same nulling procedure applied at 100 microohm resolution allowed an in-circuit [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]] to be confirmed as a 10 milliohm part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup>

[[contact-resistance|Contact resistance]] at the probe tips is a second, less controllable error source. On a 34461A binning a nominal 1 kilohm part, contact resistance alone spread readings between 999.991 ohms and 1.000008 kilohms, enough to scatter parts across multiple bins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> Probe pressure on a low-value measurement produced readings of 38 milliohms, 97 milliohms, 52 milliohms, 171 milliohms, 620 milliohms and finally 3 ohms as the force was progressively released.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup>

Probe quality is consequently not a cosmetic concern. Very sharp professional probes are required to penetrate oxide and [[flux]] residue on [[solder]] joints; poor probes cause readings to fluctuate across the display when working down in the [[noise]] on the ohms range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> A meter's continuity and ohms performance that appeared mediocre with the supplied probes proved to be a probe problem rather than a meter problem once gold-plated probes were substituted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1095" title="EEVblog #1095 - Is a $38 Multimeter any good? ANENG Q1 Review (4K!)">1095</a></sup>

## In-circuit measurement and probe polarity

Resistance measured on an assembled board is the parallel combination of everything connected between the two probe points, and semiconductor junctions in that network conduct asymmetrically. The standard defence is to take every in-circuit reading twice with the probe polarity reversed: matching values in both directions indicate a passive path, while a difference reveals active circuitry that is turning on for one polarity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup> A node reading 12 megohms one way and 13 megohms the other was accepted as passive on that basis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1663" title="EEVblog 1663 - BM786 Multimeter REPAIR + InEr Error Investigation">1663</a></sup> The technique also confirms a genuine short: 20 ohms to [[ground]] on a suspect pin remained 20 ohms with the probes swapped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1348" title="EEVblog #1348 - World&#x27;s First IBM Compatible PC - The Compaq Portable">1348</a></sup> Conversely, a multimeter's own battery ground measured 3 kilohms in one direction and 33 kilohms in the other, which established that active circuitry sat between the two points rather than a simple resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup>

Comparison against a known-good reference is the complement to polarity reversal, since an isolated in-circuit number rarely means anything on its own. A node reading 0.2 ohms on a faulty board was shown to be a fault only when the same node on a reference board measured 9.4 kilohms in both directions, which in turn revealed a missing resistor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup> Where a board contains repeated identical channels, the channels serve as their own reference: comparing resistance measurements between supposedly identical [[oscilloscope]] input modules exposed two pins on an SOT-23 that were completely shorted on one channel and not on the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

## Measuring live circuits

The ohms function must not be applied to an energised circuit. Manufacturer documentation states plainly that connecting the leads across a [[voltage-source|voltage source]] with the function switch in the [[capacitance]], resistance or [[diode]] position can damage the meter, and that using the ohms range on a live circuit may give incorrect readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> In practice most modern multimeters carry adequate input protection on the ohms range and will survive 240 V mains applied to the terminals without damage, though the reading obtained will be meaningless.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1622" title="EEVblog 1622 - The BIG BEGINNER MISTAKE with Multimeters">1622</a></sup> Not all instruments meet even that bar: a meter with a single mode switch that failed catastrophically on [[mains-voltage|mains voltage]] in ohms mode was judged unfit for a market where users are shown probing 240 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-94" title="EEVblog #94 - Near Death Multimeter Experience">94</a></sup> Where a PTC is fitted in series with the input for protection, it warms and limits during the overload event, after which the meter returns to reading its 10 kilohm reference correctly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uC-q-FjXEsA" title="1980s EEVblog Pocket Multimeter Overload Testing">•</a></sup>

The low-impedance ghost-voltage or auto-check function inverts the problem usefully. Probing filter capacitors expected to be discharged, the meter remained in a voltage-sensing state, found nothing, and automatically switched itself to ohms and displayed megohms, positively confirming the point was safe rather than merely reading zero volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-780" title="EEVblog #780 - Samsung LCD TV Dumpster Dive Teardown">780</a></sup>

## Fault finding by resistance

Locating a short by resistance depends on resolution rather than accuracy. A 6.5 digit bench meter proved highly effective for hunting shorts on wave-soldered boards, where solder bridges between pins and ground planes were common: the absolute ohms figure was irrelevant, but the reading fell steadily as the probe moved closer to the short, and hundreds of shorts were located this way.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717ampROkvc" title="PRO TIP: Using a 6.5 Digit Multimeter for finding Shorts">•</a></sup> The same gradient method applied to a shorted board gave 211 milliohms at one location and 55 milliohms at another, placing the fault nearer the second point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup> On a shorted [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]], decoupling capacitors read 0.22 ohms at the [[connector]] pins, 0.11 ohms at a tantalum, and 0.08 ohms at one particular [[capacitor]], identifying it as the lowest point and therefore the culprit; a rail measuring 0.1 to 0.2 ohms is effectively a dead short.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-230" title="EEVblog #230 - ArduCopter ArduPilot Troubleshooting">230</a></sup> Resistance measurement also reveals open circuits by the same comparison: three pins nominally tied together on a 3.3 V rail measured 0.11 ohms, 0.11 ohms, and open on the third.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-398" title="EEVblog #398 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair">398</a></sup>

Because the technique relies on a fall in reading rather than on a threshold, some instruments render it audible. A tone whose pitch varies with the measured resistance lets a short be tracked by ear while the eyes stay on the board, a feature available as a menu-selectable tone display on the Agilent U1253A and present as early as the 1980s on a Beckman multimeter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-56" title="EEVblog #56 - Agilent U1253A OLED Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">56</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1330" title="EEVblog #1330 - Mailbag with GUEST LABS!">1330</a></sup>

A related instrument-level concern is the continuity beeper threshold, which is set in [[firmware]] and is a compromise. Firmware on the BM786 originally latched the beeper on at around 270 to 280 ohms with hysteresis requiring about 300 ohms to release; revised firmware lowered the switching point to the region of 40 to 90 ohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PSdF0KNsiaY" title="BM786 Continuity Firmware Testing">•</a></sup>

## Low resistance

Milliohm measurement requires both a dedicated low range and lead nulling. Handheld meters providing a 30 ohm range reach 1 milliohm resolution, and bench instruments go further, the Keithley DMM7510 offering a 1 ohm full-scale range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Dedicated four-wire adapters sidestep lead resistance by design, one such device outputting a millivolt per milliohm into a multimeter's voltage range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-413" title="EEVblog #413 - Mailbag">413</a></sup> At 100 microohm resolution a soldered-in shunt resistor could be characterised in circuit as a precision 10 milliohm part, with the reading settling as probe force increased.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup> LCR tweezers occupy a middle ground: one instrument covers 0.1 ohms to 2 megohms at ±0.5% plus two digits with 0.1 ohm resolution, which is coarse against a dedicated milliohm meter reaching 10 microohm resolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup>

## High resistance

Above a few hundred kilohms the test current becomes extremely small and the measurement grows fragile. At hundreds of kilohms and into the megohms the currents flowing in the test cables are minute enough that triboelectric effects from cable movement and general pickup disturb the reading, and a degree of instability is normal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EJ6KCpHc--4" title="Strange Brymen BM869 Resistance Quirk">•</a></sup> Meters also slow markedly at the top of their range; improving the update rate on high resistance readings and the behaviour of ohms [[auto-ranging|autoranging]] was a specific target of a [[121gw|121GW]] firmware revision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-q4gXnpFPFzQ" title="121GW Multimeter 2.00 Firmware">•</a></sup> Charge effects can dominate the settling time outright, with one meter creeping slowly upward toward a 100 kilohm reading over an extended period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1238" title="EEVblog #1238 - Vion: The World&#x27;s Worst Multimeter!">1238</a></sup>

Beyond the roughly 10 to 100 megohm ceiling of ordinary handhelds, specialised instruments take over.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup> Insulation testers apply high voltage to measure into the gigohm region, verifying 10 gigohm and 20 gigohm resistors at 500 V, and take appreciable time to settle when measuring the insulation resistance of long, capacitive cables.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-673" title="EEVblog #673 - Mailbag">673</a></sup> Electrometers extend further still, the Keithley 617 measuring into hundreds of gigohms and a modern femtoammeter claiming resistance measurement to teraohms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1017" title="EEVblog #1017 - Enter The World Of Atto Amps">1017</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup>

Series voltage sources are a common corruption at high values. With about 0.365 V placed in series with a 10 kilohm standard, an ordinary ohms range read 6.5 megohms; a compensated ohms mode on the same instrument returned 10 kilohms while separately reporting the 366 mV offset it had removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup>

## Accuracy and standards

The basic DC volts range is always the most accurate function on a multimeter, better than resistance, current and capacitance, but a good instrument keeps its ohms accuracy reasonably close to it, and that closeness is a reasonable buying criterion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Representative figures span a wide range: 0.2% on ohms against 0.05% on DC volts for a Fluke 28 Series II, 0.2% for a Fluke 37, 0.9% for the electrician-oriented Fluke 117, 0.04% plus one digit for a cheap bench meter, and about 1% for typical pocket meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1393" title="EEVblog 1393 - Retro Fluke 37 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">1393</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-60" title="EEVblog #60 - Fluke 117 Multimeter Review and Teardown">60</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup> Specification can also degrade sharply with range: one instrument claiming a 0.025% class specified 0.3% plus eight counts at best on ohms, half a percent on the 400 kilohm range and a full percent at 1 megohm, in an ohms front end containing no precision thin-film network and relying on trimpots of unknown long-term behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup>

Verification requires a resistance standard whose value and stability are known. A metrology-grade 10 kilohm transfer standard carried a certified value of 9.9999228 kilohms with 0.1 ppm [[calibration]] uncertainty, 0.3 ppm per degree C [[temperature-coefficient|temperature coefficient]] and 1 ppm per year stability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-733" title="EEVblog #733 - Mailbag">733</a></sup> Precision decade resistance boxes at 0.1% serve as convenient working references for handheld-class instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MarjYxiudYE" title="GVDA GD118B Pocket Multimeter Review">•</a></sup>

Range placement matters when interpreting a check. A 10 kilohm standard measured on a 60 kilohm range sits well below full scale, leaving only two decimal places of [[display-resolution|display resolution]] and inflating the apparent percentage spread; the same population of meters measured at 50 kilohms on the same range gave a larger absolute spread of nine least significant digits but a tighter percentage tolerance because the reading sat closer to full scale.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-852" title="EEVblog #852 - Multimeter Mass Turbulence">852</a></sup> Across a batch of instruments checked against a 10 kilohm standard, worst-case readings of 10.002 and 9.999 represent a total spread of three counts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1051" title="EEVblog #1051 - 121GW Multimeter Mass Turbulence">1051</a></sup>

Temperature affects the meter as much as the standard. Holding a resistance standard at constant temperature in a thermal chamber and varying only the [[ambient-temperature|ambient temperature]] of the meter demonstrated that the Keithley DMM7510 ohms range has a negative temperature coefficient large enough to appear clearly in logged data, though the observed drift proved smaller than the 2.5 ppm per degree C [[datasheet]] figure over a 4 degree C change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-835" title="EEVblog #835 - Wekomm Resistance Standard Part 2">835</a></sup>

## Autoranging behaviour

Autoranging on the ohms function is a common source of anomalous behaviour because the meter must select a range before it has a valid reading. A [[bm235|BM235]] exhibited a fault in which the ohms function displayed an error, or held a wildly wrong range after a transition, when autoranging between values; the same measurements were correct when the range was selected manually, isolating the fault to the autoranging logic rather than the measurement path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-0z5dGXlRibA" title="A Very Strange BM235 Fault">•</a></sup> Meters that autorange by [[relay]] produce audible switching as they hunt, and autorange settling time on the ohms function varies widely between instruments, from effectively instantaneous to several seconds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1704" title="EEVblog 1704 - WTF? The ANENG 626 Multimeter REVIEW">1704</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup>

## Applications in identification and teardown

Resistance measurement is routinely used to characterise unknown or inaccessible structures. The elements of a thin-film resistor network can be predicted from geometry, a layout estimated at a couple of hundred ohms from a 50 ohms-per-square sheet resistance measuring 584 ohms, while a serpentine element in the same package measured about 9.9 meg against a companion element of about 6 kilohms, forming a high-ratio divider.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-730" title="EEVblog #730 - Thin Film Resistor Networks">730</a></sup> Membrane and carbon-ink structures are identified the same way: the printed carbon contacts of a remote control keypad measure a couple of hundred ohms, 270 ohms in one instance, which allows a given button to be traced back to its controller pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-505" title="EEVblog #505 - IR Learning Remote Control Hack">505</a></sup> A resistive [[touchscreen]] is verified by the pattern of which pin pairs show a resistance and which show nothing, compared against a known-good panel.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1100" title="EEVblog #1100 - Dumpster Photocopier Repair">1100</a></sup>

The method also exposes things that are not obvious from construction. Measuring through the centre conductor of a times-one [[oscilloscope-probe|oscilloscope probe]] returns about 330 ohms rather than the zero a plain [[coaxial-cable|coaxial cable]] would give, a consequence of how such probes are built.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1266" title="EEVblog #1266 - PSU Probing Screw Up!">1266</a></sup> A cable tester reading 169 milliohms between a connector's shell and ground on both A and B inputs identified a shell-to-ground short in the cable under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup> A failed [[power-supply|power supply]] output that had originally measured in the tens of kilohms measured 47 ohms after the fault, identical in both probe polarities, confirming a genuine low-impedance path across the output rather than a semiconductor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1036" title="EEVblog #1036 - PSU Fire PCB Repair">1036</a></sup>

## Analog instruments

Analog ohmmeters differ procedurally. The ohms function requires a two-step zeroing operation performed by shorting the probes and adjusting a dedicated zero-ohms control, which is separate from the meter's normal zero adjustment, and this must be repeated on each range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup> The scale is non-linear and reversed relative to the voltage scales, and a mirrored scale is provided so that parallax error can be eliminated by aligning the pointer with its reflection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1580" title="EEVblog 1580 - MacGruber Mailbag">1580</a></sup> A FET VOM inverts the arrangement again, placing infinity at full scale where a conventional analog meter places zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-694" title="EEVblog #694 - Mailbag">694</a></sup> Decade resistance substitution boxes place their decades in series, so the highest-value decade dominates the total and a mis-set or mislabelled switch shows up immediately as a grossly wrong reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1586" title="EEVblog 1586 -  Stranger Things Decade Resistor Box + Repair">1586</a></sup>
