An aluminium electrolytic capacitor is a polarised [[capacitor]] built from a rolled sandwich of aluminium foil and dielectric soaked in a liquid [[electrolyte]], sealed inside a metal can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> It is by a wide margin the worst-behaved passive component in common use — lossy, temperature-sensitive, polarity-sensitive, and finite-lived — and simultaneously unavoidable, because nothing else delivers comparable [[capacitance]] for the volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> [[power-supply]] filtering, bulk decoupling, energy storage and low-[[frequency]] [[ac-coupling]] all still depend on it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> It is also the single most common failure point in mass-market electronics: high ESR in electrolytics is probably the number one failure mode in consumer products, and a dead plasma TV, set-top box or DVD player is odds-on a dried-out capacitor in the power supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup>

## Construction and polarity

The interior is a large roll of foil and dielectric that can be unwound by hand from a sacrificed part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> The electrolyte is a liquid, and the can is sealed — which introduces both a seal that can fail and a fluid that can escape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> A cross-shaped score mark pressed into the top of the can is a deliberate weak point: if internal pressure builds, the can bursts there rather than at the seal or the base.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1727" title="EEVblog 1727 - REPAIR: Casio CT-X700 Keyboard">1727</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup> The presence or absence of that vent is the quickest way to tell a wet electrolytic from a solid polymer part on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1288" title="EEVblog #1288 - Synology NAS Dumpster Find! (REPAIR)">1288</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1152" title="EEVblog #1152 - 240V-120V = Magic Smoke!">1152</a></sup>

Polarity is marked on the body, conventionally by a stripe identifying the negative terminal, and on the leads, the longer being positive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup> Tantalums invert the convention and mark the anode instead, which is a standing trap when working across both types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup> Reverse [[voltage]] is tolerated only to roughly one volt; above about a volt to a volt and a half, internal self-heating vaporises the electrolyte, pressure builds, and the part leaks or explodes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-42" title="EEVblog #42 - Exploding Capacitors in High Speed">42</a></sup> A 470 µF 10 V part connected backwards across a 15 V supply capable of 40 A vents violently and boils its electrolyte out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup> The one-volt threshold is used deliberately in design: a single [[diode]] placed across an electrolytic clamps any reverse excursion below the damage point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup> Layout can also protect the part, as when a charger places its main electrolytic filter cap downstream of the reverse-polarity switching [[transistor]] so a backwards input never reaches it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-397" title="EEVblog #397 - Turnigy Accucell 6 Charger Teardown">397</a></sup>

## Wear-out and failure

Electrolytics fail by drying out. Internal ripple [[current]] flowing through the [[equivalent-series-resistance]] heats the part from the inside, external heat sources add to it, and the electrolyte gradually escapes or evaporates, driving ESR upward.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-682" title="EEVblog #682 - Ness D16X Alarm Panel Repair">682</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup> The process is self-accelerating: higher ESR means more internal heating, which dries out more electrolyte, which raises ESR further, until the part bulges, vents and stops working entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup> Life is inversely proportional to operating temperature — the hotter the part runs, the shorter its rated life — and heat is correctly described as the enemy of the electrolytic capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

The visible signatures are a domed top near the vent, a ruptured vent seam, ooze on the can, and corrosion of the surrounding board where the escaped electrolyte has eaten tracks and component leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1073" title="EEVblog #1073 - MORE KRK Rokit Black Gunk Speaker Repair">1073</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> Most failed parts do bulge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup> Not all do, however, and a capacitor with no external sign at all can still be electrically dead, so visual inspection alone is not a test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> A dielectric breakdown mode also exists that leaves no external trace: micro-arcing between adjacent layers of the roll under ordinary operating voltage, which in an audio amplifier presents as intermittent popping from the speaker.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

## Temperature ratings and placement

Ratings of 85 °C and 105 °C are the common grades, with 125 °C parts appearing in better-specified equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1091" title="EEVblog #1091 - LED Studio Light Repair">1091</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-764" title="EEVblog #764 - New Lab Ceiling LED Lighting Installation">764</a></sup> An 85 °C part is generally not good enough where a 105 °C part is warranted, and a [[bill-of-materials]] that stops at 85 °C is a reliable indication a product was built down to a price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1313" title="EEVblog #1313 - Bad Luck Mailbag">1313</a></sup> The rated life on the [[datasheet|data sheet]] is quoted at a stated temperature and is easily thrown away by placement: mounting a capacitor beside a [[heat-sink]], a hot regulator, a power [[resistor]] dissipating its full rating, or between two heat sinks all raise the can temperature and derate its life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-52" title="EEVblog #52 - Panasonic Plasma TV&#x27;s Suck (and a Teardown)">52</a></sup> The thermal design task is not only keeping semiconductor junctions within their data sheet limits but keeping the temperature-sensitive parts nearby — electrolytics above all — inside theirs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> In one bench-supply investigation, filter caps sitting adjacent to a heat sink running near 130 °C reached about 70 °C on the cans with no [[airflow]], and a nominal [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] run that hot leaves no design margin at all; 105 °C-rated parts in that position will still die in short order.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> An enclosure that permits 80 °C internally will shorten capacitor life regardless of the rating printed on the can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

Cold is not a free win either. ESR of a wet electrolytic rises sharply at low temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup>

## Ripple current and paralleling

Every electrolytic has a maximum charge-discharge or ripple current on its data sheet; exceeding it heats the part internally and shortens life.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> This is why the unregulated input-side capacitors in a linear supply fail before the output-side ones — they see far more ripple, work harder, and self-heat more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> Series designed for the job are specified accordingly: the Nippon Chemi-Con KMH range is intended for linear supplies and high ripple current, rated 2,000 hours of continuous operation at 105 °C at full rated ripple current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup>

Placing two or more electrolytics in parallel, rather than one large part, is a reliability technique rather than a packaging accident: the parallel bank shares ripple current, shares the resulting heat, and lowers the combined ESR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> Beyond that, a properly specified capacitor position carries a whole set of requirements — capacitance, voltage, ESR, ripple rating and more — of which several are more easily met by several parts than by one, and the practice is common enough in power supplies to be worth understanding on sight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup>

[[series-connection]] solves the opposite problem. Ten 1,000 µF 200 V parts cannot sit directly across a rail derived from 240 V mains, so they are stacked in pairs to give 500 µF at 400 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-268" title="EEVblog #268 - Xantrex 300V 4A Power Supply Teardown">268</a></sup> A 600 V supply likewise stacks 450 V parts in series, with balancing resistors across each to force equal voltage sharing rather than relying on the capacitors' own internal ESR to balance them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup>

## High-frequency behaviour

An electrolytic makes a poor high-frequency bypass. Its internal parasitic [[inductance]] and ESR mean that placing one directly at a chip's supply pin and ground barely improves the supply noise; it is useful for bulk decoupling and nothing more, with the high-frequency work left to a small film or ceramic part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> [[impedance]] sweeps bear this out: a 47 µF electrolytic self-resonates somewhere around 8 to 9 MHz and rises in impedance above that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-859" title="EEVblog #859 - Bypass / Decoupling Capacitor Tutorial">859</a></sup> Large capacitance on the output of an op-amp is separately troublesome and can provoke oscillation, so a stabilising bulk capacitor often belongs on the input side instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

## Testing

The instrument for testing an electrolytic is an ESR meter, not a capacitance meter, since capacitance can read close to nominal while ESR has risen far enough to stop the circuit working.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> Because the test signal is small, polarity is irrelevant when measuring ESR and good capacitors are not harmed by connecting the leads either way round.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup> Large-value electrolytics are also, conveniently, exactly the class of part that can be measured usefully in circuit — small values cannot be, because their reactance at the test frequency is too high — and an in-circuit reading is good enough for most diagnosis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup>

Typical numbers from bench practice: a healthy 1,000 µF 10 V secondary-side filter cap measured about 0.01 Ω at 100 kHz against a rough chart figure of 0.1 Ω; a 470 µF 35 V part reading 0.15 Ω against an expected 0.1 Ω is marginal but ambiguous; ordinary, non-low-ESR 50 V 47 µF parts sit near 0.28 Ω, and readings climbing into whole ohms are cause for concern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> A part measuring 25 Ω at 100 kHz in circuit is unambiguously dead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup> Capacitance failures can be equally dramatic — a nominal 220 µF reading 3 µF, or a capacitor so far gone that an [[lcr-meter]] in auto mode will not identify it as a capacitor at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup> Side-by-side comparison of identical parts on the same rail is a useful discriminator, though two parts that measure the same are more informative than one part that measures oddly, since intermittent faults do not always show up as gross outliers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup>

## Replacement and selection

When replacing, capacitance and voltage may be increased freely but never reduced: go up in voltage without concern, up in capacitance without concern, and never down in voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-365" title="EEVblog #365 - ESR Meter Bad Cap Monitor Repair">365</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup> For switch-mode supplies the replacement should be a low-ESR type and 105 °C rated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup> Higher-value substitutes are physically larger and may not fit the original footprint.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup> For a full refurbishment of old equipment intended for long service, every electrolytic in the unit is replaced as a matter of course; a targeted repair often gets away with recapping only the unregulated input side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1203" title="EEVblog #1203 - REPAIR: Tektronix 2465B Oscilloscope">1203</a></sup>

Brand matters more here than for almost any other passive. The reference names are Panasonic, Nippon Chemi-Con, Nichicon, Rubycon, Elna, Cornell Dubilier and, historically, Sprague.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1457" title="EEVblog1457 - Old School Mailbag - ESC Burnout">1457</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-803" title="EEVblog #803 - HP1740A Analog Oscilloscope">803</a></sup> Counterfeits and low-grade parts are widespread, so data sheets and reputable supply channels are part of the specification, not an optional extra.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup> Panasonic's FR series parts carry no Panasonic branding at all on the can, which can lead to a good capacitor being wrongly suspected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-763" title="EEVblog #763 - Dumpster Plasma TV Bad Cap Repair">763</a></sup> Brand choice is also read as a proxy for a product's overall build standard — bottom-tier capacitors in an otherwise well-engineered instrument stand out precisely because the rest of the design is careful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-347" title="EEVblog #347 - Bad Cap LCD Monitor Repair">347</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-591" title="EEVblog #591 - Agilent 54622D Retro Mixed Signal Osciloscope Review &amp; Teardown">591</a></sup>

## Alternatives and adjacent types

Solid polymer aluminium parts and hybrid electrolytics eliminate the liquid seal and the wear-out mechanism that goes with it, and are identified on a board by the absence of a pressure vent in the top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1288" title="EEVblog #1288 - Synology NAS Dumpster Find! (REPAIR)">1288</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1405" title="EEVblog 1405 - Focusrite Scarlett Solo vs Rode AI-1 TEARDOWN">1405</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1357" title="EEVblog #1357 - Dumpster Diving Santa PC">1357</a></sup> Tantalums are themselves a form of electrolytic despite being spoken of as a separate family, and are inherently more reliable than the aluminium system as well as far smaller, which makes them the preferred choice where a bulky aluminium part would not fit — negative gate bias rails, for instance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9V99J22aiLE" title="Full Discussion - Film Capacitor Failures with Ron Demcko from AVX">•</a></sup> Boards built without any wet electrolytics simply do not have this failure mode available to them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1145" title="EEVBlog #1145 - Dumpster Diving 4K TV!">1145</a></sup>

The dominance of the aluminium electrolytic in older designs is partly a materials-availability artefact. Before large-value ceramics existed, 1 µF or 10 µF was an enormous ceramic part, so anything larger had to be electrolytic; a 100 µF ceramic is taken for granted now, if still expensive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup> Well-made older parts have proven durable — the electrolytics in a 27-year-old portable computer showed no leaking or bulging at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup>

## Handling

A large electrolytic holds its charge. Without a bleeder resistor across it, a mains-side filter cap can retain several hundred volts and take tens of minutes to decay on its own, and a supply's main capacitor may still be charged after the regulator has switched off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> Connecting a charged electrolytic to an LCR meter can destroy the instrument, which is why such meters warn to discharge the device under test first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-757" title="EEVblog #757 - HP4263A LCR Meter Teardown">757</a></sup> The low-impedance voltage function on a [[multimeter]], intended for suppressing ghost voltages, doubles as a safe way to bleed a capacitor down before working on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-631" title="EEVblog #631 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Repair Part 2">631</a></sup>

Mechanically, tall cans are glued or bonded to the board with silicone to stop them flapping in the breeze and vibrating, and a can left unsupported is a legitimate criticism of a design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-814" title="EEVblog #814 - Keysight N8762A 600V 5100W PSU Teardown">814</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup> Electrolytics also survive desoldering well enough to be salvaged from scrap boards and reused, provided the donor equipment has not been thermally stressed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-542" title="EEVblog #542 - ZD985 Desoldering Station">542</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-619" title="EEVblog #619 - Dumpster Dive PABX Teardown">619</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1302" title="EEVblog #1302 - Scrapping a Dumpster Laser Printer For Parts">1302</a></sup>

## An oddity

Heating an aluminium electrolytic with a hot air source causes it to generate a current of its own — tens of microamps, enough to be measured plainly and observed across parts from entirely different manufacturers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering! WTF!">1455</a></sup> The effect explains reports of [[led]] power indicators glowing faintly on boards passing through a reflow oven, and it does not appear to damage the capacitor: measured parameters, including series [[resistance]], are unchanged afterwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1455" title="EEVblog 1455 - Capacitors Produce Current During Reflow Soldering!
