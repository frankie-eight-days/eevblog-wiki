The 555 is an eight-pin bipolar timer integrated circuit, arguably the most famous chip in electronics, built from a handful of general-purpose analogue blocks that can be wired into a very large number of timing and [[oscillator]] circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> It was developed in 1971 by Hans Camenzind, then working as a contractor at Signetics, and has remained essentially unchanged in function since.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> An early-2000s figure put annual sales at around a billion parts a year, and it is still produced in enormous volume.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup>

## Origin and naming

Camenzind's timer grew out of a circuit he had been using as the timing source for phase-locked loops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> Management at Signetics questioned why a standalone timer chip was needed at all; the marketing department disagreed, and the project went ahead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> The first version was taped out in mid-1971, hand-cut in rubylith because no CAD tools existed, and it worked — but it required nine pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> A redesign in October 1971 produced the eight-pin DIP part that became the standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup>

Signetics did not patent the design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> By 1972 there were already a dozen different suppliers, and the part was being shipped and cloned from early that year.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> It reached Australia quickly: an advertisement in *Electronics Australia* of November 1972 already promoted it as an industry standard and as "the timer of a thousand and one uses".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> It became the de facto standard timer almost immediately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup>

The name was assigned by Signetics marketing manager Art Fury.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> Camenzind consistently stated publicly that 555 was an arbitrary number, next in a sequence, and unrelated to the internal circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The internal divider nevertheless contains three nominally 5K resistors, which is the basis of the persistent claim that the number was not a coincidence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup> The CMOS variant does not use the same values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup>

Camenzind, who remained involved in the electronics community until his death, is credited as the inventor of a part still selling in the millions decades later.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-336" title="EEVblog #336 - Drive Time Rant">336</a></sup>

## Internal architecture

The chip divides cleanly into functional blocks: a threshold [[comparator]], a trigger comparator (sometimes called the upper and lower comparators), a flip-flop, an output driver, an open-collector discharge output, and a reset input tied into the flip-flop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> A [[resistor-divider|resistor divider]] of three nominally 5K resistors generates the comparator reference voltages, setting the threshold at two-thirds of the supply and the trigger at one-third.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> With a 9 V supply, the threshold sits at 6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

The control [[voltage]] pin, pin 5, is connected directly to one of the divider taps, which is why it can be used as a modulation input — driving it moves the comparator thresholds and shifts the timing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup> The recommended configuration places a [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] of around 10 nF from pin 5 to [[ground]]; the application note calls for it, and omitting it leaves the reference nodes exposed to [[noise]] and coupling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup>

In normal operation the threshold pin (pin 6) follows the exponential charging [[waveform]] of the timing [[capacitor]], the discharge pin (pin 7) sinks that capacitor when the flip-flop resets, and the output (pin 3) toggles between rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> The internal design leans heavily on constant-[[current]] sources, an arrangement common in comparator-style bipolar chips, which is what permits some of the direct [[transistor]] connections in the output stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

The transistor-level [[schematic]] reproduced in the original Signetics [[datasheet|data sheet]] remains the canonical reference for the internal circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> A transistor-level 555 model also ships as one of the bundled examples with the LTspice circuit simulator, allowing the internal nodes to be probed in simulation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

Discrete transistor-level replica kits exist that break the die out into physically separated blocks — threshold comparator, trigger comparator, flip-flop, output — so each node can be probed with clip leads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Such a replica is only functionally similar rather than identical: process differences between discrete transistors and the monolithic part are unavoidable, and preferred-value substitutions are common, since 5K is not an E12 value and kits typically use 4K7 instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Two 10K resistors in parallel give an exact 5K if authenticity matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## Timing configurations

**Monostable (one-shot).** A [[resistor]] to the supply, a capacitor to ground, and the discharge and threshold pins tied together form the classic one-shot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> A negative-going pulse on the trigger pin starts the timer, which emits a single output pulse whose width is set by the [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> The standard formula is 1.1 × RC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup>

**Astable.** With pins 8 and 4 to the supply and pin 1 to ground, the timing capacitor charges through the timing resistors toward the [[threshold-voltage|threshold voltage]] and is then discharged, giving continuous [[oscillation]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> The [[frequency]] is readily made adjustable by putting a pot in the charging network.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup> The 555 is not an especially stable oscillator, and measured frequency benefits from averaging.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-160" title="EEVblog#160 - 555 Timer Easter Egg?">160</a></sup>

**[[pwm|PWM]].** The classic pulse-width-modulation configuration uses an adjustment pot and two steering diodes so that the charge and discharge paths are separated, with the pot setting the split between them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> In practice this gives close to the full range without quite reaching the endpoints: one such circuit swept from 8.8% to 99.6% [[duty-cycle|duty cycle]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> The pot sets both the frequency and the duty cycle in this arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup> Because the drive stage may be a PNP output transistor, the duty cycle presented to the load can be inverted relative to pin 3.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-392" title="EEVblog #392 - 555 LED PWM Hack">392</a></sup>

**Variable-pulse-width sensing.** Replacing the monostable's timing resistor with a [[potentiometer]] converts shaft or lever position directly into pulse width.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> This was the basis of the analogue PC joystick: the joystick pots, ranging up to around 120K, fed the timing network, and the host measured the resulting pulse length in software — no ADC required, at a time when microcontrollers did not have converters built in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> The scheme was cheap enough that it survived integration into motherboard chipsets, and software could not distinguish a 555-based game adapter from a later ramp-generator-and-comparator implementation as long as the read and write addresses matched.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup>

## Supply range, variants, and limits

The bipolar part operates down to about 4.5 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup> Below that the internal threshold voltages, which scale with the supply, fall far enough that oscillation stops.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup> The CMOS variant operates from lower supplies, and also addresses the shoot-through current on the bipolar output stage and the [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]] consumption of the original.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup>

Multi-timer packages exist: the 556 is the dual and the 558 the quad.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> A space-rated variant is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1746" title="EEVblog 1746 - The 555 is 55 Years Old!">1746</a></sup>

One practical trap appears when substituting parts or building replicas: where the reset pin would normally be tied directly to the supply pin, some equivalents require a [[series-resistor|series resistor]] of around 100K if VCC exceeds 6.5 V, so the substitution is not unconditionally drop-in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup>

## Design practice

For pulse stretching — holding an indicator on after a very short input event — a 555 one-shot is one option, but it needs a fair number of external parts; a 74HC123 retriggerable monostable is the alternative, and simpler discrete solutions may beat both.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-471" title="EEVblog #471 - Overload Detector Circuit Design">471</a></sup> Where a circuit needs any switching source, such as the drive for a [[diode]]-and-capacitor charge-pump voltage [[inverter]], a 555 serves as well as a [[microcontroller]] or a tapped DC-DC switching node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> The chip's slow edges and modest currents make it entirely suitable for [[breadboard]] construction, unlike fast switching-supply work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Dave Jones's first paid design job, at around 12 or 13 years old, was a multi-channel 24 V to 12 V converter box for a tow truck that used a 555 to flash the lights.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-74-KI3DLdc4" title="My First Paid Electronics Gig">•</a></sup> Choosing a 555 over a more specialised timer chip attracts criticism, but the part's availability, low cost, and familiarity generally justify it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-74-KI3DLdc4" title="My First Paid Electronics Gig">•</a></sup> It is expected to remain in use for decades to come.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-97" title="EEVblog #97 - Live Show #2">97</a></sup>

A claimed hidden Easter egg — an astable running at 55.5 kHz with pin 5 left unbypassed, apparently self-modulating at 55.5 Hz — was a fabrication, staged with a concealed second oscillator injecting a 55.5 Hz [[sine-wave|sine wave]] through wires run under the bench mat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> No such behaviour exists in the part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-161" title="EEVblog #161 - 555 April Fools Revealed">161</a></sup>

## In production equipment

The 555 turns up throughout vintage computing and instrumentation. Old PCs of the 1970s and 1980s used it for one-shot and general timing duties.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup> The IBM PCjr carried a 558 quad 555 for its joystick port.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup> The Apple IIc used both a 555 and a 556.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-788" title="EEVblog #788 - Apple IIC Teardown">788</a></sup> The Amiga 500 contained one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-438" title="EEVBlog #438 - Amiga 500 Retro Computer Teardown">438</a></sup> A retro Iskra [[multimeter]] includes a 555 alongside its Intersil converter and 4511 display driver, with date codes placing manufacture in 1980.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-482" title="EEVblog #482 - Retro Iskra Multimeter Teardown">482</a></sup> Aircraft autopilot hardware has been found with 555s date-coded to 1973 and 1974.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-815" title="EEVBlog #815 - Mailbag Teardown Bonanza">815</a></sup>

It also persists in equipment built to avoid digital control entirely: an analogue ramp-and-temperature controller used a 555 together with 4000-series CMOS counters to perform all ramping and timing without a [[processor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hVIo7vmIExw" title="Mystery Dumpster Teardown - 2020-06-12">•</a></sup> Automotive tail-light assemblies have used a 555 alongside constant-current drivers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> It is common in products that need a blinking [[led|LED]] purely to indicate activity, in one case a 555 running from a 7809 regulator driving an LED and nothing else of consequence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup> Touch-sensitive tools often implement a retriggerable monostable that the 555 could equally have provided.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup>

Instrumentation for bench work is built around it too: an alkaline-[[battery-leakage|battery leakage]] test rig required three 555s to generate its pulsed load, assembled from a scrounged single part plus a 556 dual.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup> A decade [[capacitance]] box is useful for exactly this kind of work — setting 555 timing, laying clock edges, and one-shots.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-510" title="EEVblog #510 - Mailbag">510</a></sup>

## As a teaching part

The 555 is one of the first chips a hobbyist encounters, and it remains a standard fixture of beginner kits: electronic dice built from a 555 and a 4017, LED flashers, and educational bundles built around a single timer and a handful of passives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-964" title="EEVblog #964 - Mailbag">964</a></sup> Forrest Mims's *Engineer's Mini-Notebook* series devoted a volume to 555 timer IC circuits, hand-drawn and still relevant.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-733" title="EEVblog #733 - Mailbag">733</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> Magazine literature from the same era covered it exhaustively, including half a dozen different ways to drive a speaker from one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-142" title="EEVblog #142 - Electronics Magazine Memories">142</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1194" title="EEVblog #1194 - 1985 Electronics">1194</a></sup> [[oscilloscope|Oscilloscope]] education packages ship 555 lab exercises complete with schematic and formula.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MguJvnyX4fc" title="Tektronix TBS2000 Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>

Its pedagogical value is not sentimental: the RC time constant that governs it appears everywhere in electronics, and building an astable on a breadboard and having to troubleshoot it teaches the fundamentals that no amount of module-level assembly does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zyuRcsM0gjI" title="eevBLAB #10 - Why Learn Basic Electronics?">•</a></sup> A well-stocked hobby bench is expected to have a few in the junk box alongside 4000- and 74-series logic and basic op-amps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup>
