A sine wave is the elementary alternating [[waveform]], swinging symmetrically positive and negative about a reference level and containing energy at exactly one [[frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-926" title="EEVblog #926 - Introduction To The Oscilloscope">926</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7bVnsXHO6Uw" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 1 - Introduction To Digital Logic">•</a></sup> It is the assumed shape behind almost all of AC circuit analysis: once a circuit is driven with alternating [[current]] and alternating [[voltage]], the quantities of interest are sinusoids that carry both a magnitude — a peak or an RMS voltage — and a phase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1470" title="EEVblog 1470 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 3 - Complex Numbers are EASY!">1470</a></sup> It is also the default stimulus on an electronics bench, because a single-frequency, harmonic-free signal isolates the behaviour of the instrument or circuit under test from the confounding effects of harmonics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup>

## Mathematical description

The instantaneous value of a sinusoidal voltage is written as lower-case v equal to Vmax, the peak voltage of the waveform, multiplied by sine omega t.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1661" title="EEVblog 1661 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 5: Time Domain vs Frequency Domain">1661</a></sup> Omega is angular frequency, equal to 2 pi f, with units of radians per second; in AC circuit analysis it is an angular frequency rather than the angular velocity the same symbol denotes in mathematics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1661" title="EEVblog 1661 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 5: Time Domain vs Frequency Domain">1661</a></sup> Working in radians on a calculator makes the shape transparent: the sine of pi is zero because that point falls in the middle of the waveform, the sine of pi divided by two is one where the waveform peaks, and the sine of three pi divided by two is minus one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup>

Magnitude alone does not describe a sinusoid in a circuit, because two sine waves of the same frequency may be displaced in time relative to one another. That displacement is the phase component, designated phi, and handling it is what forces AC analysis into complex numbers and the rectangular-to-polar and polar-to-rectangular conversions built into every basic scientific calculator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1470" title="EEVblog 1470 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 3 - Complex Numbers are EASY!">1470</a></sup> The sine wave also serves as the canonical illustration of an analog quantity — a signal that may take any value continuously within its range, say between minus one volt and one volt — in contrast to the discrete levels of a digital system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7bVnsXHO6Uw" title="EEVacademy | Digital Design Series Part 1 - Introduction To Digital Logic">•</a></sup>

## RMS, average responding meters and crest factor

For an undistorted sine wave, a true RMS meter reads 0.707 of the peak value, and an average responding meter reads the same 0.707, so the two agree exactly and the error is zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup> This is not a coincidence but a design decision: an average responding [[multimeter]] is calibrated so that its average measurement matches the RMS value of a perfect sine wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup> The consequence is that such a meter assumes the waveform presented in AC voltage or AC current mode is a perfect sine wave, and returns an error for anything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> Square waves, triangle waves, noise, pulse waveforms and SCR switching waveforms all produce errors on an average responding meter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup>

The failure is quantified by crest factor. As a waveform narrows, its peak may be unchanged at one while its RMS value falls well below 0.707 — perhaps to 0.2 — giving a crest factor of five, which approaches the limit at which a true RMS converter itself begins to read low.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> Bench comparison bears the theory out directly: a meter that is not true RMS matches a reference instrument on a sine wave but reads out of specification on square and triangle waves at the same frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 2of2">99</a></sup> The distinction also constrains what a measurement can be trusted to mean: an average responding meter checked against an AC source that puts out a sine wave anyway is being tested only in the case where it cannot fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1692" title="EEVblog 1692 - $130 Fluke 17B MAX Multimeter REVIEW">1692</a></sup>

## The bench standard test signal

A 1 kHz sine wave of about 1 V peak-to-peak is the routine signal for verifying that an [[oscilloscope]] works at all — checking vertical accuracy against the graticule, exercising the trigger, and confirming the auto-set function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-359" title="EEVblog #359 - QDSO Pocket Oscilloscope Review">359</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-813" title="EEVblog #813 - Mailbag">813</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-553" title="EEVblog #553 - Mailbag">553</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1272" title="EEVblog #1272 - Mailbag">1272</a></sup> Amplitudes are chosen to fill the screen conveniently; 400 mV peak-to-peak gives full scale on a scope set appropriately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup> Service procedures specify their own levels, one [[calibration]] recommending a 20 mV peak-to-peak sine wave without stating a frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-208" title="Tektronix 2225 Oscilloscope Teardown and Calibration - EEVblog #208">208</a></sup> The same signal underpins tutorial work, such as the 1 kHz 1 V sine wave used to demonstrate the virtual earth of an inverting op-amp stage, where the signal is present on one side of the input [[resistor]] and apparently vanishes at the summing node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-600" title="EEVblog #600 - OpAmps Tutorial - What is an Operational Amplifier?">600</a></sup>

For dynamic-range and intensity-grading tests, a compound signal is conventional: a 1 MHz sine wave carrier, 100% amplitude modulated by a 1 kHz sine wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup> Displaying its intensity-graded structure depends heavily on acquisition [[memory-depth]] — at 50k points there is simply not enough data for the artifacts to appear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-792" title="EEVBlog #792 - Lecroy Wavejet Touch 354 Oscilloscope Review">792</a></sup> Most scopes will not trigger on such a signal without a deliberate hold-off setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup>

[[bandwidth]] measurement exploits the 0.707 relationship. Feeding a 1 MHz sine wave at 1 V peak-to-peak and raising the input frequency until the displayed amplitude drops to 0.707 of its original value gives the real minus 3 dB bandwidth of the instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-311" title="EEVblog #311 - Jim Williams Pulser Followup">311</a></sup> A 1 V peak-to-peak 100 MHz sine wave reading about 880 mV peak on a 100 MHz instrument is roughly what the minus 3 dB specification predicts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-977" title="EEVblog #977 - Keysight 1000X Hacking - Part 1">977</a></sup> At the other end of the scale, a 1 GHz sine wave at 100 mV RMS reading 102 mV establishes at least 1 GHz of bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> [[frequency-response]] measurement more generally uses a swept sine — a generator in sine wave mode and sweep mode, running from 1 Hz to 100 kHz in one second, drives the horizontal axis of a scope to produce a Bode plot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup> Where a swept sine generator is unavailable, random noise substitutes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> The minimum equipment for a frequency sweep is a [[function-generator]] as a sine source plus a multimeter or scope.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup>

Sinusoidal excitation is equally the tool for probing electromechanical devices. A 1 Hz to 2 Hz sine wave at a couple of volts RMS drives a camera lens voice coil visibly against its fixed permanent magnet,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-849" title="EEVblog #849 - Sony E-Mount Camera Lens Teardown">849</a></sup> and a 10 Hz sine wave drives a hard drive's linear actuator like a loudspeaker, with movement scaling from barely visible at 1 V up to 20 V maximum from the generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-395" title="EEVblog #395 - World&#x27;s Most Expensive Hard Drive Teardown">395</a></sup>

## Generation

Nearly every general-purpose function generator lists sine first among its built-in waveforms, typically alongside square, ramp, pulse, DC and noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-522" title="EEVblog #522 - Rigol DS1000Z Oscilloscope Quick Look">522</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup> Basic instrument-embedded generators offer frequency, amplitude, offset, symmetry and phase control,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup> while cheaper implementations may omit arbitrary waveform and modulation capability entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-149" title="EEVblog #149 - Agilent Infiniivision 3000 X Series Oscilloscope Review">149</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Restrictions can be arbitrary: an instrument whose function generator supports a full waveform set may collapse to sine only when its other modes are engaged, plausibly because the limitation is programmed into the [[fpga]] to allow everything to run at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> A generator can also be used to synthesise a sine wave by hand, inserting points to draw a rough sine or triangle shape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup> Arbitrary sources on AC power supplies extend the same idea to sine, triangle and staircase shapes with explicit clipping and crest factor control.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1698" title="EEVblog 1698 - Awesome Ebay AC Power Source: GW Instek APS-7050">1698</a></sup>

Filter order is directly visible in generated waveforms: increasing the order of a reconstruction filter at 100 kHz yields a nicer, smoother sine wave.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> Similarly, reconstruction on a measurement display can be smooth enough to suggest sine x on x interpolation is at work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4oe6c2uaepM" title="Brymen BM787BT iPhone App Update">•</a></sup>

Test signal quality differs sharply by instrument class. A proper [[lcr-meter]] produces a perfect sine wave at each selected frequency — 100 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz — while a multimeter with [[inductance]] capability may use a triangular-shaped waveform instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1465" title="EEVblog 1465 - Your Multimeter Can Measure Inductors">1465</a></sup> Signal amplitude matters for in-circuit measurement: a sine wave that drops to roughly 220 mV peak-to-peak without clipping is below the level needed to turn on active devices in the circuit under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup>

## Sampling, noise and display artifacts

A sine wave is deceptive under aliasing, because the aliased result is itself a clean sine wave at a completely different frequency — a captured trace gives no visual warning that it is wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> Aliasing is not confined to oscilloscopes but applies to all sampling systems, with frequencies folding back into range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-j49T1E4UEv4" title="Oscilloscope Alias Followup">•</a></sup> A 10 MHz 4 V peak-to-peak sine wave displayed on an undersampled instrument shows the effect plainly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1127" title="EEVblog #1127 - Mailbag Monday">1127</a></sup>

Analog display has the complementary weakness. A sine wave with 30% added noise looks like a very clean sine wave on an analog scope, because the noise is uncorrelated to the sweep signal and appears too briefly to light the phosphors enough for the eye to register.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-601" title="EEVblog #601 - Why Digital Oscilloscopes Appear Noisy">601</a></sup> Digital acquisition modes address the same problem differently: high resolution and averaging modes both clean a 1 kHz sine wave with random noise added, and the difference between them becomes substantial on a 1 MHz sine wave FM modulated at 10 Hz.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-878" title="EEVblog #878 - Oscilloscope High Res vs Average Modes">878</a></sup> Averaging can recover a sine wave from noise outright — improvement is visible at four averages and the signal is clearly extracted by around 80 averages under very low-level conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1744" title="EEVblog 1744 - NEW Micsig DP700 High Voltage Differential Probe">1744</a></sup>

Vertical resolution shows up on small sinusoids. A 100 mV peak-to-peak sine wave on a 1 V per division setting is too small to trigger on reliably, but 12-bit capture makes the recovered waveform usable when the vertical scale is expanded after acquisition.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> Faults are equally visible: a 1 kHz 1 V peak-to-peak sine wave fed into two channels can show correct amplitude and correct low-frequency shape on a defective channel while its high-frequency content is badly degraded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup> Sine waves also expose auto-calibration misbehaviour, a 5 V 10 kHz input jumping in displayed vertical amplitude and acquiring glitches after a range change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup>

## Phase measurement

Phase difference is defined as the difference in degrees between two waveforms at the same reference location, with 360 degrees corresponding to one full cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup> In XY mode, where one channel drives the horizontal axis and the other the vertical, two sine waves offset in phase trace an ellipse, which degenerates to a line at zero phase angle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1751" title="EEVblog 1751 - Oscilloscope Phase Measurement Masterclass">1751</a></sup> Two 1 kHz sine waves that are exactly matched in frequency produce a stationary figure, and offsetting one generator by 1 Hz makes it rotate at a 1 Hz rate in one direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup>

Phase is also what makes a pure sine wave misbehave on an unterminated transmission line. Because of group delay and phase delay, a single-frequency sine wave with no harmonic content at all is still reflected off an open end of [[coaxial-cable|coax]] and returns at a phase depending on frequency, so the signal at the scope can partially or wholly vanish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> For that reason a square wave, whose edges generate a whole set of harmonic frequencies, is the clearer demonstration of transmission line effects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> Simulation of a probe and scope input follows the same structure, with a 1 V amplitude sine wave source behind a 50 ohm source [[impedance]] driving a transmission line model into the oscilloscope input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1445" title="EEVblog 1445 - How to Simulate an Oscilloscope Probe in LTSPICE">1445</a></sup>

## Shaping and detection

A slowly varying sine wave passed through a Schmitt trigger squares up as it transitions through the threshold levels, so a sine wave in gives a square wave out provided the amplitude meets those thresholds — the basis of non-contact [[mains-voltage]] detection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-267" title="EEVblog #267 - Voltage Detection Stick Teardown">267</a></sup> The same principle makes frequency counting possible on sinusoidal inputs: a [[comparator]] issues one pulse each time the sine wave goes high, giving an adequate reading from a counter that otherwise only handles square waves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-NvIv-0-R6qQ" title="Guest Video: TannerTech Designing a Frequency Counter">•</a></sup>

Because a sine wave carries no harmonics, it is the control case in EMC investigation. Switching a susceptibility test from a square wave to a sine wave removes the harmonic content extending into the RF region — and if the fault persists on sine, the harmonics are exonerated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup> A 10 MHz sine wave of a few volts peak-to-peak is enough to provoke a poorly immune multimeter, worsening as amplitude rises to 10 V peak-to-peak.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

## Power sources and inverters

Mains-frequency power electronics is judged largely on how closely its output approximates a sine wave. A variable AC source delivering power at a selectable frequency between 40 Hz and 440 Hz uses [[pwm]] to generate a sine wave output with typical distortion less than 5%.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-449" title="EEVblog #449 - Absopulse VFC500 Variable Frequency Converter Teardown">449</a></sup> [[battery]]-backed inverters are assessed the same way with a high voltage differential probe on the mains output: a small amount of crossover noise at full power is of no practical consequence, and the result counts as a perfectly acceptable true sine wave output at high power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFhk1aRqLD8" title="AllPowers R1500 1500W 1150Wh LiFeP04 Battery Teardown + Review">•</a></sup> Producing a genuine sine wave rather than a switched approximation costs dissipation — the MOSFETs in a sine-output UPS must dissipate more power, which drives the large [[heat-sink|heatsink]] and paralleled devices in the H-bridge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-504" title="EEVblog #504 - UPS Tutorial &amp; Teardown">504</a></sup>

The same criterion separates good from crude AC switching in appliances. A hot air rework station drawing its full rated current can deliver a full sine wave rather than triac chopping,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1059" title="EEVblog #1059 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Waveform Measurement">1059</a></sup> whereas a portable power station showing part of a sine wave interrupted at changeover reveals mechanical input [[relay]] switching rather than electronic switching.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5JT8M8v_zNg" title="EcoFlow Delta Pro - RTFM Dummy!">•</a></sup> Grid-tied solar inverters and microinverters — functionally identical devices differing only in whether there is one per panel or one per string — produce a continuous 240 V sine wave output that is unaffected by clipping of the panel's own power curve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup>

## Deviations from the ideal

Real-world signals presented as sinusoidal frequently are not. A continuity tester's excitation displaying prominent harmonics prompts the direct verdict that "she ain't no sine wave".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1745" title="EEVblog 1745 - Continuity Tester Spectral Probing">1745</a></sup> Precision DC measurement offers a subtler case: a 1 V peak-to-peak 50 Hz sine wave superimposed on 5 V DC is rejected completely when the integration time is set to one power line cycle, but at 0.2 power line cycles the reading jumps around and the extracted data no longer forms a clean sinusoid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1379" title="EEVblog 1379 - What&#x27;s all this NPLC Stuff Anyhow?">1379</a></sup> Filter attenuation figures are equally conditional — a stated minus 60 dB at 10 kHz, meaning 1 mV out for 1 V in, applies only to a sine wave at that frequency and cannot be transferred directly to a PWM signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

Coherence measurement generalises the question. Feeding a perfect sine wave into a system and asking whether a perfect sine wave comes out, evaluated across every frequency element in the range, measures how much of the output correlates with the input; anything short of full correlation indicates non-linearity in the system.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> A single 100 Hz sine wave into such an analyser produces one peak at 100 Hz, while a swept source through a direct connection produces a flat frequency response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup>
