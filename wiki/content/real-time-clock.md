A real-time clock (RTC) is the circuit that keeps calendar time and date running in a piece of equipment while the rest of it is switched off. It is almost always a dedicated low-power counter — either a separate chip or a hardware block inside a [[microcontroller]] — clocked from a 32.768 kHz watch crystal and held alive by a [[battery-backup|backup battery]] or [[capacitor]] when the main supply goes away.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> Its presence or absence is a design decision with visible consequences: equipment without one cannot time-stamp anything and cannot even tell you what day it is after a power cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup>

## Recognising one on a board

The RTC is one of the easiest functions to identify by inspection, because it comes with a standard set of companions: a small package, a 32.768 kHz watch crystal beside it, and a [[battery]] or backup capacitor nearby.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-339" title="EEVblog #339 - Cypres Parachute AAD Teardown">339</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> The crystal is the giveaway — a 32 kHz can next to a small IC is a real-time clock until proven otherwise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-295" title="EEVblog #295 - $30 eBook Reader Teardown">295</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-417" title="EEVblog #417 - Fluke CNX3000 Wireless Multimeter Teardown">417</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-454" title="EEVblog #454 - JVC Camcorder Teardown">454</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-746" title="EEVblog #746 - Sharp X68000 Retro Computer Teardown">746</a></sup> The reasoning runs in both directions: a watch crystal on a board with no obvious clock function still implies an RTC somewhere, because nobody fits one for fun.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-712" title="EEVblog #712 - Uni-T UT71E Multimeter">712</a></sup> Conversely, a crystal at any other [[frequency]] is not an RTC crystal, and a product with no plausible need to know the date has no reason to carry one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1275" title="EEVblog #1275 - Uniden UHF Walkie Talkie Teardowns">1275</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-973" title="EEVblog #973 - Hioki Multimeter Review &amp; Clamp Meter Teardown">973</a></sup>

Backup power sits with the crystal. Coin cells are the norm, sometimes a rechargeable cell where a fixed lithium primary would be inappropriate,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> and sometimes a super capacitor instead: one [[oscilloscope]] used a 4.7 F, 2.7 V super cap as an outright battery replacement for its real-time clock.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup> Elsewhere a large reservoir capacitor keeps time and date alive for an extended period after the power is disconnected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-687" title="EEVblog #687 - EFTPOS PIN Pad Terminal Teardown">687</a></sup> A super cap rated to only 1.6 V is still enough to run an RTC chip, which is a far softer requirement than most other loads on a board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

Placement matters. Putting the RTC chip and its crystal at one end of the board and the backup battery at the other is poor layout practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1360" title="EEVblog #1360 - REPAIR - Aircon Control Panel">1360</a></sup> The 32 kHz crystal is physically fragile and gets mechanically secured: gunked down with potting compound on older machines,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-116" title="EEVblog #116 - Retro Teardown - Tandy Radio Shack TRS-80 Model 100">116</a></sup> soldered down rather than left standing on its leads on newer ones — a nice touch when it is done, and visibly bodged when it is done badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1231" title="EEVblog #1231- New Uni-T UPO3000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1231</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup> The crystal traces should be kept short with the load capacitors returning to [[ground]] close by.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> Some low-power RTC oscillators integrate their load capacitors and even the [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]], the latter switched off the enable pin so that disabling the chip opens the loop and stops the [[oscillator]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1614" title="EEVblog 1614 - Circuit Design TIP: Crystal Oscillators">1614</a></sup>

## Dedicated chips

The dedicated RTC part is old, cheap and abundant. Historic examples run from the Motorola MC146818 and MC68HC68,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-339" title="EEVblog #339 - Cypres Parachute AAD Teardown">339</a></sup> the 4543,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup> and the Dallas parts that dominated the category — including a battery-backed variant that is pin-for-pin compatible with an [[sram|SRAM]] chip of the same size but carries a built-in lithium primary cell with a guaranteed ten-year factory life from the date of purchase.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-187" title="EEVblog #187 - Tektronix TDS2024C Oscilloscope Teardown">187</a></sup> Later and [[current]] parts include the DS1307 and DS1302,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-C1on-LaIsCA" title="EEVblog #b10000000000 - 1K Micro Magic">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1018" title="EEVblog #1018 - ZeroPlus Logic Analysers">1018</a></sup> the TI BQ32002,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup> and the Microchip MCP7940.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> On PC-era hardware the function was frequently absorbed into combination chips: a floppy drive controller with the real-time clock built in,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-639" title="EEVblog #639 - IBM L40SX Retro Laptop Teardown">639</a></sup> or the multi-combo IO cards that folded serial, parallel, joystick and RTC onto one expansion card to stop each function eating a slot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1054" title="EEVblog #1054 - How an Analog PC Joystick Works">1054</a></sup>

Interface is usually [[i2c|I2C]] or a simple serial protocol, undemanding enough to bit-bang. A 1999 magazine project built a video caption generator around a PIC16F84 and a DS1307, with the main crystal and a separate 32 kHz crystal for the RTC, bit-banging an I2C port and fitting time, date and overlay into just 1 k of program memory.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-C1on-LaIsCA" title="EEVblog #b10000000000 - 1K Micro Magic">•</a></sup>

The parts cost almost nothing. Bulk Chinese catalogue suppliers list real-time clock chips at around 10 cents in quantities of five, which makes paying substantially more than that hard to justify.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1132" title="EEVblog #1132 - The 3 Cent Microcontroller!">1132</a></sup>

A common design error is over-protecting the backup cell: a series [[diode]] between the coin cell and the RTC chip is unnecessary, and so is a 1 k [[series-resistor|series resistor]], since a coin cell holder inserted backwards causes no harm.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup> Where the battery is wired straight to the RTC's supply pin, there is no way to monitor its state without an extra line to the microcontroller, and any sensing arrangement — even through a large series resistor — puts additional drain on the cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The consequence of accepting that trade-off is that a dead cell announces itself only by behaviour: the clock resets on every boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Multimeter Kickstarter Live Chat">121</a></sup> The same symptom on a PC-class board points at the same cause.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-MqECOT5j-cE" title="Latte Panda BIOS Linux Troubleshooting - Featuring Sagan">•</a></sup>

## Integrated RTC in microcontrollers

Modern low-power microcontrollers include a hardware real-time clock and calendar, and the current it draws in standby is one of the headline numbers in the [[datasheet]]. A PIC24F specifies 400 nA in real-time clock and calendar operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> An STM32L part draws 800 nA in stop mode with RTC plus 8 kB of SRAM retention, against 400 nA in stop mode alone,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> with other devices in the family quoted at 270 nA standby and 400 nA stop before the RTC is added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup> Ultra-low-power parts elsewhere reach 900 nA in deep sleep with the real-time clock still running, and 20 nA in full shutoff where nothing at all is happening.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-266" title="EEVblog #266 - Mailbag">266</a></sup> A stop mode measured at 620 nA covers the 32 kHz oscillator, the real-time clock and some [[ram|RAM]] retention together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-290" title="EEVblog #290 - Mailbag">290</a></sup>

The internal RTC is not free of constraints. It needs its own 32.768 kHz source, which on a microcontroller means either an external watch crystal or an internal 32 kHz oscillator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> Where the RTC counter is fed from the secondary oscillator, that same secondary oscillator may be the only low-frequency source available to a general-purpose timer, so the two functions compete unless a mux allows the 32.768 kHz watch crystal to drive both the RTC counter and the timer input.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup> Vendor configuration tools expose the RTC alongside the other peripherals, including its [[calibration]] values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-269" title="EEVblog #269 - Energy Micro Tiny Gecko">269</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-900" title="EEVblog #900 - STM32 ARM Development Board">900</a></sup> Selecting a microcontroller for a battery-powered product, a hardware real-time clock and calendar is a desirable feature but not usually a deal breaker, since an external chip can always be added.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup>

Integrated RTCs are also as prone to silicon errata as anything else: one PIC24F series carried an errata list covering the brownout reset, the real-time clock, I2C, UART, JTAG, SRAM, ADC, [[spi|SPI]] and core functions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3" title="EEVblog #3 - Static Myths, PIC Micro, Pocket Multimeter">3</a></sup>

## What it is actually for

Anything that logs needs to date and time stamp its records, which is why data loggers and instruments carry an RTC as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-614" title="EEVblog #614 - Cebit 2014 Highlights">614</a></sup> A smart meter with 2.1 MB of non-volatile memory recording ten years of 30-minute interval data has a built-in real-time clock as part of that capability.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup> An access control terminal with a large battery backup tracks the time and date of every card entry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-673" title="EEVblog #673 - Mailbag">673</a></sup> A defibrillator carrying a 32.768 kHz watch crystal and backup battery is almost certainly recording the time and date of the incident — though for a device that may sit installed for years after a single factory or installation-time setting, the date is likelier to be right than the time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup> Bench instruments use it for date-stamped screen captures and saved files, and a scope without one gets the date and time in the corner of every capture wrong.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> Test equipment of all kinds — multimeters, spectrum analysers, function generators, oscilloscopes, source measure units, power supplies — carries the standard battery-plus-crystal cluster for exactly this reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-384" title="EEVblog #384 - Agilent 4000X Oscilloscope Teardown">384</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-187" title="EEVblog #187 - Tektronix TDS2024C Oscilloscope Teardown">187</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - RTC functionality is also routinely folded into power management and application processors rather than provided separately: PMICs combine an RTC with [[battery-charging|battery charging]], coulomb counting and the DC-DC converters that power the [[processor]] cores,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-189" title="iRiver Story HD eBook Reader TEARDOWN - EEVblog #189">189</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-109" title="Amazon Kindle 3 3G/GSM/WiFi 6&quot; TEARDOWN - EEVblog #109">109</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup> and application processors for cameras and recorders list a real-time clock among their built-in peripherals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-672" title="EEVblog #672 - GoPro Hero 4 Silver Teardown">672</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-399" title="EEVblog #399 - GoPro Hero 2 Teardown">399</a></sup>

## Accuracy and its absence

A crystal-referenced RTC should hold time to something on the order of a minute a month at worst case, across temperature variation, with any crystal at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4vY5-obAx6w" title="Deye Hybrid Solar Inverter RTC FAIL!">•</a></sup> A hybrid solar [[inverter]] fitted with a proper RTC chip and a 32.768 kHz watch crystal that nonetheless loses time is broken, not normal, and telling the owner to enable an internet time-sync function is not an answer to that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4vY5-obAx6w" title="Deye Hybrid Solar Inverter RTC FAIL!">•</a></sup>

Omitting the RTC entirely is a cost decision that shows up in use. A low-cost 12-bit oscilloscope with no backup battery has no real-time clock and no way to set the clock for anything, so its stored captures are stamped with the wrong date and time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup> The Raspberry Pi has no real-time clock, which is why add-on boards advertise one as a feature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup> Older machines make the omission plainer still: the first portable computer had no battery backup and no real-time clock, so it could not keep time when switched off,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup> and the HP 9845B had no real-time clock at all — one had to be plugged in as an expansion card alongside the serial, plotter, printer and mass storage interfaces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1404" title="EEVblog 1404 - The Amazing HP 9845B Computer">1404</a></sup> Historic timing hardware could also impose odd constraints: on a Tandy 1000, the timer chip serving as the real-time clock required a fixed 2.38 MHz signal, so a turbo board had to synthesise that signal regardless of the processor clock speed selected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-32" title="EEVblog #32 - Tandy 1000 Retro Computer time!">32</a></sup>

The distinction between having a real-time clock and not having one had real administrative weight during the Y2K remediation effort, where every project containing a microcontroller — including those with no real-time clock in them — still needed a written report certifying Y2K compatibility.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1376" title="EEVblog 1376 - Tandy 102 Vintage Computer REPAIR">1376</a></sup>
