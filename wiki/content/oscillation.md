Oscillation is the unintended self-sustaining [[ringing]] of a circuit that was meant to sit still, and it is one of the most common ways a nominally correct design fails on the bench. Any closed-loop system — an op-amp stage, a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]], a [[current-source|current source]], an [[electronic-load|electronic load]], a bench [[power-supply|power supply]] — carries enough gain and phase shift to become an [[oscillator]] if it is compensated badly or loaded outside the conditions it was designed for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1609" title="EEVblog 1609 - Composite Amplifier Tutorial + Practical Demo">1609</a></sup> Oscillation is also useful in diagnosis: it is a visible symptom that points at a loop, a load, or a marginal logic level, whereas a circuit that simply does nothing offers no clue at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup>

## Closed-loop amplifier stability

A closed-loop amplifier needs dominant pole compensation; without it the stage oscillates like a banshee.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup> The practical method for checking a loop is to drive it with a [[square-wave|square wave]] from a [[signal-generator|signal generator]] and look at the shape of the output response.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-629" title="EEVblog #629 - How To Design a Microphone Preamplifier">629</a></sup> Excessive ringing or [[overshoot]] on that step is the precursor to outright oscillation, so the test is deliberately pushed to worst case — adding capacitive load until the overshoot looks awful — to see whether the stage will break into oscillation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup>

Capacitive loading on an op-amp output is a standard trigger. Adding bypass [[capacitance]] directly to an op-amp output, even through a [[series-resistor|series resistor]], loads it capacitively and is not a good idea as a general fix, even where it happens to cure the immediate problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-l2LBkXxN81Y" title="µCurrent Offset Voltage Fix">•</a></sup> The alternative — bypassing the input side of the divider that creates a [[virtual-ground|virtual ground]] rather than the output — changes the response away from ideal in its own way, so neither solution is free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n7XX75S98e8" title="µCurrent Opamp Divider Input Bypass Testing">•</a></sup>

Composite amplifiers, which cascade two op-amps inside one feedback loop to combine the best properties of each, oscillate readily if implemented carelessly. The general rule is that the second-stage amplifier must have a greater gain-[[bandwidth]] product than the first, otherwise the pair tends to oscillate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1609" title="EEVblog 1609 - Composite Amplifier Tutorial + Practical Demo">1609</a></sup>

Not every stage is at risk. An amplifier fed directly from a low-[[impedance]] [[battery]], with no switching supply anywhere in the signal path, is stable enough that supply decoupling can be omitted entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-528" title="EEVblog #528 - Opamp Input Noise Voltage Tutorial">528</a></sup>

## Regulators and references

Low-dropout regulators are a classic stability problem. They use a PNP or P-channel pass element, which is inherently less stable than the alternative, and they will oscillate unless loaded with the correct amount of capacitance, of the correct type, with the correct ESR.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup> Verifying that an assembled regulator is not oscillating is a routine part of characterising it: the output is examined AC-coupled on a scope — 20 mV per division is a useful setting — and if the output is clean and the part is not dropping out, the [[noise]] performance follows.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-158" title="EEVblog #158 - AVR ISP MK2 + LM317 Regulator Tutorial">158</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> A well-behaved regulator will recover from a load step with no ringing and no oscillation even into hundreds of microfarads of [[output-capacitance|output capacitance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1147" title="EEVblog #1147 - 1 Cent Regulator! That&#x27;s MAD!">1147</a></sup>

The loop of a discrete linear regulator depends strongly on the pass [[transistor]]. Substituting a 2N2222 as the output device changes the loop response enough to make the circuit oscillate at high [[frequency]], and simulation reproduces this closely enough to match what is measured on the bench.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup> When a loop is genuinely unstable, output capacitance is not a cure: a discrete regulator built with an extra gain-of-four stage folded into the feedback loop oscillated no matter how much output capacitance was added, and stepping the simulated capacitance from 10 µF down to 1 µF and then removing it entirely made no difference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-95" title="EEVblog #95 - Linear Regulators, Closed Loops, Simulations, &amp; Brand Shenanigans">95</a></sup>

Because stability is a property of the specific silicon and not just of the [[schematic]], a part carrying a new suffix must be re-qualified rather than dropped in: it may drop out differently, or oscillate with load and bypass combinations that the old part tolerated.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1752" title="EEVblog 1752 - Texas Instruments SCREWED UP the NE5532!">1752</a></sup>

## Precision current sources

A precision current source built directly from a [[voltage]]-reference application note oscillated badly enough to be unusable, which is the standard argument for breadboarding an application note rather than trusting it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> The symptom on the sense output was gross AC noise at 20 mV per division.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> Fixes proved narrowly conditional: a 10 µF ceramic [[output-capacitor|output capacitor]] landed in exactly the wrong spot and put oscillation into the loop, while other values worked, and a 470 µF [[capacitor]] across the sense [[resistor]] could not settle the loop once a 12 kΩ resistor was shorted out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup> The circuit could be made stable for one particular load, which is acceptable when the intended use is a single fixed operating point such as a 1 A generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-577" title="EEVblog #577 - Precision 1A Current Source Part 2">577</a></sup>

## Power supplies, loads and load interaction

Oscillation frequently belongs to neither instrument on the bench but to the combination of the two. A bench supply driving an electronic load in constant-power mode at 96 W oscillated because the supply's response loop and the load's response loop did not work together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> Constant-[[resistance]] mode at 1 Ω, with the supply [[current]]-limited to 5 A, produced the same audible oscillation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-439" title="EEVblog #439 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Review">439</a></sup> A supply whose output was switched off still went into [[constant-current-mode|constant current mode]] and oscillated audibly, with the front-panel [[led|LED]] blinking, when an electronic load was connected to it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> All of the difficulty in an electronic load — otherwise little more than an op-amp, a resistor, a bank of MOSFETs and a [[heat-sink|heatsink]] — is in the loop stability that keeps it from oscillating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> At very low terminal voltages, constant-power mode can break into oscillation with the readings jumping around.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-774" title="EEVblog #774 - Low Battery Discharge Testing Part 1">774</a></sup>

Current-limit behaviour in a supply is itself a controlled oscillation: as the output capacitance discharges the voltage falls, the load current falls with it, and the loop oscillates and servos around an average of the set current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-221" title="EEVblog #221 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 1">221</a></sup> A clean transition from constant voltage into constant current shows neither overshoot nor oscillation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup>

## Digital circuits

Oscillation in logic is usually the result of an input that is neither a valid high nor a valid low. In a CMOS gate, a slowly ramping input turns both transistors partially on, drawing excess current, and if the input oscillates the excess current is drawn as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> A Schmitt trigger input removes the oscillation and the problems that follow from it, though it does not remove the increased supply current caused by a slow input; the supply current of a Schmitt trigger gate still peaks at the two threshold voltages.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> The slower the input transition, the more oscillation is produced, and even a fast transition leaves some.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup> The consequence is not merely wasted power: an oscillating edge feeding an interrupt input rather than an indicator can trigger repeatedly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-941" title="EEVblog #941 - Schmitt Trigger Tutorial">941</a></sup>

The same failure appears at board level. A leaky axial tantalum produced a non-valid logic level, the following stage oscillated, and its output drove a reset line, giving intermittent operation and an anomalous 4 mA supply current — a fault that would have appeared to work if tested at the moment the level happened to be valid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1245" title="EEVblog #1245 - A Most Excellent REPAIR: IBM PCjr Keyboard">1245</a></sup> Where a counter misbehaves, zooming in on the clock edge to look for multiple transitions distinguishes a genuine count error from an input oscillating and going metastable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1208" title="EEVblog #1208 - Circuit Analysis &amp; Debugging">1208</a></sup>

## Intentional oscillation

Where oscillation is the intended function, the same loop conditions must be satisfied deliberately. An oscillator that will not start is often a phase problem rather than a gain problem: amplifier stages measuring the correct gain of roughly 40 times each will still fail to oscillate if the feedback arrives out of phase with itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XRyDyOANQhc" title="Guest Video: The Defpom - Fluke Calibrator Repair">•</a></sup> Confirming that a board oscillates at roughly the right frequencies isolates the fault to the control circuitry elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XRyDyOANQhc" title="Guest Video: The Defpom - Fluke Calibrator Repair">•</a></sup> Injecting an external oscillator signal into a running oscillator makes the result jump around as the two oscillations conflict, so the injected signal must be removed before the native oscillation can be judged.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XRyDyOANQhc" title="Guest Video: The Defpom - Fluke Calibrator Repair">•</a></sup>

A soft-latching power switch oscillates by design if the button is held down past the [[rc-time-constant|RC time constant]], and the oscillation frequency is set by that time constant — reducing the capacitor from 47 µF to 10 µF makes it oscillate correspondingly faster.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-262" title="EEVblog #262 - World&#x27;s Simplest Soft Latching Power Switch Circuit">262</a></sup> A blinking LED circuit stops oscillating well above its rated minimum supply voltage when the discharge resistor is too low; at 130 Ω the current through the internal discharge transistor is high enough to stop the circuit even at supply voltages above the chips' 4.5 V operating minimum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1653" title="EEVblog 1653 - Alkaline Battery Leakage Testing 2 - Electric Boogaloo">1653</a></sup>

## Control loops and slow oscillation

Oscillation at the timescale of seconds to minutes is the same phenomenon in a mechanical or thermal loop. In PID tuning, increasing the integral and proportional terms substantially increases overshoot; the derivative term resists change and therefore reduces the oscillations, since both overshoot and oscillation are change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VVOi2dbtxC0" title="EEVacademy #6 - PID Controllers Explained">•</a></sup> A resistance standard held in a temperature-controlled room showed a visible oscillation in its reading traceable to the air conditioner cycling on a nominal five-minutes-on, five-minutes-off pattern, superimposed on a slow ramp as the room came down from 26 °C to 22 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-834" title="EEVblog #834 - Wekomm Resistance Standard">834</a></sup> A heat-pump hot water system monitored by a dedicated [[current-clamp|current clamp]], with no history of oscillation in the logged data, began oscillating on its power draw overnight — the sort of intermittent control-loop fault that a power cycle can clear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Uq553ckJ0uQ" title="MURPHY! - Reclaim Hot Water Heat Pump FAIL">•</a></sup>

## Finding the source

The first question is whether the oscillation belongs to the circuit or to the measurement. Disconnecting the meter and the probe leads establishes whether the instrument is contributing, and an unloaded output can oscillate on its own account: a [[power-rail|power rail]] probe that behaved perfectly into 50 Ω oscillated at its output as soon as the scope input was switched to 1 MΩ, a fault attributable to the output driver disliking the absence of a 50 Ω termination.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1733" title="EEVblog 1733 - Mailbag: $100 2GHz Power Rail Probe, Retro Chip Tester">1733</a></sup>

Beyond that, the technique is substitution and selective disconnection. Swapping between chips from different manufacturers isolated an oscillation to one particular part variant in one particular circuit configuration and load, and the culprit turned out to be the virtual-rail chip rather than the obvious suspect.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup> The oscillation was high-frequency, at roughly 2.57 MHz, and increasing capacitive load from a few hundred picofarads through 2 µF to 100 µF changed its severity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1057" title="EEVblog #1057 -  µCurrent Murphy">1057</a></sup> In an amplifier repair, powering the unit with the preamp disconnected removed the oscillation, though that alone does not prove the preamp is the source — the disconnection also changes what else is switched in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1072" title="EEVblog #1072 - KRK Rokit 6 Studio Monitor Speaker Repair">1072</a></sup> Suspecting a capacitor is harder when the unit is run from a DC supply, since there is no [[ripple]] for a failed [[bypass-capacitor|bypass capacitor]] to pass and the observed symptom is oscillation of the power amplifier module itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1243" title="EEVblog #1243 - Sony Boombox REPAIR and Teardown">1243</a></sup>

A [[multimeter]] on its ohms range whose reading will not settle indicates an active driver on the pin rather than a passive resistance, and probing this way is generally safe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p-eLu1z7-cs" title="Rigol HDO1000 Serial Boot Capture for Hack + R&amp;S MXO4 play">•</a></sup> Where the oscillation is suspected to come from the construction rather than the circuit, the parasitics can be measured and simulated: the inter-contact capacitance of a solderless [[breadboard]] is 2 pF, consistent across four different types, and that figure can be dropped straight into a simulation to explain why a breadboarded circuit oscillates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-568" title="EEVblog #568 - Solderless Breadboard Capacitance">568</a></sup>

Not everything that looks like oscillation is electrical. A [[ceramic-capacitor|ceramic capacitor]] exhibiting the piezoelectric effect produces a signal with a superimposed period that is not at a fixed frequency, the interval between successive peaks visibly varying.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-855" title="EEVblog #855 - Ceramic Capacitor Piezoelectric Effect">855</a></sup>
