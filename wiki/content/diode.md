A diode is a two-terminal semiconductor device that conducts [[current]] in one direction only, which is why its [[schematic]] symbol is drawn as an arrow pointing in the direction of conventional current flow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> It is among the most fundamental components in electronics, serving in rectification, reverse-polarity protection, transient clamping, signal steering, logic, and detection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> Diodes are polarity-sensitive: the [[cathode]] end carries a band marking on through-hole and surface-mount packages, the opposite end being the anode, and schematic reference designators D or CR both denote a diode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aQ2AVLs8_7k" title="CB2 Microcomputer Kit Assembly &amp; Soldering">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (2 of 5): PCB SMD Hand Soldering &amp; Assembly">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1708" title="EEVblog 1708 - What&#x27;s This SMD Part?">1708</a></sup>

## Electrical characteristics

A silicon PN-junction diode conducts essentially nothing until the forward [[voltage]] exceeds a knee of roughly 0.6 V, the exact value depending on temperature, device, and current; above the knee it conducts freely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> The forward drop in practice runs 0.6 V to about a volt, rising toward a volt at higher currents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> Turn-on is not abrupt — the characteristic curve rises progressively rather than snapping on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-443" title="EEVblog #443 - Mailbag">443</a></sup> Reverse-biased, an idealized diode passes no current, but real devices exhibit reverse leakage, which is normally negligible yet can exceed the [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] of a good FET-input op-amp and therefore matters in precision circuits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> A PN junction also exhibits [[capacitance]], known as junction, transition, or depletion capacitance, distinct from the diffusion capacitance associated with charge storage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1384" title="EEVblog 1384 - Halve Your Processor Power Consumption!">1384</a></sup>

Several other devices are built on, or behave as, diode junctions. The base-emitter junction of a [[bipolar-transistor|bipolar transistor]] is a PN junction that functions exactly like a diode, and a [[transistor]] can be used as one; its roughly 0.7 V drop can have circuit consequences, such as clamping a node too low to drive a 2 V [[led|LED]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-748" title="EEVblog #748 - How Do Transistors Work?">748</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-242" title="EEVblog #242 - Hakko FX-888 Soldering Iron Hack">242</a></sup> An SCR is essentially a diode with an added gate terminal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1172" title="EEVblog #1172 - TRIAC Testing (WEP Meltdown Part 2)">1172</a></sup> A silicon [[solar-cell|solar cell]] is effectively just a diode that produces voltage when photons strike it, about 0.6 V per cell.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1426" title="EEVblog 1426 - WOW! This Problem DROPS Solar Output by 20% !">1426</a></sup> An LED is a diode whose light output is close to linear with current even though its voltage-drop behavior is non-linear.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

## Device types

- **Standard silicon rectifiers and signal diodes.** Workhorse parts such as the 1N4007 handle power-line rectification, while small-signal types such as the 1N4148 leak only tens of nanoamps in reverse, good enough for most isolation duty.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1449" title="EEVblog 1449 - What Causes Excess Battery Drain? (BM235)">1449</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>
- **Schottky diodes.** These drop roughly 0.2–0.3 V instead of 0.6 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup> For reverse-polarity or clamping protection this is a decisive advantage, because a Schottky clamp holds the protected node below the ~0.6 V threshold at which the silicon PN junctions of downstream transistors and ICs would otherwise conduct.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>
- **Zener diodes.** A Zener behaves exactly like a regular diode in the forward direction — same 0.6 V to 1 V drop — but also conducts in reverse at its Zener breakdown voltage, making it useful as a [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] and clamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> The breakdown voltage is not fixed: it shifts with current and with [[junction-temperature|junction temperature]], and since Zeners are typically used as power devices they self-heat, a genuine "trap for young players".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup> Ordinary and Schottky diodes also exhibit reverse breakdown, and a BJT base-emitter junction in reverse breaks down at roughly 6 V, usable as an impromptu Zener while behaving as a 0.6 V diode in the forward direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-n4NBUruLyoo" title="The 1000th Video - The Lost Tape">•</a></sup>
- **Germanium and point-contact diodes.** Vintage equipment contains germanium types, including tiny point-contact devices, alongside silicon ones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-649" title="EEVblog #649 - Power Designs 2005 PSU Teardown">649</a></sup>
- **LEDs used as ordinary diodes.** With forward drops around 1.6–1.85 V for traditional parts, LEDs occasionally appear doing plain diode duty, for instance in series with an indicator lamp and [[dropper-resistor|dropper resistor]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1209" title="EEVblog #1209 - Mailbag">1209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup>
- **Multiple-diode packages.** Dual diodes such as the BAV99 come in SOT-23, and [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]] packages integrate four diodes; a nominal 1 A bridge such as the DF02 can nonetheless be rated for a 50 A single half-sine surge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup>

## Applications

### Rectification and power conversion

A single diode with a [[transformer]] produces a half-wave rectified [[waveform]]: the positive half-cycle passes, the negative half is blocked because the diode is reverse-biased.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1417" title="EEVblog 1417 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 1: Alternating Current">1417</a></sup> A conventional bridge rectifier sacrifices at least 0.6 V per diode — up to a volt at high current — so a synchronous rectifier replaces the diodes with MOSFETs whose low on-[[resistance]] eliminates most of the loss.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> In a buck or boost switch-mode converter the catch diode carries the [[inductor]] current during the switch-off interval.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> A critical sizing rule follows: the diode and inductor must be rated for the peak current, not the [[output-current|output current]] — a converter delivering 100 mA output may see 0.74 A peaks, calling for a 2 A diode such as a B220 where a 100 mA part would fail.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup> Diode type also directly affects converter [[efficiency]], and some converter ICs integrate the diode to cut parts count and cost.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-110" title="EEVblog #110 - Let&#x27;s Design a DC to DC Switchmode Converter">110</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-139" title="EEVblog #139 - Let&#x27;s Select a DC-DC Boost Converter">139</a></sup>

Diode-[[capacitor]] networks build voltage multipliers. In a Cockcroft-Walton multiplier each stage sees only the input-level voltage, so a chain producing 5–10 kV out needs only ~1 kV rated diodes and capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup> [[microcontroller|Microcontroller]]-driven charge pumps double or invert a supply by steering charge with diodes, though real Schottky drops of ~0.3 V each pull the output below the ideal calculated value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-473" title="EEVBlog #473 - Microcontroller Voltage Doubler">473</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> Substituting a TVS device for the usual Schottky at a supply input yields both transient overvoltage and reverse-voltage protection in one part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-233" title="EEVblog #233 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 6 - LT3080 Testing">233</a></sup>

### Protection and clamping

A reverse-biased diode across a [[relay]] coil or other inductive load is close to mandatory: when the drive is removed, the collapsing [[magnetic-field|magnetic field]] keeps current flowing, and the diode conducts to clamp the spike to about 0.6 V below the rail instead of letting it reach tens of volts and destroying the driver.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1406" title="EEVblog 1406 - DC Fundamentals Part 7: DC Circuit Transients Fundamentals">1406</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-708" title="EEVblog #708 - Free Energy Overunity is RUBBISH!">708</a></sup> A series diode at a power input blocks reverse current if the supply is connected backwards, a use seen from [[usb|USB]]-powered gadgets to vintage calculators.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1571" title="EEVblog 1571 - Nobel Prize Winning Mailbag. 121GW, Retro calcs, Assembly, and Discrete Logic">1571</a></sup> Diode OR-ing selects between a main rail and a [[battery-backup|backup battery]] automatically, and a series diode isolates a [[supercapacitor]] or [[battery]] so it cannot back-feed the rest of a product at power-down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-942" title="EEVblog #942 - Mystery Monday Teardown">942</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup> Some designs avoid diodes entirely for supply selection — a DC jack with a built-in changeover switch needs no active circuitry at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1015" title="EEVblog #1015 - Beware Evil (But Clever) DC Jacks!">1015</a></sup> In a defibrillator, where high-voltage capacitors sit in series, a diode across each capacitor prevents manufacturing tolerances from reverse-charging one capacitor during discharge, which could burst it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-909" title="EEVblog #909 - Heart Defibrillator Teardown">909</a></sup>

Diode bridges form the front-line overload protection of multimeters: stacked diodes across the [[current-shunt|current shunt]] clamp the [[burden-voltage|burden voltage]] — six in series give roughly 3.6 V — forcing current through the diodes long enough for the [[fuse]] to blow rather than the [[current-shunt-resistor|shunt resistor]], and extra series diodes can be added where a higher clamp voltage is tolerable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-373" title="EEVblog #373 - Multimeter Input Protection Tutorial">373</a></sup> The scheme depends on surge ratings far above the continuous rating: parts in this role hold around 50 A for 8.3 ms or ~10 A for a full second while the [[hrc-fuse|HRC fuse]] clears.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup> Device programmers use a similar idea with a programmable clamp rail, holding each pin within 0.6 V of a set voltage [[via]] diode arrays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1060" title="EEVblog #1060 - $35,000 DataIO Unisite Universal Programmer Teardown!">1060</a></sup> Integrated circuits carry ESD protection diodes on their pins even though many datasheets never draw them, and applying a negative excursion to a de-energized board can forward-bias these junctions — a phenomenon exploited by curve-tracer-style fault finders and a confounder in other measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-831" title="EEVblog #831 - Power A Micro With No Power Pin!">831</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src--ON1AdmAMOY" title="Vintage Tek Museum: Curve Bug Signal Debugger">•</a></sup>

### Signal and measurement circuits

A diode and capacitor form the simplest peak detector, and closing an op-amp's feedback loop around the diode makes a precision rectifier in which the amplifier drives its output to cancel the diode drop in real time, compensating for temperature and even a poor diode; adding a second diode and [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]] holds zero volts across the rectifying diode at the peak, eliminating its reverse leakage from the measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> Diodes also build logic: diode NOR and OR gates steer forcing signals in discrete digital clock designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-801" title="EEVblog #801 - How To Design A Digital Clock">801</a></sup> Placing a diode across a [[resistor]] gives asymmetric charge and discharge paths, a standard trick in switch-debounce timing networks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-961" title="EEVblog #961 - Monkey Debouncing">961</a></sup> Series diodes serve as crude level shifters — one or two 1N914s add a predictable ~0.6 V per junction of offset, for example to lift an op-amp input away from a rail it cannot quite reach or to raise a [[mosfet|MOSFET]]'s effective threshold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-567" title="EEVblog #567 - Precision 1A Current Source">567</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-579" title="EEVblog #579 - Precision Low Current Source">579</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-260" title="EEVblog #260 - Tracking Pre-Regulator Simulation in LTspice - PSU Part 13">260</a></sup>

## Testing and measurement

The diode-test function on a [[multimeter]] measures forward [[voltage-drop|voltage drop]] — typically 0.5–0.6 V for silicon — and generally works with the part in circuit, making it useful both for identifying orientation and for [[troubleshooting]], including LEDs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1522" title="EEVblog 1522 - BM786 Multimeter Repair PART 2">1522</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup> Meter test voltage sets a ceiling: a 36 V Zener cannot be broken down by a meter whose diode range tops out at 15 V, though it can still be verified as conducting in one direction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1395" title="EEVblog 1395 - Onkyo Repair SUCCESS">1395</a></sup> In-circuit [[capacitance-measurement|capacitance measurement]] has a related trap — an [[lcr-meter|LCR meter]]'s test signal can itself turn on parallel diodes, ESD structures, and other active junctions, corrupting the reading; meters offering a 0.1 V RMS test level instead of 0.5 V RMS exist precisely to keep those junctions off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1474" title="EEVblog 1474 - Can You Measure Capacitors IN Circuit?">1474</a></sup> On a component tester or curve tracer a diode traces a characteristic L-shaped curve, distinct from a resistor's straight line or a capacitor's ellipse, and a source-measure unit captures the full forward characteristic curve point by point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-638" title="EEVblog #638 - Apollo Saturn V LVDC Testing">638</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-718" title="EEVblog #718 - Keithley 2400 SMU Teardown">718</a></sup> Component-testing tweezers display diode orientation directly, though units with a low test threshold cannot light even a standard 1.8 V green LED, let alone white ones; other tweezers can exercise LEDs in resistance or capacitance modes instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> A diode that measures as a similar drop in both directions is faulty or paralleled by another path — an in-circuit reading of ~0.5 V both ways is a red flag, not a pass.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1649" title="EEVblog 1649 - Mailbag: M5stack, Beelink Mini PC, ZOYI LCR, ToughOn Battery">1649</a></sup>

## Failure modes and repair

[[short-circuit|Short circuit]] is a recognized diode failure mode, and a shorted rectifier or clamp can dump supply energy where it does not belong, taking out downstream circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-538" title="EEVblog #538 - HP35670A DSA Repair - Part 2">538</a></sup> Diodes also fail open, crack physically, develop intermittent internal connections that produce current-dependent latching behavior, and — in severe overheating events — can desolder themselves entirely and fall off the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1527" title="EEVblog 1527 - Toshiba T1000LE DOS Vintage Laptop Repair HELL">1527</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1087" title="EEVblog #1087 - UNBELIEVABLE Alesis Studio Monitor Fault!">1087</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1511" title="EEVblog 1511 - Solar Analytics System FAIL! (+ Dave gets ZAPPED!)">1511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WFVfbu1Xz3A" title="Solar Analytics 4G Solar Monitor TEARDOWN">•</a></sup> Thermal failure is a real possibility in high-voltage and power stages, and as a rule of thumb diodes tend to fail before the regulators they feed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1452" title="EEVblog 1452 - Fluke PM3370B Combiscope REPAIR">1452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1364" title="EEVblog #1364 - Compaq Portable PSU REPAIR">1364</a></sup> Because they are cheap, common suspects and usually testable in circuit, checking diodes early is standard troubleshooting practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1423" title="EEVblog 1423 - Flaming Magic Repair Smoke!">1423</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-663" title="EEVblog #663 - Compucorp 322G Calculator Teardown">663</a></sup>

## Design considerations

Diode selection has thermal and economic dimensions beyond the headline ratings. Junction temperature limits of +150 °C must be de-rated through the whole thermal stack — junction-to-case, vias, and insulating pad — and a [[heat-sink|heatsink]] already running at 160 °C leaves no margin at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Surge and continuous ratings differ by orders of magnitude, which is exactly what protection schemes exploit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup> For manufacturability, consolidating on one diode type — for example using the same 1 A power diode already on the [[bill-of-materials|bill of materials]] where a separate small signal diode would otherwise be specified — reduces line items, reels, and pick-and-place setup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup>
