RF shielding is the practice of enclosing circuitry in conductive barriers so that radio-[[frequency]] energy neither escapes a sensitive or noisy block nor enters it from outside. It is far-field [[shielding]]: the electric and magnetic fields separate close to a source and only combine into an electromagnetic field further away, and it is that combined far field that conventional RF shielding addresses.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1329" title="EEVblog #1329 - Magnetic Field Shielding DEMONSTRATED">1329</a></sup> In practice it governs both directions at once — keeping RF out of an instrument and keeping it in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup>

## Cans, blocks and partitioning

The commonest form is the metal can soldered or clipped over a section of board. On a consumer device the goal can be near-total coverage: a Kindle Paperwhite carried hardly any unshielded circuitry, with one chip on a flat flex board and a few passives outside, everything else under metal shielded cans, which heads off RFI at the source.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-370" title="EEVblog #370 - Kindle Paperwhite Teardown Review">370</a></sup> Front ends and transceivers in phones and handheld radios follow the same pattern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-243" title="EEVblog #243 - Vintage Brick Mobile Phone Teardown">243</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-492" title="EEVBlog #492 - Vintage Motorola MicroTAC Mobile Phone Teardown">492</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1275" title="EEVblog #1275 - Uniden UHF Walkie Talkie Teardowns">1275</a></sup>

Higher up the scale the can becomes a machined block. A 20 GHz spectrum analyser uses fully machined cans with RF shielding gaskets, with unrelated parts deliberately kept outside them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> Where the enclosure is soldered completely shut, the lid does not come off at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> Sheet-metal outer construction is often paired with an aluminium block inside carrying the RF shielding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup>

Partitioning inside a single enclosure follows the block diagram. A design is laid out in building blocks and the shield walls come down between them, penetrated only by a small slot for the trace crossing from one section to the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> The same construction appears in a VNA: signals pass between individual blocks that are all shielded, with [[solder-mask|solder mask]] removed or gold plating exposed where the wall meets the board, and slots machined out so the signal can pass while each section stays shielded from the next.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1101" title="EEVblog #1101 - Siglent SVA1015X VNA Teardown">1101</a></sup> Shielding can also be nested — an outer can over two more inner cans, one covering the sensor elements and their signal conditioning, with a copper tab and a metallised polyester strip carrying the shield up to the sensors themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup>

## Contact: fingers, gaskets, braid and tape

A shield is only as good as its bond to the rest of the chassis, so a large share of the engineering goes into contact hardware. Spring RF fingers running the length of a seam are a mark of serious EMC attention,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-790" title="EEVblog #790 - Lecroy Wavejet 354 Touch Oscilloscope Teardown">790</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1256" title="EEVblog #1256 - GORGEOUS Mystery Test Gear Teardown!">1256</a></sup> as are RF fingers on drive mechanisms inside a larger machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup> Compliant RFI gaskets sit between metal shields in spectrum analysers and similar instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-477" title="EEVblog #477 - Mailbag">477</a></sup>

Where the joint is long or irregular, braid and tape take over. Metal braid stuck to the metal of a case gives RF screening between the case and its outer cover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup> An ultrasound machine's boards are heavily shielded with RF braiding down their length; the signals emerge from the braid and contact the top of the metal front panel [[connector]], and a large metal plate presses against all of that braid so nothing escapes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JJwxFFpnt0M" title="ATL HDI3000 Ultrasound Machine">•</a></sup> Conductive tape does the same job at connector level, giving HDMI connectors a very low [[impedance]] RF path down to the main shield.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1396" title="EEVblog 1396 - BlackMagic ATEM Mini Extreme ISO TEARDOWN">1396</a></sup> Conductive sponge bridges a chassis to the metal backing plate of an [[lcd|LCD]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> Copper tape seals small access holes in a 20 GHz assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-470" title="EEVblog #470 - Agilent N9344C 20GHz Spectrum Analyser Teardown">470</a></sup> Board-to-board shield connections get gold pads to make the transition reliable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup> Rear-panel I/O gets its own treatment, with RFI shielding tabs at the ethernet and [[usb|USB]] jacks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-892" title="EEVblog #892 - Siglent SSA3021X Spectrum Analyser Teardown">892</a></sup> A die-cast case may itself provide much of the shielding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-571" title="EEVblog #571 - Sennheiser EW100 G3 Wireless Microphone Teardown">571</a></sup>

Because contact quality depends on mechanical detail, disturbing a shield is not free. Screw torque and gasket compression affect the shielding between sections, so removing RF cans on ultra-high-end gear can measurably shift low-level performance and may warrant returning the unit for [[calibration]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup>

## Shielding for low-level measurement

Shielding is not only about radiated emissions; it protects sensitive analogue measurement as well. In a 7½-digit [[multimeter]] the 3 A [[current]] range — the one that also carries the 10 µA full-scale range, with resolution down to about 1 pA — gets special treatment, with a cutout and its shield tied directly to a large shield strap running right around the section.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Legacy instrumentation used the same reasoning at board level: heavily shielded front-end, Doppler acquisition and channel boards in an ultrasound system,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1314" title="EEVblog #1314 - Ultrasound Machine Teardown!">1314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JJwxFFpnt0M" title="ATL HDI3000 Ultrasound Machine">•</a></sup> and a switching [[backlight]] board housed inside the same case as the switching supply to keep it away from the rest of the circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-261" title="EEVblog #261 - Marconi 2023 RF Signal Generator Teardown">261</a></sup>

## Where shielding is left out

Shielding is a cost and assembly burden, so it is frequently reduced or omitted. A mixed-domain [[oscilloscope]] shipped with no can at all on the front side of its RF front end, a much simpler approach than the more traditional spectrum-analyser-style construction of the higher-end model.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> A super-regenerative receiver module had no shielding whatsoever — no can, nothing in the case, with the [[antenna]] running straight out — which is the direct cause of its poor behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-767" title="EEVblog #767 - Super Regenerative Receiver Problems">767</a></sup> In hand-built work, not every box was given a lid, and performance remained fairly adequate without them, though unshielded sections pick up nearby oscillators such as the crystal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-575" title="EEVblog #575 - DIY 1970s Spectrum Analyser">575</a></sup>

Where shielding is present in quantity, it is visible mostly as mass and metal: extra brackets and plates whose purpose is not always obvious,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> full metal chassis work,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> and instruments that are heavy precisely because of how much shielding is inside.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup> Not every shield is a rigid can — a mesh RF shielded pad appears where a formed can would not fit,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup> and a piece of sheet can be soldered down and bent over to form a half can.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-951" title="EEVblog #951 - Mailbag">951</a></sup>

## Anechoic chambers

At the test-facility end, the same principles are applied to a room. An emissions anechoic chamber is a fully sealed metal room, and sealing is critical to its performance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup> A measured facility achieved about 80 dB of attenuation across the whole frequency range inside the room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup> Emissions from support equipment are eliminated rather than tolerated: the antenna mast is driven over a fibre-optic link so there are no emissions inside the chamber, and any auxiliary equipment that must be present is itself RF shielded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup> Sealing the room is a mechanical problem in the same way that sealing a can is — a drive-in door recessed below floor level cannot be sealed properly at the bottom without dedicated provisions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup> Semi-anechoic rooms, which have no absorber on the floor, are used for military work at field strengths up to around 200 V/m, with tests generally run at 1 m so the floor matters little.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup>
