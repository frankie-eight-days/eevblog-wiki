Firmware is the code that runs inside an embedded product's [[microcontroller]] or processor, as distinct from the PC-side application software that may talk to it. In modern test and measurement gear it is where most of the product's behaviour actually lives: the analogue front end and the precision parts set the limits, but the measurement modes, the user interface, the protection logic and the [[calibration]] handling are all code.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1463" title="EEVblog 1463 - Mailbag">1463</a></sup> The consequence is that two units with identical hardware can be different products, that a hardware fault and a firmware fault can look the same on the bench, and that a fix — or a new feature — can arrive as a download.

## Firmware as the product

In a Keysight [[battery]] emulator the local processor handles overcurrent and overvoltage protection, ESR emulation, constant-power and constant-[[current]] modes; the block diagram itself is fairly simple, and "All the magic's in the firmware" and in the precision parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> The same division shows up in an electric fence controller, where the pulse shaping, [[transformer]] design and cap charge-discharge cycles are supported by a large amount of intelligence in the firmware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1292" title="EEVblog #1292 - $2 Meter vs JVA Electric Fence Controller!">1292</a></sup> A JBC [[soldering]] station carries enough code to drive a dot-matrix [[lcd]] with its own font set over [[usb]]-updatable firmware, which is a great deal more code to lock up than a basic iron running an analogue control loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-472" title="EEVblog #472 - JBC CD-2BB Soldering Iron Teardown">472</a></sup> There is a defensible engineering position that a [[soldering-iron]] does not need firmware at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-400" title="EEVblog #400 - 400th Live Show">400</a></sup>

Firmware also determines characteristics that appear purely electrical. On a [[brymen-bm786]], older firmware switched the continuity beeper on at around 270 to 280 ohms and needed hysteresis out to about 300 ohms to release; revised firmware moved the threshold to just under 40 ohms with release at 100 ohms, and changed the pulse stretching so the buzzer went from strongly latching to intermittent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PSdF0KNsiaY" title="BM786 Continuity Firmware Testing">•</a></sup> On the BM2257, the only significant difference between release 2503 and the later 2506 is the continuity tone [[frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lZYvGxQttT4" title="BM2257 Firmware Difference">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1745" title="EEVblog 1745 - Continuity Tester Spectral Probing">1745</a></sup> On the 121GW, successive releases attacked ohms auto-ranging speed: 1.04 improved it, 1.05 improved it again, and version 2.00 changed the update rate on high-[[resistance]] readings.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uUCQuIp_hzU" title="121GW Ohms Autoranging Speed Update">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-q4gXnpFPFzQ" title="121GW Multimeter 2.00 Firmware">•</a></sup> A smart meter's residual [[temperature-coefficient]] — of the order of half a degree of error over a 50 °C span — is a candidate for firmware compensation using an ambient measurement rather than a hardware change.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-409" title="EEVblog #409 - EDMI - Smart Meter Teardown">409</a></sup>

Firmware equally governs [[power-consumption]]. Phone firmware revisions repeatedly improved standby current on a Nokia handset, which makes power a first-order design concern for embedded programmers rather than an afterthought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-80" title="EEVblog #80 - Nokia E71 + Garmin Mobile XT = Embedded Hell">80</a></sup> Measuring a product's consumption profile can require modifying the product's own firmware so that its mode changes are synchronised with the capture instrument's trigger.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1331" title="EEVblog #1331 - BattLab One Review">1331</a></sup>

## Field-updatable versus burned-in

A sealed, non-upgradable firmware image can be relied upon absolutely; an upgradable one raises the question of what latent defects it is expected to fix. As instrument complexity grows — [[data-logging]] in a handheld [[multimeter]] increases software complexity by roughly an order of magnitude — field upgrade becomes the reasonable choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15" title="EEVblog #15 Part 1 of 2 - Fluke 189/289 multimeter review">15</a></sup>

At the low end the choice is made by the silicon. A multimeter chipset with 6K words of one-time-programmable program memory and 256 bytes of data memory cannot be reflashed: a bug fix means scrapping programmed stock or shipping the old code, because only blank parts can be programmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-912" title="EEVblog #912 - BM235 Multimeter Reverse Engineering">912</a></sup> A socket tester's die is programmed after board assembly [[via]] pogo pins, which permits bug fixes at the production stage but never by the user.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1598" title="EEVblog 1598 - ANENG AC11 Socket Tester TEARDOWN + SCHEMATIC Reverse Eng">1598</a></sup> Some Brymen meters are reprogrammable only through a header inside the battery compartment, using a programmer released under NDA to dealers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-JI4b-7vpIDc" title="An Unusual Repair">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Calibration constants can survive this: on a BM786 they live in the protected region of the micro rather than an external [[eeprom]], so a [[firmware-update]] preserves them while physically replacing the chip does not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1520" title="EEVblog 1520 - Troubleshooting a Faulty BM786 Multimeter">1520</a></sup>

Deliberately unupdatable firmware is sometimes a security decision. A card-format hardware wallet has closed-source firmware that cannot be updated at all, which removes an attack surface at the cost of any later fix and of independent verification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dSek3p8gyv4" title="Tangem Open Source Crypto Bitcoin Hardware Wallet REVIEW">•</a></sup>

## Update mechanisms

The common paths are a USB mass-storage stick read by the instrument,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup> a microSD card prepared on a PC — the recovery route for a signal generator shipped with pre-release firmware that failed to boot,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-tM2j9zuBiRQ" title="Siglent Signal Generator Fix">•</a></sup> a vendor utility that downloads and pushes the image,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1608" title="EEVblog 1608 - Mailbag">1608</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup> a network fetch initiated from the device's own setup menu,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1151" title="EEVblog #1151 - Dumpster Dive Yamaha Receiver">1151</a></sup> and DFU — Device Firmware Upgrade — over USB using a bootloader already present in the part, as ST parts ship pre-programmed with one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1306" title="EEVblog #1306 (3 of 5) : How to program an STM32 using DFU Bootloader">1306</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1264" title="EEVblog #1264 - uSupply Software Development Setup">1264</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-633" title="EEVblog #633 - Mailbag">633</a></sup> The most frictionless variant enumerates as a drive so the hex file is simply dragged into a directory and the device renamed and reflashed it on the next power cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1335" title="EEVblog #1335 - Miniware $70 DT71 LCR Tweezer REVIEW">1335</a></sup> Bluetooth modules can be updated over the air from the phone app.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XOuYJ1nxVJQ" title="BM787BT Bluetooth CYBLE-212006-01 Fimware OTA Update">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Q_RYG_5cQk8" title="Brymen BM787BT BLE Module Replacement">•</a></sup>

Multi-board instruments complicate this. A USB [[oscilloscope]] carries separate ADC-board and controller firmware in different directories, and its updater failed to locate the image at all when run from the packaged distribution rather than from the Python source tree, because the relative path assumed a different depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> A bench scope's logic analyser pod and [[function-generator]] each carry their own firmware version separate from the scope firmware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Pods can be locked to the host by a serial EEPROM whose ID tells the firmware which module is plugged in, so a third-party pod would also have to reproduce that firmware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> Plug-in gas sensor modules use the same identifier trick to self-configure and recalibrate on insertion.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-603" title="EEVblog #603 - Gas Sensor Teardown - Dräger Multiwarn II">603</a></sup>

## Failure, lockup and recovery

Firmware defects in instruments are common enough to be a standard suspect. A scope that stops responding entirely to front-panel input is obviously firmware, and lockups in test gear are not excused by a low price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> Documented cases include horizontal-mode freezing,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup> time-base anomalies on a bench scope,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-797" title="EEVblog #797 - Siglent SDS1000X Oscilloscope Review">797</a></sup> a battery-gauge bug in a high-speed camera,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-947" title="EEVblog #947 - Chronos High Speed Camera Review">947</a></sup> and a multimeter that produced a spurious insertion error when the rotary switch passed through Off quickly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-d5CxW3VBoZU" title="BM235 Firmware Quirk">•</a></sup> A camcorder that continued to display a running record counter while writing nothing, because the SD card had been removed, is a firmware failure regardless of what the manual says — the correct behaviour is a warning or a lockout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #650 - Canon HF G30 Camcorder Review - FULL">2</a></sup>

Diagnostic firmware is frequently the missing piece. An intercom that fails its EEPROM access and then simply does nothing illustrates the point: well-designed firmware initialises the display first and then reports a fault code when a checksum or read-write test fails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-710" title="EEVblog #710 - Intercom System Repair">710</a></sup>

Firmware can also be destroyed in the field. A Fluke 87V could be bricked outright, its firmware erased, by a nearby GSM handset — near-field EM radiation on the LCD side would at minimum lock the meter up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-185" title="EEVblog #185 - Fluke 87V Multimeter GSM Fix!">185</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-112" title="EEVblog #112 - GSM vs The Fluke 87V Multimeter">112</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-EJ6KCpHc--4" title="Strange Brymen BM869 Resistance Quirk">•</a></sup> Locked-up firmware in a returned multimeter was cleared by reprogramming through the internal header, with nothing physically wrong with the unit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> A bricked 3D printer controller can resist recovery for reasons that have nothing to do with the target — AVR programmer, driver and toolchain problems rather than the hex file itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-274" title="EEVblog #274 - Makerbot Tweaking &amp; First Print">274</a></sup>

Vendor turnaround varies. A multimeter fault traced to firmware was investigated, acknowledged and fixed with a downloadable update within three weeks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-178" title="EEVblog #178 - Agilent&#x27;s U1272A Response">178</a></sup> An oscilloscope's sample-clock jitter attracted a beta firmware fix released for public trial before general release; that build did not fix the problem and introduced keyboard lockups, and was withdrawn before a working release followed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-699" title="EEVblog #699 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Jitter Fix Testing">699</a></sup>

## Feature gating

Because features are code, the same hardware is routinely sold at several price points with firmware as the differentiator. Module keys for a mixed-domain scope contain only a serial code that enables firmware already built into the instrument, an approach with the practical weakness that the physical keys can be stolen — a software licence key is preferable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-199" title="Tektronix Mixed Domain Oscilloscope MDO4000 Review - EEVblog #199">199</a></sup> A scope's 50 ohm input path may be physically present with 800 MHz of [[bandwidth]] while the software refuses to enable it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup> A Fluke 77 IV shares enough hardware with a 179 that temperature measurement is plausibly already present, with the firmware being what is actually paid for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup> Model identity itself can be soft: sending a serial command to change the reported model number causes the firmware to switch a 50 MHz scope into 100 MHz mode, and the underlying control lines can also be tied in hardware, so the mod survives a firmware countermeasure unless the board changes too.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-70" title="EEVblog #70 - Turn your Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope into a 100MHz DS1102E (Hack)">70</a></sup>

Gating also works in the user's favour. Firmware can disable autoscale on a teaching bench so students cannot use it while the instructor's back is turned.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> A version 2.0 release added arbitrary [[waveform]] capability to an existing scope's waveform generator option as a free download.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup> Shared firmware across a product line is a deliberate economy: a scope's UI is built to scale so the same image serves a 1,024 by 600 panel and the higher-resolution screens of upmarket models,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup> and a 7.5-digit meter derived from an existing platform needed only minimal firmware change with a team of four rather than twelve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 3 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

## Reverse engineering and modification

Firmware is a target. A 121GW user decompiled the binary, located the auto-ranging code and produced a modified image that measurably improved ohms range speed, closing most of the gap to reference-grade meters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3MD88EYLdQQ" title="121GW Hacked firmware test">•</a></sup> A pocket oscilloscope hobbled by its stock code was a candidate for rehabilitation by completely reverse-engineered replacement firmware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1260" title="EEVblog #1260 - $70 100MHz Oscilloscope?">1260</a></sup> An ARM dev board's firmware was rewritten from scratch after being reverse engineered, allowing arbitrary transmit frequencies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1207" title="EEVblog #1207 - ARM Dev Boards Falling From The Sky!">1207</a></sup> A discontinued speaker was freed from its vendor's software by replacing the board entirely, rather than being discarded for firmware incompatibility.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1519" title="EEVblog 1519 - FREE Your Sonos Speaker! (HACK)">1519</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1514" title="EEVblog 1514 - Sonos: The Juicero of Wifi Speakers? (TEARDOWN)">1514</a></sup>

Such attempts fail as often as they succeed. An 8.5-digit variant of a bench meter differs from the 7.5-digit model mainly in its [[voltage-reference]], suggesting a firmware-limited resolution; a ROM dump revealed no model-number string to flip and would require full disassembly of the 65C02 binary.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-615" title="EEVblog #615 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Followup">615</a></sup> A spectrum analyser board carries unpopulated footprints for an extra IF band-pass filter that an existing digital switch could select, which a software option in the firmware might still be able to enable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1109" title="EEVblog #1109 - Spectrum Analyser Design Walk-through">1109</a></sup> A thermal imager's Lepton sensor could in principle give absolute values under rewritten firmware, but without a calibration shutter the result would not be useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-669" title="EEVblog #669 - FLIR TG165 Thermal Imager Teardown">669</a></sup> Where the acquisition path is [[fpga]] rather than [[asic]], there is no fundamental obstacle to changing a behaviour in firmware or in the reconfigurable fabric itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1311" title="EEVblog #1311 - Can Your Oscilloscope Zoom OUT?">1311</a></sup>

## Security firmware

In hardware wallets the firmware is the security boundary. Signed firmware verified against a vendor private key means tampering erases the stored keys, so a programmer attached to the debug header yields nothing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1006" title="EEVblog #1006 - Trezor Bitcoin Hardware Wallet Teardown">1006</a></sup> A power-analysis side channel that recovered a private key was subsequently closed in firmware, leaving only ordinary periodic display-update and loop activity visible in the current trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1006" title="EEVblog #1006 - Trezor Bitcoin Hardware Wallet Teardown">1006</a></sup> One device ships with no firmware at all and requires the user to install it before first use.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-uOOGXORqsqQ" title="Trezor Safe 5 Bitcoin Crypo Hardware Wallet">•</a></sup> Where a secure element is used, its firmware may be closed even when the application and interface are open source, and the device relies on a per-unit secure ID rather than a firmware self-checksum to prove authenticity.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup> Open firmware permits community verification but is not the whole defence.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1048" title="EEVblog #1048 - Ledger Nano S Crypto Hardware Wallet">1048</a></sup>

Firmware can also be used to obscure. A safe lock's power signature was not exactly repeatable between identical entry attempts, consistent with deliberate randomisation in firmware to frustrate power-line attacks — a defence a well-designed product would be expected to include.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-771" title="EEVblog #771 - Electronic Safe Lock Powerline Attack Part 2">771</a></sup> Conversely, firmware protection is a legitimate way to protect a design from copying: putting the functionality in a microcontroller and blowing the read-protect fuses makes [[reverse-engineering]] difficult enough that copying the whole idea from scratch becomes the easier path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-106" title="EEVblog #106 - Top 5 Tips To Bring Your Product to Market (and why Patents are a BAD idea)">106</a></sup>

## Development and quality

Firmware quality means working reliably and meeting the stated requirements, and the scarce skill in industry is the engineer who understands both the software and the hardware.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> Normalising devices that misbehave until power-cycled is a failure of that standard.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-818" title="EEVblog #818 - Embedded Electronics With Jack Ganssle">818</a></sup> Getting embedded behaviour right can take many iterations — a single capacitive touch button on one consumer product went through 56 firmware versions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1211" title="EEVblog 1211 - Apple&#x27;s Most Beautiful Failure. The G4 Cube">1211</a></sup>

Firmware is one of the disciplines a full product design touches, alongside specification, enclosure, layout, user-interface design and environmental testing, and a one-person design role is the fastest way to acquire it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-302" title="EEVblog #302 - Electronics Beginner Advice">302</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-138" title="EEVblog #138 - Top 5 Engineering Graduate Tips">138</a></sup> For an electronics job application, bringing source code for personal projects is concrete evidence in a way a resume line is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27" title="EEVblog #27 - More Engineering Job Interview Tips!">27</a></sup> Under the open-source hardware definition, the release must include the firmware needed to make the hardware work, not just schematics and Gerbers — hence the proposal for a badge whose letters mark which of [[schematic]], [[pcb]], firmware, mechanical CAD and documentation have actually been released.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-195" title="Open Source Hardware Explained - EEVblog #195">195</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-921" title="EEVblog #921 - Open Source Hardware Problems Solved!">921</a></sup>

Design choices reduce firmware work. USB Power Delivery negotiation once had to be written from scratch; single-chip solutions now arrive pre-programmed and pin-strapped for the desired power level, which is a reason to avoid multiple isolated processors in a redesign.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1561" title="EEVblog 1561 - µSupply USB Power Supply - Part 21">1561</a></sup> Conversely, some capabilities cannot be moved into firmware at all: sample compression in a logic analyser requires the hardware capability to exist, normally in an FPGA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-290" title="EEVblog #290 - Mailbag">290</a></sup>

## Manufacturing and provenance

Programmed parts create supply-chain failure modes. Chips arriving on the reel pre-programmed with another company's firmware — in one case an unidentified Motorola image — point either to grey-market parts recovered and re-reeled, or to a manufacturer's own mis-programmed stock being returned to inventory and sold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-134" title="EEVblog #134 - The Maxim Manipulation">134</a></sup> Production programming is visible on the board itself: a marker cross on a flash device is an operator's record that the part has been programmed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup> Kits and modules commonly ship pre-flashed, with the customisation done from a desktop application rather than a toolchain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-464" title="EEVblog #464 - Mailbag">464</a></sup>

Firmware version mismatch is a hazard in multi-node systems. Battery modules bought at different times carried different firmware, and without a gateway to update them, the mismatch was the suspected cause of one module repeatedly shutting itself down safely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-aANZNwmnC_4" title="AERL Lithium Home Storage Battery - Cloud Data">•</a></sup> Legacy equipment shows the opposite problem: a note acceptor with 2002-vintage firmware cannot recognise a banknote series introduced in 2006, and reflashing requires pulling the parallel flash and programming it externally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-525" title="EEVblog #525 - Bank Note Acceptor Teardown">525</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-526" title="EEVblog #526 - Bank Note Acceptor Followup">526</a></sup> As a general bench practice, checking for and applying available firmware on any acquired equipment is worth doing as a matter of course.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1615" title="EEVblog 1615 - Dumpster Dive HP Pro One PC">1615</a></sup>
