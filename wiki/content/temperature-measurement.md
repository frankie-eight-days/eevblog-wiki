Temperature measurement is the routine bench practice of determining how hot a component, assembly, or environment is, and it underpins most thermal design verification, fault-finding, and product characterisation work in electronics.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> It divides into contact methods, dominated by the K-type [[thermocouple]] plugged into a [[multimeter]], and non-contact methods, dominated by infrared thermometers and thermal cameras.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Both are easy to perform badly: a reading is only as good as the thermal coupling, the emissivity setting, and the mechanical state of the object under test at the moment the number is taken.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

## Thermocouples and multimeter temperature functions

A K-type thermocouple probe is little more than a wire sensor on the end of a lead; it is cheap, simple, and plugs directly into any meter with a temperature function.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup> Its tip measures the temperature of whatever it touches — air, metal, [[heat-sink|heat sink]] surfaces, or liquids — and the measurement range extends to hundreds of degrees.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup> Meters generally display in both Celsius and Fahrenheit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1636" title="EEVblog 1636 - Ultimate Multimeter Beginners Guide!">1636</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-534" title="EEVblog #534 - Mailbag">534</a></sup>

Resolution and accuracy are separate specifications and should not be confused. A mid-range handheld may offer 0.1 °C [[display-resolution|display resolution]] while specifying accuracy of only about ±1 °C, which is still useful because the fine resolution supports relative work: a delta temperature mode nulls out the starting reading so that only the change is displayed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-249" title="EEVblog #249 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Review">249</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> Doing temperature properly inside a meter requires cold junction compensation on the thermocouple input, along with a dedicated offset trim pot for the temperature channel — a level of investment that is unusual in a handheld and marks out a meter that treats temperature as a first-class function rather than an afterthought.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-853" title="EEVblog #853 - How A Multimeter Works">853</a></sup>

Some meters expose an internal [[temperature-sensor|temperature sensor]] rather than an external probe.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup> That sensor is typically a calibrated PN junction on the [[pcb|PCB]], and in some designs may be inside the [[processor]] itself, since on-die temperature sensors are common in microcontrollers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Reading it is a practical way to track the internal temperature of the instrument itself, for example when cycling a meter between hot and cold to chase a thermally dependent fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-mkZFHEeQkQI" title="Unusual Repair - Part 2 - Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup> Bench instruments expose the equivalent: a 6½-digit DMM can report internal or [[ambient-temperature|ambient temperature]] directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-489" title="EEVblog #489 - Agilent 34461A Multimeter Review">489</a></sup>

## Temperature as a multimeter buying criterion

Temperature capability is treated as a significant feature in meter selection rather than a novelty, and its absence is a real deduction.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> A meter that is otherwise excellent but cannot measure temperature is simply unsuitable for anyone who needs temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-99" title="EEVblog #99 - $100 Multimeter Shootout - Extech Amprobe BK Precision Ideal UEi Uni-T PART 1of2">99</a></sup> On a meter approaching US$90, omitting temperature while retaining a [[transistor]] tester is judged an oversight severe enough to change the overall verdict on the product.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1378" title="EEVblog 1378 - NEW Uni-T UT61E+ Multimeter - Still bang-per-buck king?">1378</a></sup> Fluke's product lines have historically been differentiated on exactly this axis, with the temperature function among the few things separating adjacent models.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-64" title="EEVblog #64 - Fluke 28 Series II Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">64</a></sup> In at least one case the temperature hardware is present and only the [[firmware]] distinguishes the cheaper model from the dearer one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1448" title="EEVblog 1448 - Convert a Fluke 77 IV to True RMS for 10 CENTS!*">1448</a></sup>

A recurring limitation is that meter temperature inputs are single channel, when differential and multi-point measurements are frequently what is actually needed; two or three blade-type thermocouple inputs would address this.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup> Where a probe is not supplied with the meter, the user has to source one separately.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-75" title="EEVblog #75 - Digital Multimeter Buying Guide for Beginners">75</a></sup>

## Infrared and thermal imaging

Non-contact instruments give a fast survey of a whole board, but every reading is conditioned on emissivity. Bright or unfinished metal is the classic problem: an un-anodised [[transformer]] reads cool and silver heat sinks read low unless emissivity is set for the surface.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> Shiny surfaces also reflect, and the operator's own hand can appear in the image as heat reflected off aluminium [[capacitor]] cans, so thermal work requires care that reflected energy is not being mistaken for the object's own emission.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> One remedy is to paint the surface of interest black.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> Where the material is not in the instrument's preset list, emissivity can be found experimentally by adjusting the setting until the image agrees with a known-good reference — for example a [[soldering-tip|soldering iron tip]] whose temperature has already been established by other means, which in one case settled at around 0.35 to give 246 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup>

A [[thermal-camera|thermal camera]]'s on-screen temperature figure is typically the maximum across every pixel in the frame, not the temperature of any particular part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup> It is therefore excellent for spotting hot spots — a display ranging from 23.8 °C room temperature up to nearly 70 °C localises a fault immediately — but identifying the temperature of a specific device still means reading individual points.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Fusion or MSX-style overlays help by superimposing visible-light outlines so that individual chips can be identified in the thermal image.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-593" title="EEVblog #593 - HP35670A DSA Repair Part 4 - Thermal Testing">593</a></sup>

## The lid-off trap

The most common mistake in thermal performance measurement is testing with the lid off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> In any product with forced-air cooling, removing the enclosure destroys the [[airflow]] path the design depends on, so the temperatures observed are not the true temperatures of the devices in service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Airflow direction and the position of vents matter concretely: with the case open, air forced in at the rear flows out over regions that would ordinarily be cool and exits at the wrong point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Blocking one of two exhaust vents changes the direction of heat spreading across the board and lowers the maximum-to-minimum spread in the image.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Ballpark lid-off readings remain useful for orientation, but they are explicitly not valid characterisation data.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup>

## Making the measurement trustworthy

Getting a genuinely accurate number is laborious. Characterising a thermoelectric generator properly requires correctly coupled probes on both the hot and cold sides, and a quick ballpark setup will not do it; a full day of setup may precede any decently accurate measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-664" title="EEVblog #664 - Peltier TEG Energy Harvesting Experiments">664</a></sup> The same ambiguity appears with [[soldering]] irons, where the tip temperature, the internal sensor reading, and an external thermocouple all disagree, and the tip-to-sensor thermocouple coupling is itself part of the problem.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> The practical answer is that the tip is what matters, but the coupling still has to be accounted for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1646" title="EEVblog 1646 - iFixIt FixHub Portable USB Soldering Station REVIEW">1646</a></sup> Instruments should also be given time to settle; an hour of operation is a reasonable interval before assuming a chassis has thermally equalised.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zoyaHOqp9gI" title="Rigol DHO800 Oscilloscope Fan Upgrade Experiment">•</a></sup>

Sensor placement is equally decisive in products themselves. A [[power-supply|power supply]] that reports a temperature but does not place its thermocouple on the heat sink is measuring the wrong thing, a cost-saving compromise that undermines the reading it presents to the user.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup> The contrary example is a [[battery]] emulator that instruments the temperature of each output [[connector]] individually, with a sensor bolted through a solid brass block.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1550" title="EEVblog 1550 - Keysight E36731A Battery Emulator Teardown">1550</a></sup> Attempting four significant digits of temperature in a data logger is ambitious given how hard the underlying measurement is.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1686" title="EEVblog 1686 - Electronex 2025 Melbourne - Walkaround">1686</a></sup>

## Diagnostic and characterisation uses

Comparative thermal measurement is a standard technique for locating a short. Where a rail is shorted and the bypass capacitors have been eliminated, measuring the remaining candidate devices and looking for the outlier is the obvious next step — but the technique fails when all of the candidates are faulty. Four ASICs on a 3.3 V rail measured at essentially the same temperature, and only a 3 to 4 °C difference between one chip and the others, which was far too small to be explained by the extra dissipation of a single short, and which ultimately indicated that every one of them was shorted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-405" title="EEVblog #405 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 3">405</a></sup>

For thermal characterisation, the meaningful figure is usually the differential above ambient rather than the absolute reading. An [[oscilloscope]] running overnight settled at 39.4 °C against a lab ambient near 24 °C, a 15 to 16 °C rise, at a roughly constant 35 to 36 W regardless of channel count or processing load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pwS6HnM9PDo" title="The Rigol DHO800 Fan(boys) Go Wild">•</a></sup> Reducing that instrument's fan drive from 8 V to 5 V cost only about 2 to 2.5 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zoyaHOqp9gI" title="Rigol DHO800 Oscilloscope Fan Upgrade Experiment">•</a></sup> A linear supply's output transistor peaked at just over 100 °C under a worst-case short-circuit dissipation test, while a breadboarded regulator at 1 A settled at about 43.5 °C on the device case and 41.5 °C on the heat sink against 28.5 to 29 °C ambient.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-224" title="EEVblog #224 - Lab Power Supply Design - Part 3">224</a></sup> Instruments may also protect themselves, warning that internal temperature is approaching a damaging point and shutting down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup>

Temperature is also a useful auxiliary axis in [[efficiency]] work: attaching a thermocouple to a converter or its heat sink allows temperature to be plotted against efficiency, output, and load dissipation, which exposes designs that behave correctly at lab ambient but not elsewhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-957" title="EEVblog #957 - How To Measure DC-DC Converter Efficiency">957</a></sup> Determining die temperature is a recognised technique in its own right.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup>

## Dedicated sensors and where temperature is the wrong measurand

Beyond thermocouples, a range of dedicated devices appear in equipment: thermistors driving [[pwm|PWM]] fan controllers with configurable low, high, and alarm thresholds, ramping fan duty from 10 % to 100 % linearly between them; the DS18B20 one-wire digital thermometer; and combined temperature and humidity parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src--9l41zNWlxY" title="Guest Video: Voltlog #142 - Mailbag">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-f_SdM6sXHD4" title="FNIRSI SG-003A Process Meter + DSO-TC2 Oscilloscope &amp; Transistor Tester">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-377" title="EEVblog #377 - Mailbag Monday">377</a></sup> Data loggers built around a small [[microcontroller]] commonly cover −40 to 125 °C, storing readings as 8- or 10-bit values.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-294" title="EEVblog #294 - Mailbag">294</a></sup> Radiosondes carried aloft under balloons measure temperature and barometric pressure with an external probe, since the Styrofoam housing that keeps the electronics warm would otherwise isolate the sensor from the air it is meant to measure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-970" title="EEVblog #970 - Mailbag">970</a></sup>

Occasionally the thermal effect of interest is too small to measure directly. Mobile phone SAR testing is nominally about how much a tissue-simulant liquid is heated, but the temperature changes involved are too small for accurate measurement, so the E-field is measured instead and SAR is computed from the dielectric parameters of the liquid.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-201" title="Mobile Cell Phone Radiation SAR Testing - EEVblog #201">201</a></sup>
