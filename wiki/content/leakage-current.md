Leakage current is the small unwanted [[current]] that flows through a component or along an insulating path that is nominally blocking — a reverse-biased [[diode]], an off [[mosfet|MOSFET]], a [[capacitor]] dielectric, or the surface of a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] between two adjacent pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup> It is never zero, and whether it matters depends entirely on what it is being compared against: a few microamps is negligible next to an amp, and fatal next to a picoamp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Most of the design effort spent on leakage is therefore not about eliminating it but about pushing it an order of magnitude or more below the smallest current the circuit is meant to resolve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-286" title="EEVblog #286 - Orders Of Magnitude">286</a></sup>

## Reverse leakage in diodes

A reverse-biased diode passes a non-zero reverse current, and that current rises with applied reverse [[voltage]] rather than staying flat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Schottky diodes are markedly worse than PN junctions in this respect: the extra metal layer of the Schottky barrier construction buys a low forward drop at the cost of roughly three orders of magnitude more reverse leakage than an ordinary jellybean PN junction part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> A representative small-signal Schottky measured 2.4 µA of reverse current at 5 V, while the equivalent PN junction device sits in the nanoamp region under the same conditions.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> A 1N4148 leaks on the order of tens of nanoamps.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

This makes the choice application-dependent rather than absolute. A Schottky is the right part for reverse-polarity or reverse-clamp protection, where the low forward drop is the point and the leakage is irrelevant, because a PN junction's 0.6 V drop can be enough to forward-bias junctions in the circuit being protected.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> It is the wrong part wherever the blocked node is high [[impedance]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

## How leakage becomes a visible error

The classic symptom is a voltage that should not be there. A reverse-biased diode feeding a node loaded only by a [[multimeter]]'s 10 MΩ input forms a divider: 10 nA of leakage across 10 MΩ produces 0.1 V, which is why such a measurement reads close to zero rather than exactly zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> With microamps of Schottky leakage instead of nanoamps, the same arrangement can read the full rail voltage on a node whose supply is disconnected, and the effect is entirely the leakage combined with the meter's internal [[resistance]] rather than a fault.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup> Bench multimeters with gigaohm [[input-impedance|input impedance]] on their lower ranges make the problem worse, not better, because they load the leaking node even less.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1747" title="EEVblog 1747 - Schottky vs PN Diodes &amp; Measurement Traps">1747</a></sup>

The same mechanism appears in precision analog circuits. In a peak detector, when the output drops low the storage diode is reverse biased into what is effectively a path to [[ground]], and its leakage can exceed the [[input-bias-current|input bias current]] of a FET-input [[op-amp|op amp]] — the diode, not the amplifier, sets the droop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> The standard fix is a two-diode arrangement that feeds the peak output back so that the voltage across the offending diode becomes zero; with zero volts across it there is no leakage current through it at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup>

In a [[battery]] gauge built around an LM3914, the leakage current of the [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] itself has to be carried explicitly as an error term in the [[resistor]] calculation, and the value comes from the [[datasheet|data sheet]] — 120 µA maximum from the adjust terminal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-204" title="Designing a Li-Ion Battery Gauge with the LM3914 - EEVblog #204">204</a></sup>

## Guarding and PCB construction

Where currents of interest fall below the microamp level, the dominant leakage path is the board itself, and the countermeasure is guarding: a trace or ring held at the same potential as the sensitive node, placed so that it surrounds and intercepts every path leading away from it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> Because there is no voltage differential between the node and the guard, Ohm's law leaves no current to flow — the leakage that does exist flows from the guard driver rather than from the measurement node.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> Guard rings appear routinely around individual pins of analog multiplexers and high-impedance amplifier inputs in precision instruments.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-485" title="EEVblog #485 - Agilent TrueVolt 34461A Multimeter Teardown">485</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1382" title="EEVblog 1382 - Keysight EDU34450A 5.5 digit Bench Multimeter TEARDOWN">1382</a></sup> Supporting details include vias arranged to block cross-contamination through the board, guard traces routed underneath the sensitive resistors and capacitors, and [[solder-mask|solder mask]] deliberately removed around the guarded pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1334" title="EEVblog #1334 - Mystery Dumpster Teardown">1334</a></sup> Isolation slots are cut under the FET switches that reset the integrating capacitors of a multi-slope converter, where any leakage would corrupt the charge and discharge timing the conversion depends on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup>

At the [[connector]], the same idea becomes the three-terminal triaxial cable: chassis or [[mains-earth|mains earth]] on the outside, a driven guard terminal inside it, and the signal at the centre, which eliminates leakage and interference paths into an electrometer front end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Instruments resolving down to 10 fA require this construction rather than ordinary [[coaxial-cable|coax]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Guarding, [[shielding]], and wiring layout account for much of the internal complexity of classic high-resistance instruments, where measuring megohm, gigohm, and teraohm resistors was otherwise not practical.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1476" title="EEVblog 1476 - Keithley 515A Wheatstone Bridge TEARDOWN &amp; TUTORIAL">1476</a></sup>

Exposed traces with solder mask stripped away are not always a leakage measure, and the interpretation depends on where they sit in the circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-559" title="EEVblog #559 - Auction Score">559</a></sup>

## Leakage as a device parameter

Leakage is a specified characteristic across component families and is frequently the axis on which two otherwise similar parts differ:

- **MOSFETs** — drain-to-source leakage in a small jellybean part runs to a maximum of about 1 µA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1736" title="EEVblog 1736 - TOP 5 Jellybean MOSFET&#x27;s">1736</a></sup>
- **Logic families** — the 4000 series has better leakage behaviour than 74HC-class parts, which trade it away for lower on-resistance and higher [[bandwidth]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1611" title="EEVblog 1611 - Top 5 Jellybean 7400 &amp; 4000 Logic">1611</a></sup>
- **Tantalum capacitors** — very low leakage is one of their genuine advantages, alongside good reliability when operated inside their specifications.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33" title="EEVblog #33 1of2 - Capacitor Tutorial (Electrolytic, Tantalum, &amp; Plastic Film)">33</a></sup>
- **ESD protection networks** — leakage is a first-order design consideration when series resistors, avalanche diodes, and capacitors are added ahead of instrumentation amplifier inputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1619" title="EEVblog 1619 - Mailbag: Featuring Don McKenzie">1619</a></sup>

Diode curves showing leakage current alongside temperature stability and the breakdown region are standard textbook material for characterising the device.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1270" title="EEVblog #1270 - Electronics Textbook Shootout">1270</a></sup>

## Mains-frequency and high-voltage leakage

At mains potentials, leakage is a safety and power-measurement issue rather than a precision one. A [[soldering-iron|soldering iron]] whose start button measured 85 V AC to protective earth, accompanied by a tingling sensation when the knob was held, points to leakage through a filter capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1291" title="EEVblog #1291 - Mailbag">1291</a></sup> Distributed [[capacitance]] is enough to draw measurable current with no load connected at all: 283 double adapters chained in series drew 229 µA at 241.3 V in, amounting to 54 mW of leakage from the capacitance alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1526" title="EEVblog 1526 - I put 283 Double Adapters in Series with a 2kW Load!">1526</a></sup>

[[input-protection|Input protection]] components can also leak in a way that degrades a meter's own specification. A multimeter using three MOVs in series across its 10 MΩ input has a leakage path through the varistors that, from the data sheet, was predicted to pull the effective input impedance down to about 9.5 MΩ and raise the current to roughly 105 µA at 1000 V.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup> Measurement showed better than predicted behaviour — the input stayed at effectively 10 MΩ, measured as 10.02 MΩ, all the way to 1200 V, so the MOV selection leaves leakage a non-issue across the meter's full range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-rT0g1QmKE5E" title="Brymen BM2257 MOV Leakage Testing">•</a></sup>

Leakage is also used deliberately. Displays driven from high-voltage rails can pre-bias unselected outputs through discrete diodes to a chosen supply, preventing digits from faintly glowing on leakage currents alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> Measuring that leakage directly on a [[nixie-tube|Nixie tube]] — all unselected pins shorted together and clamped by a 30 V Zener, against 1.6 mA flowing in the selected segment through a 22 kΩ resistor — gave about 330 µA total, low enough not to disturb operation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-950" title="EEVblog #950 - Nixie Tube Display Project - Part 2">950</a></sup> In [[alkaline-battery|alkaline battery]] discharge testing, a 100 kΩ resistor placed in parallel with each cell supplies a deliberate 10 µA to emulate the leakage current a real product would impose.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-hSkaZEgrZkY" title="Alkaline Battery Discharge Testing Part 3">•</a></sup>

## Measuring it

Very small leakage currents are often better expressed as conductance than resistance. Multimeters with a nanosiemens range, reached on some models by pressing the range button twice while on ohms, read high-resistance leakage paths directly; converting back to ohms requires inverting the reading.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-372" title="EEVblog #372 - Fluke 27 Multimeter Review &amp; Teardown">372</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup> Conductance in siemens is the conventional unit for the very high resistances found in dielectrics and PCB materials, and in materials science generally.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1728" title="EEVblog 1728 - AC Basics Tutorial Part 6: Impedance, Conductance, Susceptance, Admittance">1728</a></sup>

Isolating a leaking node also matters when storing charge. A [[supercapacitor]] or battery backing a low-power circuit must be separated from the main supply rail by a blocking diode so that no reverse current flows back into the powered-down circuitry, and the diode's own leakage — tens of nanoamps for a 1N4148 — sets the floor on how well that isolation works.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1242" title="EEVblog #1242 - Memory LCD+Supercaps+Low Power Design">1242</a></sup>

Leakage is not confined to conduction through components. In an electrostatic system where the attractive force is electrostatic and no conduction current is intended in theory, leakage and capacitive charging currents are still present in practice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1149" title="EEVblog #1149 - How An Electrostatic Whiteboard Works">1149</a></sup> Keeping [[noise]] and leakage out of the analog section is a general construction requirement in precision resistance standards and calibrators, alongside the type and construction of the reference resistors themselves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-544" title="EEVblog #544 - Fluke 5450A Resistance Calibrator Teardown">544</a></sup>
