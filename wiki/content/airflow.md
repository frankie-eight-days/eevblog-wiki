Airflow — the movement of air over heat-dissipating surfaces, whether driven by a fan, by natural convection through vents, or by an external source such as a vehicle's motion — is one of the primary variables in electronics thermal design. The [[thermal-resistance]] of a [[heat-sink|heatsink]] is not a fixed number but a function of the air moving across it, so a cooling solution that is adequate under forced airflow can fail outright in still air.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Airflow path design inside an enclosure determines which components get cooled and which are left to cook, and the presence or absence of realistic airflow also determines whether a bench temperature measurement means anything at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup>

## Role in thermal design

Thermal design must account for the airflow over a heatsink, the enclosure type, and the available ventilation, with the goal of keeping the semiconductor junction at a safe and reliable temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> The magnitude of the effect is large: a heatsink dissipating 10 W that stabilises at 76.3 °C in still air drops to 35.7 °C under fan-forced airflow of roughly 10 km/h, with the corresponding device case temperature falling from about 87 °C to 47 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> A small regulator heatsink measured at 161 °C in free air ran at 56 °C once airflow was applied, and the same converter could not deliver its rated [[current]] even momentarily without airflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Even the modest air movement from room air conditioning can make a significant difference to component temperatures.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup>

Components rated with airflow assumed will fail without it. [[led]] lamps installed in sealed "no airflow" light fittings heat up and burn out within days, regardless of heatsinking, because fins accomplish nothing without air moving over them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-735" title="EEVblog #735 - Mailbag">735</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup> Electrolytic capacitors rated at 105 °C still have a finite lifetime that is eroded when airflow is absent and thermals are poor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GbADsyp9wM8" title="Failed Lucci Constant Current LED Driver Teardown">•</a></sup> At the board level, a [[resistor]] run at its quarter-watt limit needs adequate airflow and must not be mounted beside an [[electrolytic-capacitor]], whose life is derated by the neighbouring heat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-191" title="EEVblog #191 - Mouse Trap Triggering">191</a></sup>

## Quantifying airflow and heatsink performance

Heatsink datasheets characterise thermal performance as a graph of heatsink-to-ambient thermal resistance (°C/W) against airflow, expressed in linear feet per minute (LFM). A typical rating point of 5 mph corresponds to about 440 LFM, which on one extrusion reads back to a thermal resistance of roughly 8 °C/W; a measured assembly achieving about 4 °C/W against a [[datasheet]] figure of 15 °C/W illustrates how much the surrounding design matters relative to the tabulated value.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-895" title="EEVblog #895 - BEC Pro Model Airplane Regulator Testing">895</a></sup> Airflow itself is measured with an anemometer, and the reading may need converting — for example from km/h to feet per minute — before it can be applied to the performance graph.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-105" title="EEVblog #105 - Electronics Thermal Heatsink Design Tutorial">105</a></sup> Fan throughput is also expressed in CFM (cubic feet per minute).<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4oHUuXgK_i4" title="Soldering Fume Extractor Fan Upgrade">•</a></sup> In comparative fan testing, standing the unit vertically and resting the anemometer on the exhaust gives a repeatable figure; one swap moved exhaust velocity from 3.3–3.5 km/h down to 2.7 km/h, and fan noise tracks airflow fairly directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zoyaHOqp9gI" title="Rigol DHO800 Oscilloscope Fan Upgrade Experiment">•</a></sup>

## Enclosure and airflow-path design

Good enclosure design establishes a deliberate path from intake to exhaust that carries air across the heat-generating parts. Common practice is air in through side or front vents, over the [[transformer]] and heatsinks, and out through a rear fan.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-511" title="EEVblog #511 - Rigol DP832 Power Supply Teardown">511</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1038" title="EEVblog #1038 - Uni-T UPO2104CS Oscilloscope Teardown">1038</a></sup> Ducting, lips, and surrounds around vents can force the full air volume across a [[power-supply]] rather than letting it escape sideways, though added ducting also adds air [[resistance]] and reduces volumetric [[efficiency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> Generous internal volume aids airflow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-674" title="EEVblog #674 - Rigol DS1054Z Teardown">674</a></sup>

Several failure modes recur:

- **Blocked paths.** A fan aimed straight into a shielding can so that air reflects off and exits without crossing the power supply or active circuitry is a design failure, as is an airflow path that must turn sharply around staggered heatsinks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1434" title="EEVblog 1434 - Cadex C7000 Battery Analyser Teardown">1434</a></sup> Boards mounted broadside to the incoming air dam the flow and starve downstream heatsinks; they should be oriented edge-on to the stream.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-946" title="EEVblog #946 - Apple (Raspberry) Pi Cluster - PART 2">946</a></sup> An add-in card can similarly wreck a designed airflow path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1053" title="EEVblog #1053 - The Biggest 80&#x27;s Computer FAIL - IBM PC Jr">1053</a></sup>
- **Fin orientation.** Heatsink fins must align with the airflow so air passes through them; fins at the wrong angle block it. This is a "trap for young players" and fin direction is deliberately chosen in competent designs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1761" title="EEVblog 1761 - Micsig MHO14 Tablet Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1761</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup>
- **Series heating.** Where two heatsinks sit in series in the same airstream, the downstream device receives pre-heated air and runs hotter than the upstream one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-726" title="EEVblog #726 - Dual Xeon Video Editing Machine Build">726</a></sup>
- **Dead air.** A densely packed heatsink assembly may pass almost no air from one end to the other even with system fans present.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-917" title="EEVblog #917 - Mailbag">917</a></sup>

Fans carry an airflow-direction marker that must be observed when fitting them so the flow matches the case's front-to-back path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-462" title="EEVblog #462 - Video Editing PC Assembly">462</a></sup> Larger fans move a greater volume of air at lower RPM and are therefore quieter for the same cooling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup> Running a fan below its rated [[voltage]] — 8 V on a 12 V part, for instance — is a deliberate trade of airflow for noise where full throughput is not needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pwS6HnM9PDo" title="The Rigol DHO800 Fan(boys) Go Wild">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zoyaHOqp9gI" title="Rigol DHO800 Oscilloscope Fan Upgrade Experiment">•</a></sup> Vertical mounting that intakes at the bottom and exhausts warm air at the top works with natural convection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-u22duJf4n1Q" title="Keysight P9336A USB AWG Teardown">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup> Fanless designs substitute generous grills and slots and rely on ambient cabinet airflow from neighbouring equipment.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1177" title="EEVblog #1177 - Ericsson PABX Teardown">1177</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup> Airflow leaves forensic traces: dust accumulates at the end of a power supply where air is forced in, and soot deposits can map the flow path through a chassis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-217" title="EEVblog #217 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Teardown">217</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup>

## Airflow in thermal measurement

Temperature measurements taken with the enclosure open are not representative, because removing the lid destroys the designed airflow over heatsinks and devices; components read hotter than they would in service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup> Realistic figures have been observed to be at least 10 °C lower once the fan and airflow are restored, and heatsinks measured in free air at 80–88 °C fell once the lid was replaced to re-establish the air path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> Thermal validation must also be done at the full specified ambient — a product rated to 50 °C cannot be signed off at a 22 °C lab bench, because the required airflow may not suffice at the extreme.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-PcxEO3fA_Ls" title="Tektronix 2 Series Discussion with Andy Tedd from Tektronix">•</a></sup>

Because cling wrap is transparent at thermal-infrared wavelengths, wrapping an open chassis restores something close to the lid-on airflow while still allowing a thermal camera to image the board — and under favourable conditions the airflow itself becomes visible in the image as warmed air streams across cooler regions toward the exit vent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-622" title="EEVblog #622 - How To See Through Objects With A Thermal Camera">622</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-623" title="EEVblog #623 - See Through Thermal Camera Followup">623</a></sup>

## Airflow as a disturbance

Airflow is not always desirable. Precision circuitry is deliberately shielded from it: a plastic cover over a [[multimeter]]'s [[voltage-reference]] and ADC exists not for electrical shielding but to keep fan-driven air currents from wafting over the reference and disturbing its thermal equilibrium.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> Conversely, an earlier [[oscilloscope]] revision that fully enclosed a heatsink inside a screening can was improved by cutting an opening so airflow could reach the finned top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1146" title="EEVblog #1146 - New Rigol MSO5000 Oscilloscope">1146</a></sup>

## Other contexts

Fume extraction depends on airflow volume and capture geometry: folded HEPA media maximises surface area for the airflow, and a well-shaped intake can capture fume effectively from 100 mm away even at the lowest fan setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1616" title="EEVblog 1616 - Weller Zero Smog Fume Extractor Review">1616</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-yi5pb_l8U94" title="Weller Zero Smoke EL Fume Extractor - Part 2">•</a></sup> Compact extractors trade nozzle size and total airflow for convenience.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1338" title="EEVblog #1338 - Rubber Coated Mailbag">1338</a></sup> [[solar-panel]] output depends on airflow across the panels as well as insolation and ambient temperature, since airflow sets the cell operating temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-BtQUuD6QRMw" title="Solar Panel Temperature Dependency">•</a></sup> Atmospheric-water devices use a fan — or the motion of a bicycle — to drive airflow over a Peltier-cooled condenser.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-881" title="EEVblog #881 - Fontus Self Filling Water Bottle BUSTED!">881</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1454" title="EEVblog 1454 - Water from Air AGAIN! - The Kara Pure">1454</a></sup> Air-moving sensors such as laser particulate counters draw a measured sample stream through the sensing chamber with a small internal fan.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1049" title="EEVblog #1049 - Mailbag">1049</a></sup>
