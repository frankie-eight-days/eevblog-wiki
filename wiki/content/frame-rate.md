Frame rate is the number of complete images a camera, display, or capture system produces per second. It is the parameter that decides whether an imaging system can be used for live work — [[soldering]] under a microscope, driving a vehicle from a remote feed, catching a fast transient — or only for still documentation.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-817" title="EEVblog #817 - Mantis Elite Cam Inspection Microscope">817</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-886" title="EEVblog #886 - Audi Lunar Quattro Moon Rover">886</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-42" title="EEVblog #42 - Exploding Capacitors in High Speed">42</a></sup> In practice it is almost never a free choice: it trades against resolution, against available light, against the [[bandwidth]] of the link carrying the data, and against the processing time needed to encode the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-566" title="EEVblog #566 - Cheap USB Microscope Reviews">566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup>

## The trade against resolution

For [[usb]] imaging devices the frame rate is set by the selected image size, and the headline figure in the specification generally applies only at the smallest one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-566" title="EEVblog #566 - Cheap USB Microscope Reviews">566</a></sup> A cheap USB microscope claiming 30 frames per second delivers that only at low resolutions; raised to 1280x960 the image begins to lag and show a jello effect, and at 1600x1200 the update rate collapses to roughly one or two frames per second, which is useless for anything live.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-566" title="EEVblog #566 - Cheap USB Microscope Reviews">566</a></sup> The same behaviour appears on better hardware: a Mantis Elite inspection camera manages 37 frames per second in an 800x600 live window and 33 frames per second at 1280x720, but falls to somewhere between 17 and 12 frames per second once switched to its maximum 1600x1200 mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-817" title="EEVblog #817 - Mantis Elite Cam Inspection Microscope">817</a></sup> Host performance data from that system showed 12.7 frames per second coming from the camera at about 70% CPU loading, against a stated CPU-limited maximum of 70 frames per second — the bottleneck was the camera and the resolution, not the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-817" title="EEVblog #817 - Mantis Elite Cam Inspection Microscope">817</a></sup>

The consequence is a straightforward selection rule for inspection optics. Where the work is live — moving a [[soldering-iron]] under the lens, placing or removing an 0201 — the choice is a lower resolution at a high update rate; a 60 frames per second microscope gives lag low enough to remove an 0201 part under it directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1125" title="EEVblog #1125 - Amazing $500 Soldering/Inspection Microscope!">1125</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-817" title="EEVblog #817 - Mantis Elite Cam Inspection Microscope">817</a></sup> Where the work is capturing a still record, the highest resolution is worth accepting a couple of frames per second and visible dropped frames for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-846" title="EEVblog #846 - Solar Panel Micrometeorite Impact!">846</a></sup> For USB microscope capture work, it is "pretty much all about the frame rate" together with the resolution, and a unit without a high frame rate camera option is simply the wrong tool for live work regardless of how good its optics are.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-817" title="EEVblog #817 - Mantis Elite Cam Inspection Microscope">817</a></sup>

## Light as a frame rate limit

A camera running automatic exposure buys sensitivity with integration time, so frame rate falls with illumination. A webcam's frame rate varies with the amount of light available, and a poor frame rate seen locally — before any encoding or network stage — points at insufficient lighting rather than at the computer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup> Studio lighting is therefore part of the imaging chain, not just the aesthetics: 200 watts of daylight-wavelength compact fluorescent behind a softbox was fitted specifically to hold the frame rate up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-89" title="EEVblog #89 - EEVblog Live Screwup">89</a></sup> The effect is directly observable. A Microsoft LifeCam holding a genuine 30 frames per second dropped to 15 frames per second the moment the studio lights were switched off, the camera compensating rather than holding constant exposure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-590" title="EEVblog #590 - DIYINHK 1080P HD USB Soldering Microscope Review + Tagarno FHD ZIP">590</a></sup> A well-designed sensor resists this: one full HD USB soldering microscope held 60 frames per second in about 300 lux of ambient lab light with barely visible grain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-590" title="EEVblog #590 - DIYINHK 1080P HD USB Soldering Microscope Review + Tagarno FHD ZIP">590</a></sup>

## Capture and production chains

Shooting frame rate in Australia was historically fixed by the PAL cameras sold there, which run at 25 frames per second rather than 30.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-698" title="EEVblog #698 - GPU Video Rendering">698</a></sup> Delivery support for 50 and 60 frames per second changed that, and bench footage moved to those higher rates.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-686" title="EEVblog #686 - Mailbag">686</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-698" title="EEVblog #698 - GPU Video Rendering">698</a></sup> The cost lands on the encoder: 59.94 frames per second material at around 35 megabits per second is a much heavier load than the same length shot at 17 megabits per second, and some codec profiles do not follow — AVCHD and XDCAM support 60 frames per second only at 1280x720, forcing a move to a Sony AVC codec.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-698" title="EEVblog #698 - GPU Video Rendering">698</a></sup> A Canon HF G30 offers 35 megabits per second at 50p and 24p, with 17 megabits per second the usual operating point, while its AVCHD mode tops out at 28 megabits per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2" title="EEVblog2 #650 - Canon HF G30 Camcorder Review - FULL">2</a></sup>

Mixed-source editing is where frame rate becomes real work. A typical mailbag sequence combines a Sony NEX-VG30 at 60 (59.94) frames per second with a Canon HF G30 at 50 frames per second, and is exported at the lower 50 frames per second, so the clips cannot simply be dropped straight into the timeline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-726" title="EEVblog #726 - Dual Xeon Video Editing Machine Build">726</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Converting between rates is harder on the renderer than a single-rate project, which is why a rendering benchmark built from mixed footage is more demanding than the usual case.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-726" title="EEVblog #726 - Dual Xeon Video Editing Machine Build">726</a></sup> A 1920x1080 50 frames per second project matched to 50 frames per second source material, output at 50 frames per second and 26 megabits per second, is slower to render than the older 25 frames per second workflow was.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-943" title="EEVblog #943 - How EEVblog Does Video Editing">943</a></sup> Where the material is screen capture at 30 frames per second, the project itself is dropped from 60 to 30 frames per second so the mixed content behaves.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-SzU4crnexnc" title="Vegas Video Editing &amp; Voukoder Encoding">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-wq0Y-yoCHhI" title="TEST #2: Constant Qp Test - Using Qp">•</a></sup> Later bench work settled on 30 frames per second as the default shooting rate, with 60 frames per second regarded as an unnecessary expense for a teardown.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dJJpCxoNgoM" title="My Automated Handbrake Video Conversion Batch File Script">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cPuMgh-BElU" title="Davinci Resolve 20: Automatic Silence Removal Tested">•</a></sup>

Batch transcoding preserves rate by default: HandBrake's constant frame rate mode matches the source, so 50 or 60 frames per second in yields the same out, with command-line options available to force a change for derived versions such as a 720p podcast cut.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-dJJpCxoNgoM" title="My Automated Handbrake Video Conversion Batch File Script">•</a></sup> Transcode throughput is itself measured in frames per second: an RTX 4060 processed 1080p material at about 480 frames per second against roughly 330 to 350 for an RTX 2060, and 4K at around 130 frames per second against 80 to 100 previously.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cm1HFKOOCgo" title="RTX4060 AV1 vs H.265 Encoder Using Handbrake">•</a></sup>

Frame rate also sets the resolution of timing measurements made in an editor. Stepping frame by frame through 30 (29.97) frames per second footage of a chroma key system showed the keying window trailing the main camera by exactly one frame.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-eOAAHcR0YY0" title="BlackMagic ATEM Mini Pro Audio/Video Delay Measurement">•</a></sup> It sets the granularity of automated editing tools as well — a silence-removal minimum of 50 frames is about one and a half seconds at 30 frames per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cPuMgh-BElU" title="Davinci Resolve 20: Automatic Silence Removal Tested">•</a></sup>

## Capture cards, links and displays

Every stage in a chain has its own ceiling, and the slowest one wins. A Tagarno microscope outputting 60 frames per second fed a capture card capable of only 30, and was in turn captured at 25 frames per second to match the main camera.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-625" title="EEVblog #625 - Retro Teardown: Sony&#x27;s First Digital Camera">625</a></sup> A television that accepted other HDMI sources produced no signal at all from that 60 frames per second microscope output, while a Raspberry Pi on the same input worked, isolating the fault to the display's inability to handle the rate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-630" title="EEVblog #630 - How To: Soniq LCD TV Troubleshooting Repair - Part 1">630</a></sup> Notebook hardware able to bring in 60 frames per second data intermittently jerked and recovered, but had ample margin for the 30 frames per second it actually needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-590" title="EEVblog #590 - DIYINHK 1080P HD USB Soldering Microscope Review + Tagarno FHD ZIP">590</a></sup>

Where the link is the constraint, frame rate is budgeted rather than chosen. A 2K by 2K imaging sensor capable of 150 frames per second — 100 in the intended configuration — was flown with a target of 15 frames per second, because the surrounding hardware could not sustain getting the data out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-896" title="EEVblog #896 - Space Electronics">896</a></sup> For a lunar rover, the real-time requirement was quarter VGA at a frame rate merely sufficient to show motion, and the frame rate directly bounds the achievable speed: at one frame per second the vehicle can barely move, while a 3.6 kilometre per hour driving speed demands more.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-886" title="EEVblog #886 - Audi Lunar Quattro Moon Rover">886</a></sup>

## Frame rate as an instrument specification

Instruments quote update rates for the same reason cameras do. On an [[oscilloscope]], raising the screen update rate from automatic through 30, 40 and 50 frames per second progressively improved the odds of catching a runt pulse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-487" title="EEVblog #487 - Tekway DST1102B Oscilloscope Review">487</a></sup> On a USB oscilloscope the equivalent counter measured almost 400 frames per second, where a frame is one capture buffer — one entire acquisition window — rather than a displayed image.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup> Thermal imagers sit at the other end: a FLIR ETS320 updates at 9 frames per second, which is adequate for a production and R&D tool, and a Lepton sensor's output was inferred at about 26 frames per second from video packet bursts spaced 38.5 milliseconds apart.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-988" title="EEVblog #988 - FLIR ETS320 Benchtop Thermal Camera Review">988</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-670" title="EEVblog #670 - FLIR TG165 Lepton Sensor Followup">670</a></sup>

Camera frame rate and exposure also determine what artefacts appear in footage of other equipment. Display flicker invisible to the eye is a function of the recording camera's rate and shows up only on camera; stepping a camera down through 1/1000th, 1/500th, 1/250th and 1/120th of a second progressively resolves the pulsating of a [[multimeter]] [[lcd]], at the cost of a moving finger blurring at anything short of the slowest setting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-894" title="EEVblog #894 - Keysight U1461A Insulation Resistance Multimeter Teardown">894</a></sup>

## High-speed capture

Above normal rates the objective changes from smooth motion to resolving an event, and the required rate is set by the event's timescale. A camcorder high-speed mode running 300 frames per second at about 500x340 and limited to ten seconds of recording proved too slow to capture an [[electrolytic-capacitor]] explosion properly; 1,000 to 2,000 frames per second is the region where the expansion can be seen frame to frame.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-42" title="EEVblog #42 - Exploding Capacitors in High Speed">42</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup> At 1,000 frames per second the rupture and boiling of a [[capacitor]] resolve into successive distinct frames.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-857" title="EEVblog #857 - High Speed Camera Fun">857</a></sup> A dedicated Chronos high-speed camera runs 1280x720 at 1,500 frames per second with playback scrubbing at 60 frames per second, and other hardware reaches a limit of 3,271 frames per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-947" title="EEVblog #947 - Chronos High Speed Camera Review">947</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VfGUM66j5zg" title="Destin From SmarterEveryDay - High Speed Video Capture">•</a></sup> Higher is not automatically better: for a slap sequence shot at 1,500 frames per second, a subsequent take was deliberately set to around 960 because the slower rate produced the more effective result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VfGUM66j5zg" title="Destin From SmarterEveryDay - High Speed Video Capture">•</a></sup>

## Aliasing and mechanical frame rates

Frame rate need not come from a sensor at all. A persistence-of-vision phone accessory spins at 30 times a second and therefore produces 30 frames a second, the slit sweeping past the observer once per revolution.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup> Frame rate is likewise the natural figure of merit for rendering benchmarks, where 39, 43 and 51 frames per second describe graphics performance, and for novelty displays running at 12 frames per second.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1280" title="EEVblog #1280 - Dumpster Lab PC Upgrade">1280</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1069" title="EEVblog #1069 - Mailbag">1069</a></sup>
