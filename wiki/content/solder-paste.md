Solder paste is a suspension of fine [[solder]] alloy powder in [[flux]], deposited onto [[pcb|printed circuit board]] pads before components are placed and the assembly is heated until the alloy melts and wets the joint — the process called [[reflow-soldering|reflow soldering]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Because the flux is already carried inside the paste, no separate flux application is needed: the paste is put down on the board first, the part is laid on top, and heat does the rest.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> It is the foundation of essentially all modern surface-mount assembly, and it is also the only practical way to solder packages whose contacts are hidden beneath the body, such as QFN and MLF devices with a central [[thermal-pad|thermal pad]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>

Paste is not a universal replacement for wire solder. Ordinary leaded solder is easier to work with and adequate for most bench tasks; paste is worth adopting specifically for advanced surface-mount work, where it can be very useful.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup>

## Composition and grades

Paste is sold in alloys matching conventional wire solder. A typical leaded formulation is SN63 — 63% tin, 37% lead — close to ordinary 60/40 and explicitly not lead-free.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup> Lead-free tin-based pastes are standard for production boards, and a stencil printer loaded for a lead-free board is running lead-free paste.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>

Pastes are graded by the size of the solder powder. The finest commonly available is type 6, whose individual solder balls measure 5 to 15 microns.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-163" title="EEVblog #163 - Solder Paste Porn">163</a></sup> Finer powder is what makes very small apertures print cleanly.

The flux system matters as much as the alloy. No-clean paste is the preferred choice because it leaves behind no residue requiring a post-reflow cleaning step.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> Where cleaning is unavoidable, paste selection and the matching cleaning agent have to be treated as a single decision: on boards built from low dielectric absorption laminates such as Rogers material, an assembly house washing with its usual general-purpose cleaner can leave residue that ruins the board's performance, and datasheets for such designs specify both the paste and what to clean it with.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1755" title="EEVblog 1755 - Tolicore Femtoammeter TEARDOWN + REVERSE ENGINEER">1755</a></sup> Residue from paste flux is visible on finished joints as a film around the pins.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-115" title="EEVblog #115 - Extech 380193 LCR Meter Review">115</a></sup>

## Storage and shelf life

Paste must be refrigerated and kept airtight, with the nozzle replaced on the syringe before it goes back in the fridge; left out, it goes off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-180" title="EEVblog #180 - Soldering Tutorial Part 1 - Tools">180</a></sup> Before use it has to be brought back to room temperature over a couple of hours.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> It also carries a printed shelf life, beyond which performance is no longer guaranteed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> Paste already dispensed onto a stencil printer can be scraped back and reused for perhaps two or three days.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup>

These handling demands, together with the cost, make paste a poor fit for very occasional work: for one or two boards a year it may not be worth the trouble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> For QFN rework the quantities involved are trivial — a ten-dollar tube goes a long way — and a 15 g syringe is enough for many stencil prints of a typical board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>

Working time is also a constraint. Hand-placing several hundred parts on a pasted panel can take long enough that the paste begins to dry out before the last component is set, which is one of the arguments for a pick-and-place machine on larger boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-740" title="EEVblog #740 - LitePlacer Pick &amp; Place Machine Kit Unboxing">740</a></sup>

## Stencil printing

The standard way to deposit paste is through a stencil cut from the paste mask layer of the [[pcb-design|PCB design]], so that paste is applied only to the pads and nowhere else on the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup> Production stencils are stainless steel, but these are expensive for one-off use; Mylar, Kapton or polyamide stencils can be laser cut directly from the CAD file cheaply enough that they are effectively given away with small board orders.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup>

Manual printing technique:

- Surround the board with scrap boards of the same thickness so the stencil sits level — 1.6 mm offcuts for a 1.6 mm board, thin ones for a 0.8 mm board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-407" title="EEVblog #407 - Mailbag">407</a></sup>
- Inspect the stencil first to confirm every aperture is actually cut.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>
- Tape only one edge, forming a hinge, so the stencil can be lifted straight up as a flap without dragging across the printed deposits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>
- Spread with a squeegee or putty knife at a controlled angle of attack; a plastic credit card works well and is better for the job than a rigid metal spreader.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-407" title="EEVblog #407 - Mailbag">407</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-658" title="EEVblog #658 - Mailbag">658</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1003" title="EEVblog #1003 - Mailbag">1003</a></sup>
- Lift the stencil cleanly and do not touch the result.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>

Small amounts of paste smeared between adjacent pads after lifting are normal and not a cause for concern.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> Placement afterwards need not be precise, since surface-mount parts self-centre on their pads as the paste reflows, though the strength of that effect varies by component.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup>

Production stencil printers automate the same sequence: the board is aligned by a fiducial camera, raised hydraulically to compress it against the stencil, and the squeegee wipes across.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-264" title="EEVblog #264 - SMD PCB Pick &amp; Place Machine Assembly">264</a></sup> The result is described in industry terms as silk-screening the paste onto a bare board, and it feeds directly into pick-and-place and then the reflow oven.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-239" title="EEVblog #239 - PCB Design For Manufacture Part 2">239</a></sup>

## Syringe and dispenser application

Paste is also supplied in syringes for hand application, either before the part is laid down or afterwards, with the quantity being the whole difficulty — too much and it has to be cleaned off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-186" title="EEVblog #186 - Soldering Tutorial Part 3 - Surface Mount">186</a></sup> For a fine-pitch package with no accessible pins, a bead or line of paste is laid along each row of pads plus the thermal pad, the part is held down, and a hot air gun at roughly 250 to 260 °C brings the whole area up to a uniform temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup>

The consistent bench judgement is that too little paste is safer than too much: a joint short of solder can be touched up with an iron afterwards, whereas excess has to be removed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup> In practice this errs far enough that reflowed joints sometimes come out visibly starved.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup>

Manual syringe dispensers with a lever mechanism need a delicate touch — where twenty full lever strokes drive the plunger to the bottom of the barrel, a single full depression dispenses far more than one pad's worth and simply oozes paste over the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-556" title="EEVblog #556 - Mailbag">556</a></sup>

Automated paste dispensers occupy a middle [[ground]] between hand syringes and stencil printing. Such a machine places paste only and is not a pick-and-place; a representative unit holds the board on magnetic clamps that allow arbitrary positioning on the platen, references itself to the board's fiducials, and drives a paste syringe through interchangeable nozzles.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-163" title="EEVblog #163 - Solder Paste Porn">163</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1686" title="EEVblog 1686 - Electronex 2025 Melbourne - Walkaround">1686</a></sup> The output is a board with paste on the individual pads ready to go to a pick-and-place machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-200" title="EEVblog #200 - Retrospective">200</a></sup> Dispensing is inherently slower than stencil printing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1686" title="EEVblog 1686 - Electronex 2025 Melbourne - Walkaround">1686</a></sup>

## Reflow profile

Reflow proceeds in phases: a slow, even preheat that avoids cracking components through thermal shock; a soak that activates the flux inside the paste and drives off its volatile chemicals; the reflow spike itself, which melts the solder; and a controlled decay.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> The correct profile comes from the paste manufacturer's [[datasheet]], and that profile is what gets programmed into a reflow oven or a converted toaster oven.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>

A profile is specified as a band rather than a line — an upper and lower curve the board temperature must stay between — typically peaking around 180 seconds before tapering off.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> A no-clean lead-free paste supplied with a reflow oven kit specified a peak of 250 °C and a ramp rate of 2 °C per second, with the board expected to pass at least 220 to 230 °C for the joints to form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-558" title="EEVblog #558 - Beta Layout DIY SMD Thermal Reflow Oven">558</a></sup> The right profile varies with the paste and also with the board itself: heavy ground planes without adequate thermal relief change how pads heat and can pull parts into tombstoning.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> Surface-mount parts are designed to survive this, tolerating a couple of hundred degrees for a sustained period.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup>

Where a board fails to reflow, high local [[thermal-mass|thermal mass]] is the usual cause — a large pad with a heavy trace running off it reaching insufficient temperature, or the flux embedded in the paste failing to clean the joint properly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-588" title="EEVblog #588 - How To Do PCB Production Testing">588</a></sup>

## Failure modes and layout interactions

**Solder balls.** Paste volume is never perfectly repeatable from board to board, and a deposit slightly over target can leave a stray solder ball loose on the assembly, which then becomes an unpredictable source of shorts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-884" title="EEVblog #884 - EEVBlog BM235 Multimeter REPAIR">884</a></sup>

**Bridging.** Where the board has no [[solder-mask|solder mask]] between adjacent pads, reflowed paste can bridge across, though such shorts are straightforward to clean up.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup>

**Wicking down vias.** Vias in pads — including thermal [[via]] arrays under a power package — let molten [[solder-wick|solder wick]] down through the board, which is a genuine drawback of the technique.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-744" title="EEVblog #744 - SMD Thermal Heatsink Design - µSupply Part 15">744</a></sup> Bare-board fabricators do not care about vias in pads, but they offer a via-plugging option specifically so the paste is not wicked away during reflow; unplugged vias are acceptable if the solder loss is tolerable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Where solder does leech through, the amount left on the top side becomes uncontrolled, and if the loss is not accounted for the device may end up with insufficient solder holding it down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup>

**Joint geometry.** A paste-and-reflow joint wets the underside of the terminal and forms only a small fillet at the side; it does not climb over the top of the lead the way a hand-soldered joint does, so the hand joint is mechanically more robust.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> The same signature distinguishes reflow from [[wave-soldering|wave soldering]], where a molten bath plates the legs of the parts right up their length.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-431" title="EEVblog #431 - PCB Wave Soldering">431</a></sup>

**Thermal pads.** A package with a bottom-side thermal pad cannot be soldered by pre-tinning the pad and reflowing, because tinning leaves a lumpy surface before it melts; paste solves this chicken-and-egg problem by going down flat first.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup> The thermal pad can also dominate the joint mechanically, preventing the part from self-aligning as the perimeter pads pull it in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-346" title="EEVblog #346 - MLF/QFN SMD Reflow Soldering">346</a></sup>

At the extreme of miniaturisation, some large [[processor]] packages carry no balls at all: paste is simply applied to the land pattern and reflowed, and with pad widths of the order of 180 microns the resulting quantity of solder is so small that modest shear force can strip every pad at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1341" title="EEVblog #1341 - AMAZING $250,000 IBM Processor TEARDOWN!">1341</a></sup>

## Related uses

Paste-based deposition is used to fill vias with solder, which is done at the assembly stage rather than by the bare-board fabricator: the vias are left untented and solder is placed via the paste mask, raising the [[current]]-carrying capability of the via.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup> The alternative is wave soldering, where the wave bubbles up and fills the holes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Paste can also be applied to the underside of a board so that a part with a bottom die pad protruding through a cut-out is soldered from the reverse side.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-677" title="EEVblog #677 - Mailbag">677</a></sup> Hot-bar attachment of flat flex to a board relies on a conductive paste under the joint which melts as the bar passes across, forming the connection.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-808" title="EEVblog #808 - Fluke 196 Scopemeter Repair">808</a></sup>

Where a design is released with full manufacturing data, the paste layer accompanies the assembly drawing and pick-and-place files as one of the standard outputs.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1581" title="EEVblog 1581 - Tesla Roadster now OPEN SOURCE?">1581</a></sup>
