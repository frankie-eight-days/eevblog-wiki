A linear power supply derives a regulated DC output by rectifying and filtering a mains [[transformer]]'s secondary, then dropping the surplus [[voltage]] across a series pass element operated in its linear region.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-715" title="EEVblog #715 - Mailbag">715</a></sup> Its defining characteristic is that the excess voltage is not recovered but dissipated as heat, which makes it heavy, bulky and inefficient — and, in exchange, gives it [[noise]] and [[ripple]] performance a switch-mode supply is very hard pressed to match.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> It remains the preferred topology for bench supplies and for the internal rails of precision instruments, where output cleanliness matters more than [[efficiency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1173" title="EEVblog #1173 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz Power Supply Bonanza">1173</a></sup>

## Topology and construction

The canonical arrangement is a mains transformer, a [[bridge-rectifier|bridge rectifier]], bulk filter [[capacitance]], and a regulator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-715" title="EEVblog #715 - Mailbag">715</a></sup> At its simplest the regulator is a three-terminal device with only an input and an output plus a couple of filter capacitors, stepping something like 9 V down to 5 V in the manner of a 7805.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-90" title="EEVblog #90 - Linear and LDO regulators and Switch Mode Power Supply Tutorial">90</a></sup> Historic instrument supplies follow exactly this pattern: a TO-3 LM309K fed by a 4700 µF filter [[capacitor]] generating the [[5-volt-rail|5 V rail]] for the digital section of a bench [[multimeter]], with smaller local linear regulation distributed around the boards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-613" title="EEVblog #613 - Prema 6047 Multimeter Teardown">613</a></sup> Larger examples scale the same blocks up — a dual bridge rectifier built from four panel-mounted diodes feeding three 15,000 µF 40 V filter capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-574" title="EEVblog #574 - NEC Analog TV IF Modulator Teardown">574</a></sup> Multiple electrolytic capacitors in parallel after the bridge are a standard sight in such supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-742" title="EEVblog #742 - Why Electrolytic Capacitors Are Connected In Parallel">742</a></sup> The bulk capacitance at the supply output stores charge for low-[[frequency]] events, notably smoothing the 50 or 100 Hz mains-derived ripple of a bridge-rectified linear supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup>

Rectifier diodes in these supplies are unremarkable parts — a nominal 1 A bridge such as a DF02 is typical of a basic linear supply, though its single half-sine peak forward surge rating can reach 50 A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-376" title="EEVblog #376 - Multimeter Fuse Diode Followup">376</a></sup> The forward drop is the cost: roughly 0.6 V per [[diode]], rising toward a volt at higher currents, so two bridges in a supply throw away about two volts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup> [[mosfet|MOSFET]]-based synchronous rectifier controllers such as the LT4320 substitute low on-[[resistance]] for that drop and recover the loss, making an already-linear supply more efficient still.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-711" title="EEVblog #711 - Mailbag">711</a></sup>

Not every linear supply is regulated. The external brick that powered the Apple IIc was a transformer, a full-wave bridge rectifier and output capacitors with a nominal 15 V DC at 1.2 A rating and no regulation at all, with regulation handled downstream by a DC-to-DC converter inside the machine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-788" title="EEVblog #788 - Apple IIC Teardown">788</a></sup> A homebrew 25 A linear supply on a homebrew chassis is a recognised amateur construction project, and a bench supply built around an [[lm317|LM317]] and a couple of knobs is one of the classic build-it-yourself projects for a new lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-428" title="EEVblog #428 - Wyong Field Day 2013">428</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup>

## Dissipation, heat sinking and transformer taps

The central design constraint is the power lost in the pass element, equal to the input-to-output differential times the load [[current]]. A supply rated 30 V at 3 A set to deliver 1 V from a 30 V rail must drop 30 V at 3 A, an enormous dissipation in the output [[transistor]] and [[heat-sink|heat sink]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> The same arithmetic applies at 3.3 V out from a 40 V transformer rail, and to [[battery]]-powered equipment, where a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]] dropping 12 V to 3.3 V wastes a large fraction of the battery's capacity as heat in the heat sink.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-258" title="EEVblog #258 - PSU Housing Design - Part 11">258</a></sup> A 34 V maximum output at 5 A [[output-current|output current]] would likewise mean dissipating a huge amount of power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup>

The standard mitigation in variable bench supplies is to tap the transformer secondary and switch taps as the [[output-voltage|output voltage]] setting rises, keeping the differential across the pass element bounded. An Atten triple-output supply brings out 7.6 V, 19 V and 34 V taps switched by two relays, which is why its heat sink can be far smaller than the roughly 150 W per channel rating would otherwise demand.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-440" title="EEVblog #440 - Atten PPS3205T-3S Triple Output Power Supply Teardown">440</a></sup> The Korad KA3005P uses three taps and two relays on the same principle, switching in taps above certain voltages to reduce heat sink dissipation in a 30 V 5 A supply whose heat sink is not large by the standards of that rating.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup> A Siglent bench supply switches taps according to the selected voltage range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> The Rigol DP832 does the same job with triacs and triac drivers instead of the more traditional relays.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-509" title="EEVblog #509 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply">509</a></sup> Range switching is also what gives such supplies their split specifications — 0 to 8 V at 3 A per channel and 0 to 20 V at 1.5 A per channel in a roughly 60 W two-output unit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup>

Even with tapping, the topology costs size and mass. A linear supply needs a big heat sink on the back and is wasteful in energy for a given output power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup> Weight is a reliable tell: a 40 A switch-mode supply weighing three and a half to four kilograms would weigh a ton as a linear, and the presence of a linear supply inside an instrument can be identified by its weight alone.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-272" title="EEVblog #272 - Manson 9400 40A 3-15V Switchmode PSU Teardown">272</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-314" title="EEVblog #314 - Korad KA3005P PSU Teardown">314</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1173" title="EEVblog #1173 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz Power Supply Bonanza">1173</a></sup> Delivering 60 W per module, or 180 W total, linearly demands a big thumping transformer, which is precisely why high-density instrument supplies go switch-mode.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-755" title="EEVblog #755 - Fluke PM2812 PSU Ebay Score">755</a></sup> Where a 7.5-digit multimeter has no room for a large linear transformer, a switch-mode supply becomes mandatory and the design effort shifts to keeping its [[switching-noise|switching noise]] out of the analog section, using an electrostatically shielded transformer to lower primary-to-secondary capacitance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> A 360 W bench supply is a switching design for the same reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup>

## Ripple and noise behaviour

Ripple is correctly understood as the charge-discharge cycle of the storage element in any supply, linear or switch-mode; it is not exclusively mains hum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> In a linear supply, however, that ripple does appear at the mains-derived frequency — 50 or 60 Hz, or 100 or 120 Hz after full-wave rectification.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> This has a practical consequence for measurement: an [[oscilloscope]] can be triggered from the AC line to hold the ripple stationary on screen, a technique that works only for a linear supply and fails on a switch-mode converter with a free-running [[switching-frequency|switching frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup>

Because a linear supply has no switching stage, any high-frequency content observed on its output is diagnostic rather than intrinsic. Measuring a linear bench supply and seeing high-frequency peaks in the trace indicates common-mode noise entering the measurement system from elsewhere, not noise generated by the supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-441" title="EEVblog #441 - How To Track Down Common Mode Noise">441</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-594" title="EEVblog #594 - How To Measure Power Supply Ripple &amp; Noise">594</a></sup> The absence of switching components is also the basis for using a linear bench supply as a clean reference source when characterising the noise of some other converter under test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1606" title="EEVblog 1606 - $45 AlienTek DP100 100W USB-C PSU REVIEW">1606</a></sup>

## Applications and design judgment

As a class, the best bench power supplies are linear rather than switch-mode, because the noise and ripple performance is much better than a switching design can readily achieve.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup> The consistent engineering position is that linear is heaps better than switch mode on simplicity and noise grounds, and that switch-mode should be adopted only where it is required — to step up or boost a voltage, or to obtain higher efficiency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> Instrument teardowns bear this out: linear supplies are standard inside older laboratory gear, and the internals of a linear bench supply are typically undramatic, with commodity op-amps such as the LF442, LM393 and LF411 and ten-turn pots to trim the output range.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-166" title="EEVblog #166 - HP Agilent E3610A Lab Power Supply">166</a></sup> A ten-turn [[potentiometer]] is the recommended adjustment element for a self-built linear supply without [[microcontroller]] control; coarse-and-fine dual controls on a commercial supply are a giveaway that a cheaper pot was used instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-225" title="EEVblog #225 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 4 - PWM Control">225</a></sup>

Linear supplies are also chosen where isolation or output quality is the point — a linear mains adapter supplied with an insulation resistance meter for its higher isolation voltage, or a linear rail feeding an audio DAC specifically to avoid switching components in the signal path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-468" title="EEVblog #468 - Uni-T UT513 Insulation Resistance Meter Teardown">468</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1613" title="EEVblog 1613 - Audiophile REPAIR: $4500 Chord Hugo DAC">1613</a></sup> Instrument-internal linear supplies show up wherever the digital and analog sections need only modest power, such as the small linear supply inside an [[electronic-load|electronic load]], which powers only its own circuitry rather than a load output.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup> Commercially, a 150 W linear programmable supply with [[usb|USB]] serial control sold for AUD 199, and a completely linear laboratory supply offering 0 to 100 V with 1 µA current resolution and 40 W per channel, 80 W total, represents the high end of the topology.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Advertised linear supplies are not always purely linear: a linear output stage may be preceded by a switching tracking pre-regulator, an arrangement that yields more output power in a smaller form factor at the cost of a switching element in the chain.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup>

One failure scenario is specific to the topology: feeding voltage back into the supply from the output, as when a charged battery remains connected while the input voltage is switched off, is a common situation for traditional linear supplies and raises the question of whether the regulator survives it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-233" title="EEVblog #233 - Lab Power Supply Design Part 6 - LT3080 Testing">233</a></sup> For characterising any supply, linear or switch-mode, a precision DC electronic load is the appropriate instrument, and is among the most overlooked items in a lab.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup>

The rectifier arrangements learned in linear supplies generalise. The half-wave and full-wave rectifiers of a linear supply are the reference point for understanding Cockcroft-Walton multipliers, where a full-wave version is built by mirroring the circuit and adding a [[transformer-tap|transformer tap]] exactly as in a linear supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-469" title="EEVblog #469 - Cockcroft-Walton Multiplier">469</a></sup> The diode-and-capacitor output filter of a microcontroller voltage [[inverter]] is the familiar linear-supply filter with the diode reversed for negative rails.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-483" title="EEVblog #483 - Microcontroller Voltage Inverter Tutorial">483</a></sup> A precision peak detector behaves like the ordinary diode rectifier of a linear supply, except that the op-amp forces the output to equal the input exactly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-490" title="EEVblog #490 - Peak Detector Circuit">490</a></sup> A linear power supply is itself one of the everyday realisations of an ideal [[voltage-source|voltage source]], which in practice must be modelled with a series resistance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1397" title="EEVblog 1397 - DC Fundamentals Part 2: DC Voltage &amp; Current Sources (Thevenin &amp; Norton Theorems)">1397</a></sup>
