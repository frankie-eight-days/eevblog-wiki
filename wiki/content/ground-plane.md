A ground plane is a large uninterrupted area of copper on a [[pcb|printed circuit board]] dedicated to the ground net, either flood-filled across an outer layer or given an entire internal layer of a multi-layer stackup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Its purpose is to give every return [[current]] a short, low-[[impedance]], low-[[inductance]] path back to its source, and in doing so it governs radiated emissions, [[signal-integrity]], DC [[voltage-drop]] and the thermal behaviour of the board during assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> As a first approximation the correct action on almost any layout is to flood fill the ground: a big low-impedance, low-inductance plane over the whole thing generally does the business, and a star point should only be used where there is a specific reason for it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

## Return current and loop area

Every current forms a loop, and the ground plane is where the return half of that loop lives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> Current leaving a supply through a [[mosfet]] and into ground must come back through the plane to close the loop, which is why measured current direction reverses between charge and discharge in a [[battery]] circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup> The plane can be modelled as a grid of resistors, and the path the return current takes through that grid depends on [[frequency]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> At low frequencies bulk decoupling current spreads out and its position barely matters.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> As frequency rises, the return current stops spreading across the copper and concentrates directly beneath the trace it is returning for, even though the plane is one big continuous sheet of copper — the rest of the copper ceases to matter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1439" title="EEVblog 1439 - Analysing Veritasium&#x27;s Electricity Misconceptions Video">1439</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

That concentration is what makes the plane an EMC tool: it minimises the area enclosed by the outgoing trace and its return, and loop area sets the radiated field for a given switching current.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Probing directly on the leg of a [[bypass-capacitor]] shows the current flowing through that lead, having crossed the ground plane to get there, so moving the [[capacitor]] closer to the load shrinks the loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1085" title="EEVblog #1085 - Bypass Capacitors Visualised!">1085</a></sup> The corollary is that anything obstructing the return path is destructive. A gap in the flood fill — not a routed slot, merely an unfilled region — forces the return current the long way around, wrecking the timing budget and the signal integrity of a memory interface at once.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup> The rule follows directly: do not split ground planes under high-speed signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1247" title="EEVblog #1247 - DDR Memory PCB Propagation Delay &amp; Layout">1247</a></sup>

## Two layers versus four

The effect has been measured directly on an identical layout produced as both a two-layer and a four-layer board, with no trace changed, the same clock, and the same bypass capacitors — the only difference being an internal ground plane and a 5 V plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The two-layer version has no internal plane at all, and its returns run over flood fills on top and bottom joined by [[via]] stitching, with paths of high inductance wandering across the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> On the four-layer version the flood fills are unnecessary because the whole board is one plane, so a ground pin connects to the bypass capacitor over a path of the same length but far lower inductance, and the ground and power planes add shielding against magnetic and electric fields.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> The measured reduction in near-field radiated emissions was 15 dB — enough to be the difference between passing and failing compliance, and between shipping and respinning the product at a cost of five or ten thousand dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup>

Solid ground and power planes across a board are without competition; via stitching on a two-layer board improves matters but does not equal them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> Where two layers are mandated, the practices that help are keeping ground and power on top of each other wherever possible, not splitting the ground, and stitching and flood filling aggressively; a well-laid-out two-layer board can radiate less than a poorly laid out four-layer one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1176" title="EEVblog #1176 - 2 Layer vs 4 Layer PCB EMC TESTED!">1176</a></sup> A board with no plane at all, such as the single-sided phenolic construction found in cheap [[usb]] hubs, has neither a return path nor controlled impedance for high-speed signalling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12" title="EEVblog #12 Part 2 of 2 - Shanghai Special - Dodgy USB Hubs">12</a></sup> Early home computers built on double-sided board without a plane failed FCC certification outright, with long parallel buses, memory interfaces and unshielded ribbon cables to the expansion box acting as antennas.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-645" title="EEVblog #645 - TRS-80 Model I Retro Computer Teardown">645</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-955" title="EEVblog #955 - World&#x27;s First Portable Computer Teardown">955</a></sup>

## The plane is not an ideal conductor

A large plane has non-zero [[resistance]], non-zero spread inductance and is not the same node everywhere.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Carrying 8 A across a board, a plane can easily drop 40 mV, and that figure is for the power side alone, before the ground connection is added; where a design has only 120 mV of margin, this is not a rounding error.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Measuring across a board from one bypass capacitor to another gives readings in the tens of milliohms — 67 mΩ in one case — comprising the plane itself plus the thermal relief spokes and joints at each pad, which is enough to defeat low-resistance short-hunting until a meter with milliohm resolution is used.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1061" title="EEVblog #1061 - Data IO Programmer REPAIR - Part 1">1061</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-717ampROkvc" title="PRO TIP: Using a 6.5 Digit Multimeter for finding Shorts">•</a></sup> The same shared impedance produces ground bounce and power bounce when a switching section shares copper with sensitive digital parts, an effect that a big plane reduces but does not eliminate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1365" title="EEVblog #1365 - Viewer PCB &amp; Circuit Design Review">1365</a></sup> On an [[oscilloscope]], energising a channel [[relay]] pulls extra current through the plane and shifts the [[dc-offset]] of a neighbouring channel by a couple of hundred microvolts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-704" title="EEVBlog #704 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Features Review">704</a></sup> Every conductor, including a plane, has inductance, and lowering the inductance from pad to via to plane makes a measurable difference to bypassing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup>

## Splitting

Splitting a ground plane is the most consequential decision in plane design and the one most often taken wrongly. For low-power, non-fast digital work, one plane for the entire board is normal and analog and digital may share it; with 8-bit converters one big plane is fine.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> Separation becomes necessary when the performance demands it — 16-bit dynamic range with high-speed digital on one side of the board and analog on the other.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> A split is really a star ground: separate planes for separate domains, joined at a single point, with the intervening space left deliberately bare so that current flowing to an [[lcd]] connector cannot disturb an [[fpga]] and the FPGA's switching cannot disturb the LCD supply.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Grounds may overlap freely on different layers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup> Against this, splitting power planes is dangerous for EMC, and a plane must remain continuous over the whole of the power track loop regardless of any split — break it and EMC problems follow.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup> Splitting grounds is generally more of a problem than splitting power.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup>

Deliberate splits appear throughout galvanically isolated equipment: totally separate planes between the channels of a power analyser, a visible break under a digital isolator, split copper across an isolated USB-to-RS232 interface, a gap between current and [[voltage-measurement]] sections of a 600 V power meter, and a plane that stops at the mains-isolated boundary of an instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-875" title="EEVblog #875 - NI VirtualBench Teardown">875</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-680" title="EEVblog #680 - Mailbag">680</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1693" title="EEVblog 1693 - Uni-T UTE310 Power Meter Teardown &amp; Practical Demo">1693</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-164" title="EEVblog #164 - Agilent Fly To The Moon">164</a></sup> Splits are also made for signal integrity by removing plane copper: the bottom plane taken out beneath two scope input channels, plane removed under a metal shield covering low-noise op amps, and copper cleared from beneath antennas and sensor pads.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-360" title="EEVblog #360 - Rigol DS2000 Oscilloscope Teardown">360</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-529" title="EEVblog #529 - HP 35660A DSA Upgrade Investigation">529</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1190" title="EEVblog #1190 - Mailbag Review Bonanza">1190</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-171" title="EEVblog #171 - Agilent U1272A Multimeter Teardown">171</a></sup> Conversely, on a fast [[comparator]] input the plane was carried straight through rather than split.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup>

## Stackup and layer assignment

The conventional four-layer order is signal, ground, power, signal.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Putting signal, power, then ground is workable but inferior, because having ground directly beneath the signal layer is what sets the loop area and bypassing behaviour.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Inner layers should be reserved for solid power and ground planes rather than routed with traces — both for signal integrity and EMC, and because traces buried on inner layers cannot be inspected, cut or modified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> The dielectric spacing matters: for lowest emissions the core should be thick and the prepregs between the planes and the outer signal layers as thin as possible, bringing each signal layer close to its reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> Ground and VCC are effectively the same node for this purpose, which the bypass capacitors ensure.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> Copper weight can be specified per layer, with 2 oz on power and ground layers carrying real current and 0.25 oz sufficient for shield layers that carry none.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

A plane on an outer layer over a trace on top is a microstrip; a signal sandwiched between two planes is a stripline.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1530" title="EEVblog 1530 - Siglent SDS2000X HD 12 Bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1530</a></sup> Boards routed as a single-sided surface-mount layout preserve the most plane area, and shooting for that is the goal on every SMD layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1323" title="EEVblog #1323 - PCB Layout Review &amp; Analysis">1323</a></sup> Blind and buried vias matter here because every through via punches a hole in every plane it passes: breaking out a BGA without them chops the planes severely, whereas blind and buried vias leave a solid plane and buy routing density as well.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup> Boards for high-density work go to six, eight or ten layers precisely to put a ground layer between everything.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-598" title="EEVblog #598 - Sony DME7000 Video Multi Effects Processor Teardown">598</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-444" title="EEVblog #444 - Car Lane Guidance Camera Teardown">444</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VnrtneFP_6A" title="eevBLAB #51- Industry Story - Design Deadlines &amp; Unusual PCB Designs">•</a></sup>

## Distributed capacitance

An internal ground plane and an adjacent power plane form a capacitor — two sheets of copper separated by a dielectric, in this case the epoxy-glass of the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> Measured against discrete parts, the plane pair on a four-layer board showed roughly 2 nF with a broad, flat impedance response, in contrast to the sharp resonant peak of a 100 nF film capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> The broadness is the point: a plane does not present the peaky response that can cause LC resonance trouble.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Position on the board makes only a small difference, since the plane is essentially one big capacitor with edge effects.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1117" title="EEVblog #1117 - PCB Power Plane Capacitance">1117</a></sup> Bulk distributed plane [[capacitance]] is a real mechanism, sufficient to keep a board running with individual bypass capacitors lifted, and there is published work and practical demonstration showing it can outperform discrete bypassing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> It is not, however, a substitute for bypass capacitors in general, and where a board has only split planes with scattered via paths rather than a solid one, the mechanism is largely absent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1081" title="EEVblog #1081 - Are Bypass Capacitors REALLY needed? (Muntzing)">1081</a></sup> Even on a two-layer board where the top is a positive rail and the bottom is all ground, the two layers act as one big bypass capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup> Plane capacitance can also work against a design: ferrite beads fitted between two grounds in a [[multimeter]] resonated with the plane capacitance and had to be shorted out in a later revision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

## Flood filling in practice

Ground is normally the last net left unrouted, and flood filling it is the easiest way to finish a layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Filling need not cover the whole board — a high-[[voltage]] Nixie section is a reason to keep the pour to one half.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> Saving a few millimetres of trace length on the plane layer to avoid cutting up the copper matters little on a low-speed board but becomes significant on a critical one.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Star points are created by placing keepouts that stop the polygon joining, then bringing the two pours together at the chosen point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup>

Several mechanical rules recur. Planes should be pulled back from the board edge rather than taken to it or beyond, and the tool's automatic trimming should not be relied on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup> Pour clearance need not inherit the trace design rules — an 8 thou routing clearance can be relaxed to 10 or 15 thou for the pour, and a plane crowding every pad is a sign the designer never changed the rule.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-759" title="EEVblog #759 - Mailbag">759</a></sup> Clearance around a meter's voltage input jack was judged too tight where the fill had been brought close in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1096" title="EEVblog #1096 - ANENG Q1 Multimeter Teardown (Now in 4K!)">1096</a></sup> A pour that breaks into isolated islands leaves dead copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup> Filled zones must sometimes be poured manually rather than existing as true planes, depending on the tool.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup> Via stitching joins pours on opposite layers, shortens returns instead of creating large loops, and lowers plane inductance; one via is a rough rule of thumb for around half an amp.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1034" title="EEVblog #1034 - Mailbag">1034</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-856" title="EEVblog #856 - Mailbag">856</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-643" title="EEVblog #643 - Mailbag">643</a></sup> Edge plating, where used, is almost always connected to ground.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> On flex circuits solid planes should be avoided and crosshatched fill used instead.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1262" title="EEVblog #1262 - Designing a Flex PCB + uSupply Update">1262</a></sup>

Errors in plane definition are expensive and easy to make. Assigning two zones the same net incorrectly shorts the planes together, and on a board carrying thousands of dollars of parts an unconnected or shorted plane found after assembly is a costly mistake.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup> A manufacturer that expanded the ground pour beyond the submitted Gerbers ate away an 8 thou trace entirely, breaking the board; the clearance submitted already met the 6 thou specification, and manufacturers should not modify Gerbers without written permission.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1193" title="EEVblog #1193 - KiCAD PCB 4 Layer Swapping &amp; Stackup">1193</a></sup>

## Thermal behaviour

Copper plane is [[thermal-mass]], and this dominates both assembly and rework. A pad tied to a large plane sucks heat away from the joint; if the other pad of the same component goes to a thin trace, the two ends reflow at different times and the part tombstones.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-127" title="EEVblog #127 - PCB Design For Manufacture Tutorial - Part 1">127</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup> The same differential can thermally stress and crack a ceramic capacitor.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1037" title="EEVblog #1037 - Solving Ceramic Capacitor Cracking">1037</a></sup> Thermal reliefs — a pad connected to the pour through four spurs rather than solidly — exist to let the pad heat faster than the surrounding copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup> Whole-board effects are just as real: an 8-layer, 3.2 mm board full of planes retains so much heat that a reflow profile written for 1.6 mm material caused widespread tombstoning and unsoldered joints, and heavily planed BGA boards demand a soak profile and cool slowly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VnrtneFP_6A" title="eevBLAB #51- Industry Story - Design Deadlines &amp; Unusual PCB Designs">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-104" title="EEVblog #104 - Live Show #3">104</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-860" title="EEVblog #860 - Mailbag">860</a></sup>

At the bench, the plane sets the demand on a [[soldering-iron]]. Irons with the tip loosely coupled to the element lag when heat drains into a plane, and because the tip is outside the control loop the sensor reaches setpoint before the tip does.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1064" title="EEVblog #1064 - Soldering Irons OLD vs NEW">1064</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1065" title="EEVblog #1065 - Soldering Iron Power Delivery Explained">1065</a></sup> A high-thermal-capacity iron and a large chisel tip are what make plane [[soldering]] practical; an 8 W USB iron cannot do it, and a nano iron with almost no tip mass is not intended to.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1113" title="EEVblog #1113 - Is a $5 USB Soldering Iron Useful?">1113</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QbeTv41l2EQ" title="JBC Nano Soldering Iron">•</a></sup> Temperature is raised to 370–400 °C for plane work such as soldering a wire to a plane or an RF can, while delicate parts are done as cool and as briefly as possible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1106" title="EEVblog #1106 - Pace ADS200 Soldering Station Review (JBC Killer?)">1106</a></sup> Hot-air rework on motherboards with large planes and large components can exceed a small station's air flow and heat capacity, and a preheater raising the whole board's temperature reduces the thermal shock when the hot air arrives.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1058" title="EEVblog #1058 - Quick 861DW Hot Air Rework Station Review">1058</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1154" title="EEVblog #1154 - Surprising 4K Dumpster TV Fault">1154</a></sup> Solder on a vertically mounted board with large exposed planes and no [[solder-mask]] runs downhill under gravity, so such boards must be laid flat.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1183" title="EEVblog #1183 - RIFA Madness (Schaffner Repair)">1183</a></sup>

The same mass is exploited deliberately. A TO-220 tab or a QFN thermal pad is soldered down to the plane so the board acts as the [[heat-sink]], with thermal vias carrying heat from the pad into the internal plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-183" title="EEVblog #183 - Soldering Tutorial Part 2">183</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-E1IqcGcZKHE" title="RPi CM4 Compute Module FAILURE Part 2: Thermal Boogaloo">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1322" title="EEVblog #1322 - JBL LSR308 Studio Monitor Speaker REPAIR">1322</a></sup> Thermal jumpers route device heat through copper, through the jumper, down vias and into the plane, using the whole board as a heat sink while occupying very little area.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1347" title="EEVblog #1347 - SMD Thermal Jumpers could be GAME CHANGING!">1347</a></sup> Plane copper spreads heat far enough that a pad remote from a hot component measured 35 °C and climbing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-401" title="EEVblog #401 - Lecroy 9384C Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">401</a></sup>

## Reading and probing planes

On a finished board the plane is visible and diagnostic. A dark patch under illumination indicates internal power and ground layers, and the number of visible layers with no corresponding surface traces establishes a minimum layer count.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-452" title="EEVblog #452 - Stanford Research SR430 Teardown">452</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-145" title="EEVblog #145 - Agilent LAN/VGA Module Teardown">145</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1415" title="EEVblog 1415 - Reverse Engineering the DP10007 Differential Probe">1415</a></sup> A large solid pour on an unmarked board is almost certainly ground and is the safest place to take a reference.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-932" title="EEVblog #932 - How Does A HV Differential Probe Work?">932</a></sup> Extensive via stitching together with flood fill and shielding indicates high-frequency circuitry.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-687" title="EEVblog #687 - EFTPOS PIN Pad Terminal Teardown">687</a></sup> A solid internal plane also defeats [[reverse-engineering]] by backlighting, since inner traces cannot be seen through it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-675" title="EEVblog #675 - How To Reverse Engineer A Rigol DS1054Z">675</a></sup>

For measurement, the plane is the ground return, and the quality of the connection to it sets probe [[bandwidth]]. Where no convenient ground point exists, the options are scraping back solder mask and soldering to the plane, soldering in a contact loop pin, or applying copper tape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-GS0WqUKZ-3c" title="PCBite Handsfree Oscilloscope Probe!">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-689" title="EEVblog #689 - Back To The Future Time Circuits Troubleshooting">689</a></sup> A short spring tip with a ground blade reaches around 2 GHz; a soldered copper pad gives a flexible ground point.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Y7t6BIhBZhc" title="High Frequency Active FET Probing DEMONSTRATED">•</a></sup> A shorter inductive path to the plane gives higher probe bandwidth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1718" title="EEVblog 1718 - Cheap 1GHz Oscilloscopes are Useless? ($5 DIY 1GHz Resistive Probe)">1718</a></sup> Where a board's designer has provided ground test points on the digital side but none on an isolated section, probing that section requires soldering something in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-660" title="EEVblog #660 - Electrocardiogram (ECG) Experiments">660</a></sup> A current probe traced over a plane shows current flowing under some regions and nothing at all under others, confirming that return current does not spread uniformly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-296" title="EEVblog #296 - AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 Current Probe Review">296</a></sup>

## Ground planes in EMC testing

The term also denotes the large metal sheet used as a reference in emissions testing rather than a feature of a board. For conducted-emissions pre-compliance, the device under test sits on a 5 cm polystyrene block above a ground plane, with the LISN connected to the same plane and a spectrum analyser also sitting on it — everything referenced to one plane, because a cable leaving the plane invalidates the setup.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup> A metal plate is required; an antistatic or static-dissipative mat is not adequate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup> The standards specify a horizontal and a vertical plane with defined dimensions and separations, the planes serving to stop capacitive coupling to other equipment in the room; at minimum the horizontal plane is used, and the vertical one is often omitted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> Such bench testing gives a ballpark indication of margin for a few hours' work rather than thousands of dollars of laboratory time, but the plane does not shield the setup from ambient fields such as [[led]] lighting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-548" title="EEVblog #548 - EMC Pre-Compliance Conducted Emissions Testing">548</a></sup> In an anechoic chamber the receive [[antenna]] is raised to 4 m precisely because of reflections off the ground plane.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-202" title="EMC RF Anechoic Test Facility Tour - EEVblog #202">202</a></sup>

## Historical practice

Before ground planes were routine, ground and power were run as traces between chips, with a bus down each side of the board branching to each device, and chips deliberately aligned in rows so the two traces could be routed underneath them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Boards of that era were laid out with one big plane and no controlled impedance because signal integrity at PCB level was not the concern it later became.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-410" title="EEVblog #410 - Microtek 8086 In-Circuit Emulator Teardown">410</a></sup> Double-sided boards were sometimes built specifically to obtain a plane on the top layer, even without plated-through holes, with component leads used as the through connections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-256" title="EEVblog #256 - Yamaha PSR-80 Keyboard Teardown">256</a></sup> The crinkled appearance of large pours on old boards comes from tin plating the bare copper before solder mask, rather than the modern practice of solder mask over bare copper.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-557" title="EEVblog #557 - Retro Sinclair ZX Spectrum Computer Teardown">557</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1258" title="EEVblog #1258 - MORE Dumpster Wandel And Goltermann!">1258</a></sup>
