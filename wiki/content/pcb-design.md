PCB design is the discipline of turning a verified [[schematic]] into a physical board: placing components, routing copper, setting clearances and thermal properties, and generating the manufacturing data a fabricator and assembler can build from. It sits in the middle of a fixed sequence — schematic capture, [[electrical-rules-checking|electrical rules checking]], [[pcb-layout|PCB layout]], [[design-rule-checking|design rule checking]], generation of manufacturing files, then assembly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1307" title="EEVblog #1307 - TUTORIAL: PCB BOM Consolidation">1307</a></sup> The same data then travels onward through prototype manufacture, in-circuit test and assembly, across a product development cycle in which every stage historically maintained its own file formats, some paper and some electronic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-349" title="EEVblog #349 - SMCBA Lecture IPC-2581 Open Standards for PCB Design Data">349</a></sup>

It is a specialisation in its own right rather than a step tacked onto circuit design. Competence requires sustained work on substantial boards; throwing a few timer chips onto a board does not confer it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-83" title="EEVblog #83 - Do You Suck At Hardware Or Software?">83</a></sup> It is one of the areas an electronics engineer can reasonably choose to specialise in, alongside embedded programming or electromechanical work.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-79" title="EEVblog #79 - Peak Oil Crisis &amp; Career Options">79</a></sup>

## The craft

Layout resists reduction to procedure. Experienced designers describe the sensation of a board resolving itself — after hundreds of components and thousands of traces, the last trace falling into place — and treat that as part of the art of PCB design, difficult to teach because it rests on experience and an innate feel for how the whole system fits together.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Laying out a board someone else designed is harder than laying out one's own.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-244" title="EEVblog #244 - How To Lay Out A PCB - PSU Design Part 9">244</a></sup> Thermal relief design on pads belongs to the same category of judgement: too much copper connection into a [[ground-plane|ground plane]] and one end of a component reflows before the other, tombstoning it off the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-415" title="EEVblog #415 - SMD Stencil Reflow Soldering Tutorial">415</a></sup>

The effort involved is substantial and largely invisible in the finished article. A four-layer micro-[[bga|BGA]] [[impedance]]-controlled board can represent four to six months of one person's work once first and second spins are accounted for, and a former professional layout designer can attest to the work and artistry which goes into boards like that.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2vJ0c0ioAXY" title="Electronex 2014 - Walkaround">•</a></sup> A twelve-layer board with fine-pitch BGAs, DDR3 memory and a quad video interface, built for safety equipment on mining trucks, took in excess of about 200 hours by its designer Chris Kulik's account — including research into DDR3 routing requirements — and could have used fewer layers had schedule and volume not argued otherwise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-795" title="EEVblog #795 - Electronex 2015">795</a></sup>

Layout is also iterative rather than single-pass. Even when every footprint and net is correct on the schematic and ERC passes clean, changes continue throughout, and there is sometimes value in beginning the PCB layout before the schematic is finished.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-974" title="EEVblog #974 - PCB Layout Walkthrough - PART 4">974</a></sup> A useful mental model when reading or drawing a board is that the designer is looking through the board, so the bottom side maps onto the top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1216" title="EEVblog #1216 - PCB Layout + FPGA Deep Dive">1216</a></sup>

## Design goals and constraints

A recurring aim in simpler work is minimum layer count with no wire links: single-sided with no links, or double-sided with no links on a complex board, without resorting to internal layers — described as the holy grail of PCB design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-353" title="EEVblog #353 - Mailbag">353</a></sup>

Trace and [[via]] sizing is set by acceptable temperature rise rather than by a hard [[current-limit|current limit]]. A via behaves essentially like a trace for this purpose, but with a less controlled manufacturing process. A default assumption of 10 °C rise is common; conservative practice reserves that only for genuinely high-power boards at the physical limits of achievable trace width, and otherwise designs to around 1 °C so that almost no power is dissipated in the copper — belt and braces type stuff.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-543" title="EEVBlog #543 - PCB VIA Current Investigation">543</a></sup> Trace widths are similarly graded rather than uniform: a fine width such as 4 thou may be needed to fan out from a dense package, then widened to something like 6 thou for general routing, since the finest width should not be used all over the board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup> The industry mixes millimetres and thousandths of an inch freely, so both units have to be juggled within a single design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-193" title="FPGA Implementation Tutorial - EEVblog #193">193</a></sup>

Clearance is governed by conductor spacing and creepage requirements, and [[solder-mask|solder mask]] changes the answer dramatically. Where a bare-copper spacing of 1.5 mm might be required at a given [[voltage]], solder mask — an excellent insulator — allows the same case to be met at 0.13 mm. Exposed pads at connectors do not benefit, since the mask is absent there.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1222" title="EEVblog #1222 - Apple&#x27;s MacBook Design FAIL - Who&#x27;s To Blame?">1222</a></sup>

## Design for the rest of the product

A board cannot be designed in isolation and thrown over the wall to the [[pcb|PCB]] person to lay out; it has to be integral with the 3D mechanical designers, the user interface people and marketing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-334" title="EEVblog #334 - History of PDA&#x27;s in Teardowns">334</a></sup> The failure mode when that does not happen is visible in finished instruments: a main board that has to be flexed to clear an obstruction because a small cutout was never added, the result of the PCB designer not talking to the housing designer, or of a design review that missed it and prototype builders who never fed the complaint back for the next revision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1664" title="EEVblog 1664 - REPAIR with Junk Bin Parts! Keithley 2302">1664</a></sup> Mechanical constraints propagate the other way too — a 9 V [[battery]] is thick enough that choosing it over AA cells forces a different board shape.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-ILIO5b1BliE" title="Why Use AA&#x27;s Instead of 9V Batteries in Multimeters?">•</a></sup> A large, flat enclosure is correspondingly a gift to the designer, permitting a single large flat PCB where a compact modern product would need rigid-flex.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-495" title="EEVblog #495 - World&#x27;s First DSLR Camera - Kodak DCS315 Teardown">495</a></sup>

[[silkscreen|Silkscreen]] and legend are part of the design, not decoration. Polarity marking for electrolytic capacitors should be placed outside the component outline rather than under the body, because once the part is fitted the marking is covered and can no longer be checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1714" title="EEVblog 1714 - Presonus Eris Speaker REPAIR (Capacitor Pop Noise)">1714</a></sup>

Procurement precedes layout. Parts should be ordered, and ideally held in stock, before the board is designed around them — taking the financial risk of buying early is preferable to laying out a board around a part that turns out to be unobtainable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1538" title="EEVblog 1538 - NEW PROJECT Part 2 - Microcontroller Selection">1538</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1539" title="EEVblog 1539 - NEW PROJECT Part 3 - STM32L vs PIC24F">1539</a></sup>

## Cost and intent visible in the artefact

Because layout decisions are frozen into copper, a finished board records what its designers intended. Bare-PCB-level cost decisions can be read directly: copper deliberately left off under shield locations, and no additional holes for metalwork, show that the [[shielding]] requirement was understood as inter-channel coupling from the outset rather than discovered in test.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Shield pads designed into the copper indicate the shield was part of the layout rather than a post-compliance hack.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-271" title="EEVblog #271 - Zoom H1 Recorder Teardown">271</a></sup> Silkscreen text naming the colour of levers to be fitted later shows the mechanical decision was already made at the PCB design stage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1237" title="EEVblog #1237 - Old School Teardown: HP3785A Jitter Analyser">1237</a></sup> A white line separating isolated halves of a source-measure unit documents the isolation barrier on the board itself.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-607" title="EEVblog #607 - Agilent B2912A Source Measure Unit SMU Teardown">607</a></sup> Even between revisions of the same product, components shift by small amounts — turned to a different angle and shuffled aside — to open room for one extra trace.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1510" title="EEVblog 1510 - $699 Rigol 12bit HDO1000 Teardown - WOAH!">1510</a></sup> Deliberate obstruction is equally legible: masking over [[connector]] pins so they are not connected at all is a PCB-design-level decision.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1181" title="EEVblog #1181 - Car ECO OBD2 OptiFuel Fuel Saver SCAM!">1181</a></sup>

Errors are just as durable. A board too narrow at one point to carry a required trace has to be fixed with a bodge link, which cannot be corrected without changing the physical layout.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1667" title="EEVblog 1667 - Reverse Engineering the Brymen BM2257 Multimeter LowZ Mode">1667</a></sup> Bodge wires are common in professional gear where boards take many years and countless engineering hours to design and a full respin is unattractive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-738" title="EEVblog #738 - Yamaha DME32 Digital Mixer Teardown">738</a></sup> Fabrication defects compound the problem: a broken track and excessive clearance around pads on a cheap prototype run can screw up a design completely, and subtle [[ground]] problems follow when [[current]] does not flow along the intended path — which is an argument for thorough inspection of bare boards before assembly rather than a quick glance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-155" title="EEVblog #155 - ITead Studio PCB Prototype Goof">155</a></sup>

## Tools

Board layout began as a manual craft. Rubylith artwork was cut by hand; an intermediate generation drew the board in pencil and then entered it into a computer with a large digitizer, because graphics hardware was not yet good enough for direct on-screen editing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 2 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Modern packages perform design rule checking against entered constraints — safety spacings, transmission lines, ampacities for every track.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 2 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> The schematic side matters to layout because the netlist is what crosses over: components that appear connected but sit fractions of a millimetre off grid produce no electrical connection and therefore an incorrect netlist, which propagates into the PCB and invalidates design rule checking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1129" title="EEVblog #1129 - Creating a Nice Readable Schematic">1129</a></sup>

Professional practice has long centred on integrated schematic-plus-layout suites, with Protel for DOS and later Altium representing a two-decade continuity for some designers, and hotkey fluency being a large part of layout speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-747" title="EEVblog #747 - PC Based Logic Analyser Project">747</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup> KiCad is the open-source alternative, split into separate modules for schematic capture, layout and [[gerber|Gerber]] viewing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-253" title="EEVblog #253 - KiCAD Install &amp; Schematic - First Impressions">253</a></sup> Free professional-grade options appeared with Altium's CircuitMaker, released without layer, size or connection limits.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-754" title="EEVblog #754 - Altium Circuit Maker First Impressions">754</a></sup> Around 2007–2008 Altium's own strategy briefly assumed custom board design would become unnecessary — that engineers would assemble products from purchased [[fpga|FPGA]] modules — to the point that the PCB module was sold as an optional extra on a $4,300 development board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VnrtneFP_6A" title="eevBLAB #51- Industry Story - Design Deadlines &amp; Unusual PCB Designs">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-500" title="EEVblog #500 - Live Show">500</a></sup>

Data interchange has been a parallel problem. IPC-2581 was developed to replace the proliferation of per-stage formats with a single two-way data highway covering design, fabrication, assembly and test, defining a common viewing convention and a standard dictionary of primitives such as contours, rounded rectangles and corner types.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-349" title="EEVblog #349 - SMCBA Lecture IPC-2581 Open Standards for PCB Design Data">349</a></sup>

## Learning it

Dedicated instructional material was scarce before online video; a three-part PCB design article published in Silicon Chip Magazine and later posted online circulated widely and became a route into the subject for many designers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-riBwRC_CaAA" title="UNSW Talk - How I Became The EEVblog Guy">•</a></sup> Books that teach a specific CAD package are of limited value because they explain the software rather than how to lay out a board,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-546" title="EEVblog #546 - Mailbag Monday">546</a></sup> and no good beginner-level PCB design book has been identified.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-107" title="EEVblog #107 - Live Show #4">107</a></sup> The gap is filled instead by hour-long walkthroughs of designing a board with hints and tips along the way, plus material on manufacturing options, Gerber generation, panelization and design for manufacturing.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Ep4r-wD7PPs" title="KiCAD PCB Design">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1259" title="EEVblog #1259 - PCB Manufacturing Options EXPLAINED">1259</a></sup>
