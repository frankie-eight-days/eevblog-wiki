A cooling fan is the forced-air element of a thermal design: a motor-driven impeller that pulls ambient air through an enclosure and across heat sinks so that dissipated power leaves the case faster than natural convection allows. In benchtop test equipment, power supplies, computers and consumer electronics it is usually the only moving part, and it is simultaneously the component that determines whether the instrument survives full load and the component that determines whether it is tolerable to sit next to. Its two design axes — [[airflow]] path and acoustic [[noise]] — are frequently in tension, and the way a manufacturer resolves that tension is one of the clearest signals of the care taken over the rest of the product.

## Thermal necessity

Where a fan is fitted, it is generally load-bearing rather than precautionary. A 12-bit [[oscilloscope]] of slim form factor, run in still lab air with its fan disconnected, passed just over 70 °C internally, rebooted, hung on its splash screen and began to smell before it could be shut down; with the fan running and an ambient of roughly 24 °C, the same measurement point sat at 39.4 °C overnight.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-pwS6HnM9PDo" title="The Rigol DHO800 Fan(boys) Go Wild">•</a></sup> Another oscilloscope operated with its case off logged an over-temperature event and shut itself down within a couple of minutes, having already warned that internal temperature was approaching a damaging point and that the fans and clearance should be checked.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> A scope under repair with its fan unpowered produced an input-termination overheat message purely from lack of cooling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-565" title="EEVblog #565 - Tektronix TDS3054 Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">565</a></sup>

The temperatures involved justify the hardware. An ADC [[asic|ASIC]] in a high-end scope was measured at 84 °C and still climbing toward 85 °C, with a companion chipset near 74 °C and analog front ends too hot to keep a hand on.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1124" title="EEVblog #1124 - Rigol 7000 Oscilloscope Teardown">1124</a></sup> A linear bench supply's output [[transistor]] reached 100 °C at worst case, within margin but only with the fan running at full tilt.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup> A fifteen-minute NiMH charger cannot work without one: an 8 A charge rate to 70% capacity in fifteen minutes makes the cells themselves the heat source, so the fan draws air in from the bottom past the contacts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-811" title="EEVblog #811 - How The Varta 15 Minute Battery Charger Works">811</a></sup> and its presence forces the design to sense each cell individually rather than relying on a single in-case sensor, since the forced air destroys any correlation between case air and cell temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-35" title="EEVblog #35 - Inside the Varta 15 minute NiMH Battery Charger">35</a></sup>

## Airflow architecture

The canonical arrangement draws cool air in through vents on one face, directly over the finned heat sinks carrying the power devices, and exhausts it from the opposite face.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1023" title="EEVblog #1023 - Rigol DL3021 Electronic Load Teardown">1023</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-800" title="EEVblog #800 - Siglent 1000X Oscilloscope Teardown">800</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-828" title="EEVblog #828 - Siglent SPD3303X Precision Lab PSU Teardown">828</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Placing the inlet vents low, so the incoming stream is drawn across the board and over the heat sinks before leaving through the rear exhaust fan, is better than venting at the top.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-864" title="EEVblog #864 - Siglent SDS2000X Series Oscilloscope Teardown">864</a></sup> An [[electronic-load|electronic load]] teardown showed the principle duplicated: each [[mosfet|MOSFET]] [[heat-sink|heat sink]] had its own fan pulling air from its own vent holes through its own fin stack and out the back independently.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-281" title="EEVblog #281 - BK Precision 8500 Electronic Load Teardown">281</a></sup>

Fin orientation is part of the same system. Fins must run along the flow direction; rotating them so the air meets the flat faces produces turbulent flow and destroys the effectiveness of the arrangement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup> In one slim scope the fan pulls through the fin stack directly, and that [[efficiency]] is what permits the reduced depth.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1717" title="EEVblog 1717 - Rigol&#x27;s INSANE New $999 350MHz Oscilloscope: TEARDOWN">1717</a></sup> Multiple fans may be spread evenly across a large board set rather than concentrated at one end.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1289" title="EEVblog #1289 - Mystery Huawei Teardown">1289</a></sup> Stacking instruments reduces fan efficiency slightly but need not block intake, because the feet maintain clearance for air to be drawn in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1173" title="EEVblog #1173 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz Power Supply Bonanza">1173</a></sup>

Failures of that architecture are common enough to be recognisable. One scope drew air from a rear vent and blew it straight into the shield can of the front end, where it could only reflect and exit again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-824" title="EEVblog #824 - GW Instek GDS-1000B Oscilloscope Teardown">824</a></sup> A bench [[multimeter]]'s inlet path was obstructed by the mains [[transformer]] sitting in front of the vents.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-829" title="EEVblog #829 - Siglent SDM3055 Bench Multimeter Teardown">829</a></sup> In a 7.5-digit meter the fan sat hard against the back of the case with no usable path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> A logic-analysis instrument with the fan mounted at the very top could only circulate air inside the lid rather than exchange it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e98cYNPUxcg" title="Fluke PM3370B Solder Joint Inspection">•</a></sup> Two instruments from the same manufacturer, sharing an identical fan brand and model, had them fitted in opposite directions — one intake, one exhaust — with no evident reason.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1431" title="EEVblog 1431 - Keysight EDU33212A Function Generator Teardown">1431</a></sup> A self-built PC ran for some time with its cooler fan installed backwards.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1098" title="EEVblog #1098 - 4K Video Editing PC Build">1098</a></sup>

## Sizing and noise

Small fans must spin fast to move useful air, and speed is what produces the whine. A 45 mm frameless fan of the type once common on graphics cards is audible and high-pitched, and no small fan of that class will be genuinely silent no matter how it is swapped; reaching silence means moving to a 100 mm fan on the rear panel, at the cost of depth and of interference with VESA mounting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zoyaHOqp9gI" title="Rigol DHO800 Oscilloscope Fan Upgrade Experiment">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1566" title="EEVblog 1566 - Rigol DHO800 12 Bit Oscilloscope FULL REVIEW">1566</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1563" title="EEVblog 1563 - New $389 12bit Rigol DHO800 Scope TEARDOWN">1563</a></sup> The same logic applies to a 2 kW supply brick whose small fan was already loud at 150 W, an order of magnitude below rating: a larger fan turning slower would have been the better choice.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup>

A quiet fan is treated as a first-order specification rather than a refinement — the most underrated feature of any benchtop test equipment is a quiet fan.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> Large fans, sometimes angled to exhaust out the side, are audible only as a low hum or not at all;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1545" title="EEVblog 1545 - R&amp;S MXO4 TEARDOWN. The World&#x27;s Fastest Oscilloscope!">1545</a></sup> two-fan dual-exhaust arrangements and small high-speed units are the ones that intrude in a quiet room.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1503" title="EEVblog 1503 - Rigol HDO4000 12bit Oscilloscope TEARDOWN">1503</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-480" title="EEVblog #480 - 300MHz Owon SDS Oscilloscope">480</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1" title="EEVblog #1 - Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope Review">1</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup> Bearing quality is audible independently of size: a rumble suggests a lower-grade bearing,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1220" title="EEVblog #1220 - Siglent SDS5000X 1GHz Oscilloscope Review">1220</a></sup> and a rattle or the noisy ball-bearing character indicates wear or a loose mounting.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-474" title="EEVblog #474 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Series Oscilloscope Unboxing &amp; First Impression">474</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-lXvXBdTP7Tg" title="Haasoscope Pro - An Oscilloscope Space Oddity">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1709" title="EEVblog 1709 - Haasoscope Pro USB Oscilloscope: An Oscilloscope Oddity">1709</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1279" title="EEVblog #1279 - Best Dumpster PC Find Yet!">1279</a></sup> Mechanical decoupling addresses part of the problem — plasma television chassis used shock-compliant fan mounts and foam surrounds specifically to reduce transmitted noise,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-725" title="EEVblog #725 - LG Plasma TV Teardown">725</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-52" title="EEVblog #52 - Panasonic Plasma TV&#x27;s Suck (and a Teardown)">52</a></sup> whereas a basic rigid mounting with no compliance is a visible cost saving.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>

## Speed control and start-up behaviour

Temperature-controlled fans are widespread, cycling with load rather than running at a fixed speed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1279" title="EEVblog #1279 - Best Dumpster PC Find Yet!">1279</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1280" title="EEVblog #1280 - Dumpster Lab PC Upgrade">1280</a></sup> A bench supply increases fan flow rate as the main heat sink warms, with the side effect of cooling adjacent heat sinks that are not themselves the load;<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-512" title="EEVblog #512 - Rigol DP832 Bad Design Investigation">512</a></sup> the same family of supply audibly speeds up at power-on and then slows once running, rather than being fixed at full speed in [[firmware]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-549" title="EEVblog #549 - Rigol DP832 Lab Power Supply Followup">549</a></sup> Full speed at boot is a deliberate strategy: with the environment unknown before the system has come up, the fan is run flat out until temperature data is available.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-nO09bc5ozng" title="NEW Tektronix 2 Series MSO Oscilloscope FIRST REACTION">•</a></sup> The cost is a loud turn-on transient that persists until the [[processor]] has booted.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1402" title="EEVblog 1402 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz NGA100 PSU Teardown + GIVEAWAY">1402</a></sup> Once running, a well-controlled fan spins up briefly and settles to a low-level hum.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-587" title="EEVblog #587 - Tektronix MDO3000 Mixed Domain Oscilloscope Teardown">587</a></sup> Not all designs stop the fan when there is nothing to cool: one supply ran its fan continuously with no load at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-315" title="EEVblog #315 - Korad KA3005P Review/FAIL">315</a></sup> Conversely, a low-power standby mode may exist largely to stop the fan,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Fx3YixoQS6E" title="Improving the Rigol MHO98 Boot Time + Low Power Mode">•</a></sup> and mini-PCs stop theirs entirely when idle or powered down.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1739" title="EEVblog 1739 - UNUSUAL FAULT! in a Beelink Ryzen 9 Mini PC">1739</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-p287qKMXqxQ" title="Lenovo Legion 5i 17&quot; Ryzen 7 RTX2060 Laptop">•</a></sup>

Post-shutdown run-on is a related control behaviour: a hot-air rework station cuts the heating element but keeps the blower running until the sensed temperature falls to 100 °C.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-167" title="EEVblog #167 - Atten 858D Hot Air Rework Review">167</a></sup>

## Fan presence as a monitored condition

A fan can be part of the boot interlock. A 500 MHz oscilloscope repeatedly cycle-booted and flashed a diagnostic code that the service flowchart attributed to the processor board; the actual cause was the disconnected fan. The connection is only two pins with no tachometer wire, so the detection must be some form of load sensing on the supply, and the fan plugs into the processor board rather than the [[power-supply|power supply]] board.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1515" title="EEVblog 1515 - Dumpster Tektronix TDS540D 500MHz Oscilloscope LCD Upgrade">1515</a></sup>

## Retrofits, service and dust

Because most fans are standard sizes, replacement with a quieter modern unit is a routine modification, and a free-standing fan not integrated into a casting or heat pipe is trivially swapped.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-591" title="EEVblog #591 - Agilent 54622D Retro Mixed Signal Osciloscope Review &amp; Teardown">591</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-655" title="EEVblog #655 - Auction Score">655</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-703" title="EEVblog #703 - Rigol DS1054Z Oscilloscope Review Summary">703</a></sup> Where the thermal load is small enough — a processor with a TDP of roughly 30 W — a passive heat sink can replace the fan entirely, though it may require custom engineering.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Pq8ncblgqss" title="Dumpster Intel Atom Embedded PC update">•</a></sup>

Dust is the dominant service issue. Fans in laptops and desktops accumulate enough debris to lose most of their effectiveness while the fan itself remains mechanically sound, with no broken blades and a bearing that still spins silently; cleaning it and renewing the thermal compound restores normal behaviour, and the resulting change is audible as the fan no longer running at full blast on boot.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1345" title="EEVblog #1345 - HP DV7 Laptop Repair">1345</a></sup> The cleaning is done outdoors rather than indoors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1345" title="EEVblog #1345 - HP DV7 Laptop Repair">1345</a></sup> Genuine fan failure also occurs, as motor windings that no longer engage at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1280" title="EEVblog #1280 - Dumpster Lab PC Upgrade">1280</a></sup>

## Fan as afterthought

Several designs betray a fan added late. One power analyser had its fan wired with a red and a black lead bodge-soldered directly onto the leads of the axial main output filter capacitors, where a design that had planned for a fan from the start would have carried a two-pin [[connector]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-589" title="EEVblog #589 - Voltech PM300 Power Analyser Teardown">589</a></sup> A monitor repair found a small fan hot-glued in place with its wires bodged across the main filter capacitors.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1202" title="EEVblog #1202 - LCD Repair Redux">1202</a></sup> In a 7.5-digit multimeter the fan reads as tacked on as an afterthought to the whole design, with no proper path and no acoustic consideration.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-731" title="EEVblog #731 - Keithley DMM7510 7.5 Digit Multimeter Teardown">731</a></sup> The counter-example is a supply in which a second fan, added to move air over the main switching transformer, was held by a soldered metal bracket on the main board — mechanical integration that indicates a deliberate late-stage decision rather than a bodge.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1691" title="EEVblog 1691 - Uni-T UDP6731 360W Bench PSU REVIEW">1691</a></sup> A television power supply board carried a dedicated fan supply rail and labelled fan connectors that were simply left unpopulated for the model in question.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-446" title="EEVblog #446 - Dumpster Diving Samsung Plasma TV">446</a></sup>

## Designs that omit the fan

A fan is not universally desirable. A precision bench multimeter deliberately has none: the instrument is rated for around 30 W maximum and the airflow itself is the objection, because drawing air through side vents and out again creates thermal gradients across the board and over sensitive parts, the [[voltage-reference|voltage reference]] in particular.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-426" title="EEVBlog #426 - HP 3457A Multimeter Teardown">426</a></sup> Comparable low-dissipation instruments are fanless outright, which is a point of comparison against otherwise similar equipment that is not.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1" title="EEVblog #1 - Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope Review">1</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kdjpbWLi7UI" title="Full Review of the Rigol DS1052E  Oscilloscope">•</a></sup> The same threshold appears in power conversion: the 5 kW member of an [[inverter]] family is passively cooled, while the 8 kW top model carries two fans.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-kKfZgAyFu8Q" title="Solis Hybrid Inverter">•</a></sup> Where a small regulator is put into a sealed case, adding a fan and vent holes becomes necessary once dissipation reaches the tens of watts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1030" title="EEVblog #1030 - $20 DIY Bench Power Supply!">1030</a></sup>

## Beyond enclosure cooling

Fans also serve as process tools rather than enclosure exhaust. A 3D printer's part-cooling blower feeds a duct wider than the print head so that airflow crosses the whole deposited layer, switchable per filament type in software,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-jFapDuowXKU" title="Mailbag: Creality K1 Max 3D Printer: Unboxing + Setup + First Print">•</a></sup> and a pair of 50 mm fans mounted over a heated bed exists to bring the bed temperature back down quickly, the cool-down being the slow half of the cycle.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-520" title="EEVblog #520 - Michael&#x27;s 3D Printer">520</a></sup> Extruder hot ends carry their own heat sink and fan.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-843" title="EEVblog #843 - David&#x27;s rPrint 3D Printer Design">843</a></sup> Low-noise 12 V fans are used at the bench simply to blow [[solder]] fumes away from the work, where a full extractor would be too loud.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-555" title="EEVblog #555 - 555 Timer Kit">555</a></sup> Forced air is also used to cool components that carry no heat sink at all, such as banks of ceramic power resistors in an electronic load, where the fan alone provides the entire thermal path.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-SoeVNaz7KGc" title="EEVsmoke #2 - Magic Smoke Captured!">•</a></sup>
