A function generator is a bench test instrument that produces standard electrical [[waveform|waveforms]] — sine, square, triangle, ramp, pulse, [[noise]], and DC — at user-selected [[frequency]], amplitude, and [[dc-offset|DC offset]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup> It is one of the fundamental pieces of laboratory equipment, providing the stimulus half of stimulus-and-measure work: any bench that has an [[oscilloscope]] also needs a way of generating basic signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup> Entry-level capability starts with kit instruments costing as little as $15 that produce basic sine, square, and triangle waveforms by DDS, while high-performance arbitrary waveform generators can cost tens of thousands of dollars.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>

## Analog versus DDS architectures

Function generators come in two architectural types: analog designs that generate the waveform with analog circuitry, and direct digital synthesis (DDS) designs that generate the signal digitally and convert it with a digital-to-analog converter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> The DDS type is the recommended choice for a general lab instrument, and nearly all modern function generators are DDS-based.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup> This distinguishes them from RF signal generators, which are proper analog oscillators — for example spanning 9 kHz to 1.5 GHz — rather than DAC-based waveform synthesizers.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-823" title="EEVBlog #823 - Rigol DSG815 RF Signal Generator Teardown">823</a></sup>

DDS is inexpensive but has characteristic weaknesses: it exhibits jitter, and square waves in particular suffer badly from jitter in DDS implementations. As one Keysight engineer put it, "Square waves are horrendous with jitter in DDS technology."<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> Keysight's Trueform architecture was developed to address these problems and to simplify the internal structure of the generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> At the high end, even the instrument's own [[power-supply|power supply]] becomes a signal-integrity problem: in one high-end Keysight generator, switching power supplies feeding the high-power ASICs created spurs in the output at 120 MHz, requiring a power-supply redesign to reduce the noise.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup>

In practice, the waveform synthesis hardware takes many forms depending on instrument class. Oscilloscope-integrated generators are commonly implemented in an [[fpga|FPGA]] with op-amps providing the analog output drive.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-842" title="EEVblog #842 - Rohde &amp; Schwarz HMO1202 Oscilloscope Teardown">842</a></sup> Low-cost instruments may use a [[microcontroller]]'s internal DAC — a 4 megasample-per-second update-rate DAC in a GigaDevice MCU suffices for a basic 10 MHz square/triangle/sine source<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1723" title="EEVblog 1723 - AlienTek DM40 Multimeter/Oscilloscope Review">1723</a></sup> — or even a discrete R2R ladder network driven by a PIC32.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup> Keysight integrates the waveform generation into its MegaZoom oscilloscope ASICs rather than using a separate DAC.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1639" title="EEVblog 1639 - Keysight InfiniiVision HD3 Oscilloscope Teardown">1639</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup>

## Output stage, termination, and amplitude behavior

Function generators have a 50-ohm output [[impedance]], and proper use demands attention to termination. Instruments offer selectable output load settings — high impedance or 50 ohms — and the setting must match the actual load.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup> For accurate amplitude measurement, the high-impedance output setting should be used, because with 50-ohm termination the accuracy of the load, the divider formed with the source impedance, and the cabling all enter the error budget.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>

Feeding a generator through unterminated [[coaxial-cable|coaxial cable]] produces transmission-line reflections that make the measured amplitude vary strongly with frequency; at particular frequencies the reflected wave cancels the incident wave right at the generator output, and the signal at the oscilloscope can nearly vanish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> An apparent amplitude-versus-frequency problem in such a setup is therefore usually a measurement-technique error, not a faulty generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup> Conversely, the 50-ohm output impedance is too high to drive low-impedance devices directly — a curve-tracer application, for instance, requires an op-amp buffer stage after the generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> A function generator output also cannot drive heavy loads such as a 24-volt [[solenoid]] directly; a power [[transistor]] stage is needed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-87E3wqfxHqg" title="Dodgy Silicone Test Leads!">•</a></sup>

Output amplitude is not perfectly flat over frequency. A typical specification is ±0.5 dB amplitude flatness over the full range for a 20 MHz class generator<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup>; a better unit may hold within about 0.4 dB<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-652" title="EEVblog #652 - Oscilloscope &amp; Function Generator Termination Demo">652</a></sup>, but the working rule is to never assume constant output amplitude over frequency and to verify it during swept measurements.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-572" title="EEVblog #572 - Cascading Opamps For Increased Bandwidth">572</a></sup> Accuracy also degrades at very small amplitudes: a generator set to 10 mV peak-to-peak may actually deliver 4.7 mV at the low end of its range<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup>, and generators with a fixed full-scale output (e.g., 2.5 V) produce noisy, erroneous waveforms when asked for tiny signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup> Care is also required when injecting signals into circuitry carrying DC: connecting a generator directly to a node sitting at several volts DC can feed that [[voltage]] back into the generator's output stage, so [[ac-coupling|AC coupling]] through a series [[capacitor]] is the safe approach.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-966" title="EEVblog #966 - CPM700 Counter Surveillance Monitor Repair">966</a></sup>

## Modes and auxiliary features

Beyond continuous waveforms, function generators provide burst mode, [[frequency-sweep|frequency sweep]], and modulation. Burst mode generates a train of cycles followed by a dead period, useful for exciting resonant devices such as glass delay lines with pulses near their 4.43 MHz resonance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-386" title="EEVblog #386 - Glass Delay Lines Part 2">386</a></sup> Sweep capability drives applications like vibration testing, where a 30-second logarithmic sweep from 10 Hz to 200 Hz can be programmed for a shaker table.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1443" title="EEVblog 1443 - They Don&#x27;t Teach This in School! (Coherence)">1443</a></sup> Amplitude, frequency, and pulse-width modulation are standard on mid-range and better instruments<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup>, as are symmetry and DC-offset controls.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup>

Most generators provide a sync or trigger output: a pulse generated at the start of each burst or cycle, with selectable polarity, which gives an oscilloscope a clean trigger reference and is the preferred fix for trigger-jitter problems when measuring burst signals.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-387" title="EEVblog #387 - Oscilloscope Trigger Jitter">387</a></sup> Some oscilloscopes can similarly reconfigure their built-in generator's auxiliary output as a trigger output for waveform-update-rate measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1478" title="EEVblog 1478 - Waveform Update Rate Shootout - Tek 2 Series vs Others">1478</a></sup> Some generators also include a hardware [[frequency-counter|frequency counter]] input; the Rigol DG4000 series implements reciprocal counting, giving high resolution even at low frequencies, though the function is considered an odd fit for the instrument class.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-325" title="EEVblog #325 - Rigol DG4162 Voice">325</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-456" title="EEVblog #456 - CSIRO Rubidium Frequency Standard">456</a></sup>

## Bench applications

Component and curve testing. A function generator driving an oscilloscope in XY mode forms a simple component tester: a circle or ellipse indicates a capacitor or [[inductor]], an L-shaped curve a [[diode]], a sloped straight line a [[resistor]] (slope indicating value), a vertical line a [[short-circuit|short circuit]], and a horizontal line an open circuit.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> Unlike a fixed-frequency component tester, this arrangement lets the component be characterized at any desired test frequency.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1137" title="EEVblog #1137 - Mailbag Monday">1137</a></sup> The same generator-plus-series-resistor topology is the internal operating principle of LCR meters: the generator drives the device under test through a known [[series-resistor|series resistor]], and the resulting voltage is amplified and digitized.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-81" title="EEVblog #81 - Smart Tweezers LCR Meter Review And Teardown">81</a></sup>

[[frequency-response|Frequency response]] measurement. A generator swept in frequency, with output amplitude read on a [[multimeter]] or oscilloscope, yields a frequency-response plot without any specialized instrument.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-396" title="EEVblog #396 - Bode Plotting on Your Osciloscope">396</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-620" title="EEVblog #620 - Repair: Stanford Research SR650 Programmable Filter">620</a></sup> Combining a function generator with an oscilloscope in a network-analyzer arrangement — especially with 14-bit resolution on both sides — produces magnitude and phase Bode plots directly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup> Oscilloscopes with built-in generators increasingly offer this as a one-button Bode plot feature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-N63dJHbCdbk" title="Sneak Peek: NEW Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>

EMC and [[troubleshooting]]. A generator is a standard tool for recreating interference problems: a 5–10 MHz [[square-wave|square wave]] at 10 V peak-to-peak applied near a multimeter's [[current]] input reproduced a conducted common-mode susceptibility fault in the Keysight U1272A.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-933" title="EEVblog #933 - Keysight U1272A EMC Issue">933</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-987" title="EEVblog #987 - Keysight U1272A Multimeter EMC Followup">987</a></sup>

Electromechanical drive. Low-frequency sine output drives voice-coil-type actuators such as hard-disk head positioners ([[bandwidth]] around a kilohertz or two), camera lens motors at 1–2 Hz, and shaker tables [[via]] a power amplifier.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-395" title="EEVblog #395 - World&#x27;s Most Expensive Hard Drive Teardown">395</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-849" title="EEVblog #849 - Sony E-Mount Camera Lens Teardown">849</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1442" title="EEVblog 1442 - DON&#x27;T DO THIS! (PCB Vibration Testing)">1442</a></sup>

Oscilloscope characterization. A 1 kHz square wave with a fast edge from a generator substitutes for a high-frequency source when checking [[oscilloscope-bandwidth|oscilloscope bandwidth]] via [[rise-time|rise time]]<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-196" title="Tektronix 2225 Analog Oscilloscope - EEVblog #196">196</a></sup>; a 1 kHz triangle wave into a high-impedance input reveals ADC clipping for dynamic-range measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6qjqhnQiQXQ" title="How to Measure Oscilloscope ADC Dynamic Range">•</a></sup>

## Integration into other instruments

Oscilloscope-integrated waveform generators began with the Agilent 2000 X-Series, whose [[asic|ASIC]] included a 20 MHz generator producing sine, square, ramp, pulse, DC, and noise, with 0.1 Hz to 20 MHz range, ±0.5 dB flatness, −40 dB (1%) harmonic distortion, 500 ps square-wave jitter, and 18 ns rise/fall time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-143" title="EEVblog #143 - Agilent 2000 X Series Infiniivision Oscilloscope Review">143</a></sup> The original implementation conspicuously lacked arbitrary-waveform capability; it was added later as a free [[firmware]] upgrade.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-209" title="New Agilent 3000X Oscilloscope Firmware - EEVblog #209">209</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1032" title="EEVblog #1032 Part 1 - John Kenny Keysight Interview">1032</a></sup> The feature then propagated across the industry: the Keysight 1000 X-Series carries a 20 MHz generator in a $450–700 scope<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-976" title="EEVblog #976 - Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope Teardown">976</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-N63dJHbCdbk" title="Sneak Peek: NEW Keysight 1000 X-Series Oscilloscope">•</a></sup>, the Siglent SDS2000X Plus a 50 MHz generator enabling Bode plots<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1309" title="EEVblog #1309 - Siglent SDS2000X Plus Scope Teardown+Hack">1309</a></sup>, the Tektronix 2 Series a 50 MHz generator plus a four-channel digital pattern generator<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qE-SwtYQM6E" title="Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope Pattern Generator">•</a></sup>, and the Rohde & Schwarz MXO4 a 100 MHz built-in generator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1529" title="EEVblog 1529 - R&amp;S MXO4 12bit Oscilloscope Unboxing and Experiments">1529</a></sup>

Integrated generators carry real limitations, however. The GW Instek GDS-2000A has two generator BNCs on the front panel, but the manual states only one generator module can be used at a time.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-475" title="EEVblog #475 - GW Instek GDS-2000A Oscilloscope Teardown">475</a></sup> On the Moku:Go, opening the oscilloscope instrument switches off a running waveform generator, and the generator lacks a selectable 50-ohm/high-impedance load setting entirely.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1701" title="EEVblog 1701 - $599 Australian Made Moku:Go Oscilloscope REVIEW">1701</a></sup> Generators built into ultra-cheap handheld scope/multimeters are frequently non-functional in practice: the function generator in the Zoyi ZT-703S is described as useless, and the signal source in the FNIRSI DPOX180H has a non-usable pin output and is best treated as absent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1597" title="EEVblog 1597 - Zoyi ZT-703S $80 2CH 50MHz Oscilloscope/Multimeter Review">1597</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1582" title="EEVblog 1582 - FNIRSI DPOX180H 180MHz DPO for $123?">1582</a></sup>

Function generators also appear inside instruments of other classes. The Keithley 2015 THD multimeter contains a low-distortion audio-frequency generator on rear-panel BNCs to support automated total-harmonic-distortion measurement.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-478" title="EEVblog #478 - Keithley 2015 THD Multimeter Teardown">478</a></sup> Some multimeters provide simple generator or square-wave outputs<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-184" title="EEVblog #184 - Open Hardware Multimeter Concept">184</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1007" title="EEVblog #1007 - Is a $25 Multimeter Any Good?">1007</a></sup>, and [[usb|USB]] all-in-one instruments build them in alongside scopes and supplies: the Digilent Analog Discovery pairs a 14-bit, 100 MS/s generator with a 14-bit scope front end<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-692" title="EEVblog #692 - Digilent Analog Discovery Review">692</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-464" title="EEVblog #464 - Mailbag">464</a></sup>, the OpenScope MZ offers a basic generator limited to 3 V peak-to-peak with ±1.5 V offset<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup>, and the NI VirtualBench provides sine, square, triangle, DC, and arbitrary waveforms.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-876" title="EEVblog #876 - NI VirtualBench Review">876</a></sup> Retro combination instruments such as the BWD 603B MiniLab integrated a 2 MHz analog generator with symmetry, offset, TTL output, and AM/FM modulation alongside an oscilloscope and supplies.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1724" title="EEVblog 1724 - BWD 603B MiniLab RETRO TEARDOWN">1724</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1716" title="EEVblog 1716 - University Dumpster Diving: Kikusui Oscilloscope">1716</a></sup>

## Performance range

The price-to-performance span is extreme. Kit generators cost about $15 and produce basic low-frequency waveforms<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-954" title="EEVblog #954 - How To Setup An Electronics Lab For $300">954</a></sup>; a new analog 3 MHz instrument such as the Instek GFG8219A represents the traditional entry-level bench unit<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5" title="EEVblog #5 - Maxim product marketing, Function Generator Review">5</a></sup>, and usable generators were available around $150.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-168" title="EEVblog #168 - How To Set Up An Electronics Lab">168</a></sup> Mid-range DDS arbitrary generators such as the Siglent SDG5082 provide 80 MHz bandwidth at 500 MS/s for a four-figure price.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-497" title="EEVBlog #497 - Siglent SDG5000 Function Generator Teardown">497</a></sup> At the top of the general-purpose market, the Uni-T UTG9504T offers four channels at 500 MHz bandwidth and 2.5 GS/s for nearly $6,000 — though only channels 1 and 2 achieve the full 500 MHz (at 14-bit resolution), while channels 3 and 4 are limited to 200 MHz at 625 MS/s, albeit at 16 bits; its closest competitor is the two-channel Siglent SDG6000 series.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-fM4bbKVw2og" title="Uni-T 500MHz 4CH ARB Signal Generator Unboxing">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-cziiWo6Uh5M" title="$5000 Uni-T UTG9504T 500MHz Function Generator Partial Review (The Lost Tapes)">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup> Combined instruments such as the Agilent 81160A — a 330/500 MHz pulse, function, arbitrary, and noise generator in one chassis — reach roughly $20,000.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-308" title="EEVblog #308 - Agilent 81160A Function Generator Teardown">308</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-309" title="EEVblog #309 - Mailbag">309</a></sup> Such prices reflect the cost of the high-speed DACs and the substantial software and engineering development behind a modern generator, and specialized instruments can exceed these figures by an order of magnitude.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1679" title="EEVblog 1679 - Why Does a Uni-T AWG Cost $5800? - BOM Cost Analysis">1679</a></sup>
