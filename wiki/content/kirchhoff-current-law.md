Kirchhoff's [[current]] law, abbreviated KCL, states that the sum of the currents flowing into a junction equals the sum of the currents flowing out of it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> It ranks alongside Ohm's law as one of the most fundamental laws in electronics, and it is paired with Kirchhoff's [[voltage]] law, which governs the voltages around a loop.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> Both are named after Gustav Kirchhoff, who formulated them in about 1845.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

The law is a statement of the conservation of charge: charge put into a circuit must come out again, and at a junction point this reduces to current in equals current out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> That single sentence is the entirety of the law — nothing more complicated is involved, and the concept matters more than any formula derived from it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

## Statement and algebraic forms

For a junction with one current entering and two leaving, the relationship is written directly as I1 = I2 + I3.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> The form extends to any number of branches: an additional current I0 entering simply appears on the input side of the equation, and the law can be written for any number of currents flowing in or out, even the trivial case of one in and one out.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

More powerful mathematically is the rearranged form in which the algebraic sum of all currents at a junction equals zero.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> This requires a sign convention — currents entering the junction taken as positive and those leaving as negative, or the reverse.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> Which polarity is assigned makes no difference provided the choice is applied consistently, and a negative result that falls out of the algebra is informative rather than an error, since it reveals something about the direction of current in the circuit being analysed.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

## Use in circuit analysis

Nodal analysis is built directly on KCL.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> Choosing a reference point is arbitrary, and by convention all currents at the node under examination may be assumed to be leaving it, with none flowing in.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> Each branch current is expressed by Ohm's law in terms of the unknown node voltage, and the resulting nodal equation — of the form I1 + I2 + I3 = 0 — is solved for that voltage.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> Applied to a textbook circuit of three resistors and two voltage sources, this procedure yields a node voltage of 2.3636 V, from which the current through the target [[resistor]] follows as 0.11818 A by Ohm's law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup> Mesh analysis, which rests on Kirchhoff's voltage law, produces the identical answer for the same circuit, as does superposition; agreement between the three methods is a check that both laws hold.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-820" title="EEVblog #820 - DC Fundamentals Part 5: Mesh &amp; Nodal Circuit Analysis Tutorial">820</a></sup>

KCL also marks the boundary of simpler shortcuts. The two-resistor current divider formula applies only to that specific case and cannot be extended by summing terms; for three or more resistors in parallel, the analysis must fall back on Kirchhoff's current law.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1399" title="EEVblog 1399 - DC Fundamentals Part 3: Voltage Dividers">1399</a></sup>

In highly symmetrical networks the law supports a shortcut of its own. In the resistor cube problem, recognising that equal resistances force equal branch currents identifies equipotential nodes, so the network collapses without writing out the full set of Ohm's law and KCL equations.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1472" title="EEVblog 1472 - Resistor Cube Problem SOLVED">1472</a></sup>

## Application on the bench

Design by inspection leans on KCL as a standard DC circuit theorem alongside knowledge of basic building blocks.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup> For a [[linear-regulator|linear regulator]], the [[capacitor]] on the output is an open circuit at DC, so the [[output-current|output current]] equals the load — a measured 50 µA in one worked case — and the input current is fixed by I in = I Q + I out, with the [[quiescent-current|quiescent current]] read from the [[datasheet|data sheet]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1285" title="EEVblog #1285 - How to do Design By Inspection">1285</a></sup>

The law also constrains an inverting op-amp node: the current up the [[feedback-resistor|feedback resistor]] plus the [[leakage-current|leakage current]] into the input must equal the current flowing in, and with a good enough practical op-amp that leakage can actually be measured.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

For a shunt regulator, the resistor must pass the zener test current and the load current together. With a 50 mA test current from the data sheet and a 50 mA load, the resistor carries 100 mA; across a differential of 12 V input minus a 5 V zener, that gives 69 Ω.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-908" title="EEVblog #908 - Zener Diodes">908</a></sup>

Fault diagnosis is a natural fit, because KCL bounds where missing current can be. In an [[oscilloscope]] supply where excess current was dragging a rail down, the law reduced the possibilities to a short list: the current was not passing the [[current-sense-resistor|current sense resistor]], leaving only the base of a pass [[transistor]] into the LM723 regulator, or the regulator's [[current-limit|current limit]] pin.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-804" title="EEVblog #804 - HP1740A Oscilloscope Repair - Part 2">804</a></sup> The same reasoning applies to return paths — current leaving a [[mosfet|MOSFET]] into [[ground]] must complete a loop, and the [[ground-plane|ground plane]] is what carries it back.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-812" title="EEVblog #812 - Varta 15min NiMH Charger Part 2">812</a></sup>

Multiplexed displays run into the law as a hard limit. A 75 Ω [[series-resistor|series resistor]] sized for a nominal 20 mA at 3.3 V delivers that current to a single segment, but turning on all 40 segments forces the same total current to be shared, dropping each segment to half a milliamp and leaving the display very dim.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup> Driving segments in smaller groups rather than all at once is the way around it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1491" title="EEVblog 1491 - The MacGyver Project - Part 1">1491</a></sup>

## Ideal assumptions and real parts

An exam question involving one [[led|LED]] feeding two parallel LEDs illustrates both the reliability of the law and the fragility of what is assembled around it. Given 2 V at 20 mA and matched devices, KCL forces the 20 mA in the lower branch to split evenly, so each parallel LED carries 10 mA.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> Of all the idealisations such a question demands, the assumption that Kirchhoff's current law holds is the one that must be granted above everything else.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

The rest of the idealisation does not survive contact with real components. LED forward voltage is non-linear, so a device specified at 2 V and 20 mA sits nearer 1.8 V or 1.85 V at 10 mA, and rigidly holding VF constant while insisting each branch carries 20 mA implies 40 mA in the series LED — a current at which the forward voltage has moved again.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> The current split is nonetheless real and continuous: at any instant the two upper branch currents sum to the lower branch current, even as the division shifts with temperature and lead connections.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup> It is not, however, visible by eye — a branch carrying twice the current of its neighbours can appear to have much the same brightness.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1427" title="EEVblog 1427 - An INFURIATING Electronics Exam Question!">1427</a></sup>

## Standing in the fundamentals

The law is generally already obvious to anyone who has thought about circuits, and is used daily in circuit theory without conscious effort.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> The stated position is that if the only thing carried away is that current in equals current out, and that the voltages around a loop sum to zero or the applied voltages equal the voltages dropped, that is enough for everyday circuit analysis.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup> Deriving equations and solving circuits algebraically is optional; the concepts alone are powerful, and become more so the deeper the circuit theory goes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-819" title="EEVBlog #819 - DC Fundamentals Part 4: Kirchhoff&#x27;s Laws Tutorial">819</a></sup>

Together with Ohm's law and basic building block circuits, KCL is treated as part of the minimum foundation for [[troubleshooting]] hardware rather than merely assembling it.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-zyuRcsM0gjI" title="eevBLAB #10 - Why Learn Basic Electronics?">•</a></sup>
