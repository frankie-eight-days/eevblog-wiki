3D printing is additive fabrication of plastic parts directly from a CAD model, and in electronics development its dominant role is producing enclosures, brackets and fixtures before — or instead of — committing to injection mould tooling.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1537" title="EEVblog 1537 - NEW PROJECT Enclosure Search FAIL">1537</a></sup> Its practical value is that a physical part can be held, fitted to a [[pcb|PCB]] and revised in a day, at a cost small enough that being wrong does not matter, whereas a mould costs thousands of dollars and locks the design.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup> Its practical limits are surface finish, warping on large parts, and a set of process failures — bed adhesion, layer misregistration, extrusion [[calibration]] — that dominate whether a print succeeds at all.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-843" title="EEVblog #843 - David&#x27;s rPrint 3D Printer Design">843</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-318" title="EEVblog #318 - Makerbot Replicator 3D Printer Unboxing &amp; Review">318</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-257" title="EEVblog #257 - Makerbot Troubleshooting">257</a></sup>

## Prototyping enclosures before tooling

The strongest case for the technology in product development is the look-and-feel prototype. The original [[121gw|121GW]] [[multimeter]] prototype had a completely 3D printed case: the shell, the rotary knob, the tilt stand, the whole assembly, in rigid plastic standing in for the moulded rubber overmould of the production part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Custom Multimeter Prototype Unboxing">121</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xUAKjWMsUfA" title="TWO BOXES of Mystery Multimeters!">•</a></sup> It required liberties in the design — the case was split differently than the production part would be — but produced something close enough to a finished product that its printed origin was not obvious by eye.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-121" title="EEVblog 121GW Custom Multimeter Prototype Unboxing">121</a></sup> An earlier clear-cased version served the cruder but still necessary purpose of establishing physical size, something no rendering substitutes for.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-e9cpKN69Avk" title="121GW Development History">•</a></sup>

This leads to a design rule: where possible, design plastic parts so that they are 3D printable even when the production intent is ABS injection moulding, and design metal parts so they can be machined, so that a real part can be obtained before tooling money is spent.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup> Tooling is committed only once the design is confirmed, because that is the point at which thousands of dollars go into moulds.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup> Where in-house machine quality is insufficient, parts can be ordered from bureaus running professional machines, which also paint them to a finished appearance.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1196" title="EEVblog #1196 - Custom Heat Sink Design (µSupply Part 19)">1196</a></sup>

## Design and orientation for printing

Part geometry drives print quality more than machine settings. A large flat cosmetic surface should be oriented face down on the bed so it comes out smooth, with protruding features such as buttons printed as separate parts and attached afterwards, rather than printing one part with a large protrusion that prints badly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-736" title="EEVBlog #736 - World&#x27;s First IR Thermal Camera Watch">736</a></sup> Bridging across open spans works better than expected on well-tuned machines.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-736" title="EEVBlog #736 - World&#x27;s First IR Thermal Camera Watch">736</a></sup> Large items are difficult because they warp, which is a reason to break a design into smaller printed pieces.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup>

Finish is the standing limitation: the surface quality wanted for a presentation prototype is not obtainable straight off a print, and requires a secondary process such as an acetone vapour finish.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-843" title="EEVblog #843 - David&#x27;s rPrint 3D Printer Design">843</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-736" title="EEVBlog #736 - World&#x27;s First IR Thermal Camera Watch">736</a></sup> Layer lines are the giveaway — a printed part is normally identified on sight by them.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27aG9xhfk6s" title="See Blocks Review">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1410" title="EEVblog 1410 - Mailbag">1410</a></sup> Conversely, plastic that feels wrong on a finished consumer product is sometimes described as feeling as though it were printed, used as a criticism of moulding and material quality.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1298" title="EEVblog #1298 - $70 2000W Power Supply">1298</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-866" title="EEVblog #866 - Mailbag">866</a></sup>

Printed parts also accept conventional hardware. Heat-set metal threaded inserts push into printed bosses to give real screw threads, and small transparent parts can be printed to act as light pipes.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup> A single-extruder machine can approximate two-colour work by pausing the print and swapping filament.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup>

## Materials

ABS and PLA are the two working materials. ABS printed on a heated bed at 110 °C with a 230 °C hot end is used where the part needs to behave like a normal engineering thermoplastic.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-736" title="EEVBlog #736 - World&#x27;s First IR Thermal Camera Watch">736</a></sup> PLA is the easier general-purpose filament; impact modified PLA — IM PLA — is a variant intended to behave more like a proper impact-resistant thermoplastic while still printing on an ordinary machine, sold at roughly $48 per kilogram spool and, in that instance, in 3 mm rather than the standard 1.75 mm diameter.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup>

## Bed adhesion, rafts and calibration

Adhesion to the build platform is the single largest determinant of success: get a good base to stick, whether by raft or directly to the platform, and the remainder of the part prints essentially perfectly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-318" title="EEVblog #318 - Makerbot Replicator 3D Printer Unboxing &amp; Review">318</a></sup> The point is put bluntly as "It's all about the base".<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-318" title="EEVblog #318 - Makerbot Replicator 3D Printer Unboxing &amp; Review">318</a></sup> Tape on the platform is the classic fix for parts not sticking.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-237" title="EEVblog #237 - Makerbot Thing-O-Matic Unboxing">237</a></sup> Modern machines may instead specify a glue stick applied to the plate before printing, and adhesion can [[overshoot]] — parts stuck so firmly that removal risks the build plate.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Th71og-rLH8" title="Creality K1 Max Print SUCESSS">•</a></sup> Printing without a raft, where achievable, gives a markedly smoother bottom surface, and a smooth heated plate transfers its own finish to the first layer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-341" title="EEVblog #341 - Mailbag">341</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-327" title="EEVblog #327 - Makerbot Replicator Troubleshooting">327</a></sup>

The recurring mechanical failure is layer misregistration: axis motion skipping so that a circular feature comes out displaced, or layers not lining up, traced to belt slippage or to a calibration problem in extrusion rate and hot end temperature.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-327" title="EEVblog #327 - Makerbot Replicator Troubleshooting">327</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-257" title="EEVblog #257 - Makerbot Troubleshooting">257</a></sup> Complete loss of an axis is also seen, with all Y-axis motion dead after a failed print.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-327" title="EEVblog #327 - Makerbot Replicator Troubleshooting">327</a></sup> These faults are treated as the machine ruining an otherwise good experience rather than as inherent to the process.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-327" title="EEVblog #327 - Makerbot Replicator Troubleshooting">327</a></sup>

## Jigs, fixtures and bench engineering

Printing earns its place on the bench as a source of one-off mechanical parts that would otherwise not exist. Examples include knob adapters coupling a stepper drive to a rotary switch for an automated range-switch endurance test jig,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QSmiMlWEpy0" title="121GW Range Switch Cycling Test Jig">•</a></sup> a bracket modelled in Onshape as an extruded cylinder with notches, printed and fitted to mount a [[heat-sink|heat sink]] to a microscope,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-992" title="EEVblog #992 (Part 2) - How To Clean &amp; Service A Microscope">992</a></sup> a ring to carry an illumination ring on a microscope,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-VmPo0XPuKWM" title="IBM PC jr 512KB Memory Sidecar Teardown">•</a></sup> a wall mount for a Raspberry Pi acting as a solar monitoring node,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1628" title="EEVblog 1628 - Home Solar Power Re-Install + Upgrade + FAIL">1628</a></sup> a bracket to hold a replacement [[lcd|LCD]] module inside an [[oscilloscope]] during repair,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-WHwf26JCUec" title="Tektronix TDS540D Repair Part 1">•</a></sup> and a focus adjustment tool for a [[thermal-camera|thermal camera]], published on a model sharing site, that reaches the internal focus mechanism the manufacturer left inaccessible.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-988" title="EEVblog #988 - FLIR ETS320 Benchtop Thermal Camera Review">988</a></sup> Missing mechanical parts on second-hand equipment — a power button, a [[battery]] compartment door, a front-panel button — are treated as printable rather than as faults.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-559" title="EEVblog #559 - Auction Score">559</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1662" title="EEVblog 1662 - Vintage Tandy 200 Portable Computer Claytons Repair/Teardown">1662</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-502" title="EEVblog #502 - $19 Hameg Analog Oscilloscope">502</a></sup>

The same applies to production support hardware. Drone teams print landing feet, localisation fins and LIDAR mounts as custom parts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-766" title="EEVblog #766 - Vivid Sydney 2015 Light Festival">766</a></sup> SMD tape trays for manual pick-and-place have been produced as printed parts,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-722" title="EEVblog #722 - Mailbag">722</a></sup> and a robot actuator design reduces to a PCB, a motor, bearings, bolts and printed housing and reducer parts whose cost is negligible against a total of roughly $30 to $40.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1759" title="EEVblog 1759 - ACES Hardware Meetup (Startup Hardware is Hard)">1759</a></sup>

## Printed parts in shipped instruments

Low-volume test equipment ships with printed parts where tooling cannot be justified or has not yet been cut. A Tektronix 2 Series oscilloscope supplied very early carried a 3D printed tilt bail because the moulding was not yet done, fitted to the existing rear screw bosses, with production units expected to be moulded.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1477" title="EEVblog 1477 - TEARDOWN! - NEW Tektronix 2 Series Oscilloscope">1477</a></sup> A mains instrument used a printed shim to retain a front-panel AC [[connector]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-993" title="EEVblog #993 - Mailbag">993</a></sup> An isolated [[current]] sensor shipped in a printed case with a printed front-panel bezel, visibly layered but well enough executed to be mistaken for a moulded part.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1410" title="EEVblog 1410 - Mailbag">1410</a></sup> A 2000 V isolated oscilloscope demonstration used printed replicas of 10 kV SHV connectors because the real parts were not obtainable in the required form.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1753" title="EEVblog 1753 - Designing a 2000V Isolated Oscilloscope (Cleverscope)">1753</a></sup> For a first production run of around a hundred units, professionally printed cases are a plausible alternative to moulding.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1715" title="EEVblog 1715 - Euler Precision eSAP-30 3GHz Active Probe REVIEW">1715</a></sup>

Open hardware projects extend this by distributing the case as source: STL and CAD files published alongside [[firmware]] and schematics so an owner can print, modify and reprint the enclosure — seen with instrument cases, kit computers and modding-oriented lights.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1056" title="EEVblog #1056 - Digilent Open Scope MZ Review">1056</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1463" title="EEVblog 1463 - Mailbag">1463</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1690" title="EEVblog 1690 - Mailbag: Zifnu LED, Quantum Diamonds, Logic Gates, M.2 NAS">1690</a></sup>

## Machines and the desktop market

The desktop machines of the MakerBot era arrived as kits — the Thing-O-Matic shipped as a bag-of-parts build — before pre-assembled machines such as the Replicator.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-237" title="EEVblog #237 - Makerbot Thing-O-Matic Unboxing">237</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-318" title="EEVblog #318 - Makerbot Replicator 3D Printer Unboxing &amp; Review">318</a></sup> The RepRap line was designed around self-replication, printing a large fraction of its own structural parts, on the order of 80% depending on generation, with individual parts taking about two hours each.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-337" title="EEVblog #337 - HSC School Electronics Projects">337</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-203" title="Melbourne Hacker Space Visit - EEVblog #203">203</a></sup> Printed structural parts were in some cases preferred over moulded equivalents because the internal holes and features came out better.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-203" title="Melbourne Hacker Space Visit - EEVblog #203">203</a></sup>

Venture funding of MakerBot was accompanied by an analogy between the 3D printing industry and the early microcomputer hobby market of the Altair and Apple 1, and to the LaserJet's role in making personal 2D printing viable.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-198" title="Makerbot Venture Capital Funding - EEVblog #198">198</a></sup> That analogy is judged wrong in scale: 3D printing is expected to remain several orders of magnitude more niche than the microcomputer boom, even while the investment itself is sound.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-198" title="Makerbot Venture Capital Funding - EEVblog #198">198</a></sup> Market positioning at the time centred on whether 100 micron resolution machines could be scaled down into a physically smaller unit at around a $1,000 price point without a race to the bottom.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-356" title="EEVblog #356 - Makerbot Replicator 2 Announcement">356</a></sup>

Industrially, machine prices collapsed over the same period, from around a million dollars to a quarter of that for an early professional installation, displacing the practice of outsourcing prototype plastics to an external service.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup> Such machines produce geometry that cannot be made subtractively at all, such as articulated chain mail emerging assembled from the build chamber.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-684" title="EEVblog #684 - Ness SMT Manufacturing &amp; Assembly Factory Tour">684</a></sup> At the desktop end, machines have reached a price at which owning several is reasonable, kept permanently set up with different filaments and configurations to avoid changeover.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1375" title="EEVblog #1375 - Mailbag: IEEE">1375</a></sup> Contemporary machines print complex articulated models — fully jointed multi-part figures that move as printed — on default settings with no tuning beyond the specified glue.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-Th71og-rLH8" title="Creality K1 Max Print SUCESSS">•</a></sup>

## Spare parts and repair

In the right-to-repair context, increasing use of 3D printing is identified as a potentially lower-cost route to supplying spare parts for products no longer manufactured, analogous to print-on-demand for rarely purchased books, since holding physical spare parts inventory is itself costly.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-xiXucGYuQ6M" title="Australian Right to Repair Inquiry with Productivity Commissioner Paul Lindwall">•</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-qDWLTSfrq_E" title="2021 Australia Right to Repair Summit - Session 2 of 4">•</a></sup> The same reasoning appears at the individual level: a corroded slide-out battery holder or a worn bicycle light mount with no available replacement is a candidate for reproduction on a printer.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1133" title="EEVblog #1133 Mailbag Monday">1133</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-gTTsvYbQOT4" title="Olight RN1500 Bicycle Bike Light REVIEW + TEST">•</a></sup>

## Overclaiming

The technology attracts claims well beyond what it does. The proposition that a self-sustaining off-world colony can be bootstrapped by taking a 3D printer along is dismissed outright, on the grounds that reproducing the infrastructure to make electronics and chips is nothing like printing plastic parts.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-XzuH8sECMJQ" title="Self Sustaining Mars Colonies">•</a></sup> Naming 3D printing alongside artificial intelligence in vehicle marketing, where the underlying performance numbers are unremarkable, is treated as promotional [[noise]].<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1480" title="EEVblog 1480 EXTENDED VERSION - LightYear Zero + Aptera Solar EV">1480</a></sup><sup class="cite"><a href="#src-QkFioBbH5aM" title="Aptera Solar Powered Electric Car - Is It Practical?">•</a></sup> Printed parts also serve as a tell in dubious hardware: an aesthetic prototype of a purported magnetic repulsion generator consisted of a printed magnet holder,<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-HfqpuhYiR3o" title="eevBLAB #60 - Kickstarter Free Energy SCAMS!">•</a></sup> and a patent describing exotic melted-quartz spheres resolves, on inspection, to objects that look like printed balls extruded from a custom filament.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1633" title="EEVblog 1633 Part 2 - WAVJA Patent, 300kW Solar Balls!">1633</a></sup> A high-speed 3D printing technology promised by a well-funded company was never actually produced.<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-C_tXWwcyZlc" title="Dumpster HP3488A Switch Teardown + Silverbrook STORY">•</a></sup>
