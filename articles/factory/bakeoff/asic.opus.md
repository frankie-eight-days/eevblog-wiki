# asic

An ASIC is a chip designed for one product or one job rather than sold as a general-purpose part, and it sits at the far end of a spectrum whose other end is the FPGA — the closest thing available to designing a chip from scratch without committing to silicon.[496] Rolling custom silicon buys performance that no processor can reach at any clock rate, because the function is wired rather than executed: a scope acquisition ASIC can push a million waveform updates per second straight onto the display,[148] and a power meter can integrate voltage and current at one megasample per second without the main processor ever seeing the samples.[1693] The cost of that is non-recurring engineering — a fixed sum paid before the first part exists, which then has to be recovered across the units shipped.[1639][1146]

## The economics of rolling your own

The NRE argument decides almost every case. Once the development cost is absorbed, the ASIC becomes cheap relative to buying an ADC from Texas Instruments or Analog Devices and an FPGA from Xilinx, because the custom part replaces both.[1639] Before it is absorbed, the same cost is punishing: a manufacturer with a new chipset is still recovering years of NRE on the first products that carry it.[1146]

The threshold is volume. An instrument selling in the hundreds or low thousands of units does not justify custom silicon, and that is why a logic analyser of that class uses an FPGA even where the function is fixed and well understood.[1018] Consumer volume inverts the argument entirely, which is why a mobile phone built down to the lowest possible bill of materials reduces to a main ASIC plus perhaps external memory and an RF chipset,[514] and why a mass-market keyboard is one large ASIC with essentially nothing else on a board that is otherwise single-sided.[1727]

A middle path exists for a large customer without the volume to fund a chip outright: approach a semiconductor vendor with an existing device and ask for a variant — a feature added, a feature removed to cut power — typically on terms that let the vendor sell the result to others.[1229] Vendors will play along if the quantity is there.[1229]

## Gate arrays and FPGAs as the cheaper alternatives

A gate array occupies the ground between a standard part and a full custom spin. Configuring metal over a pre-fabricated array is much cheaper and simpler than spinning a full ASIC, and it was the normal way to implement custom logic in specialised, price-insensitive equipment.[969] Sinclair's uncommitted logic array is the canonical consumer example: a single ULA alongside a Z80 collapsed a design that had previously needed roughly ten times as many chips.[872] Where a teardown shows a custom-branded part in an old design, a gate array is often the more plausible reading than a true custom ASIC.[663]

The FPGA now occupies the same niche with none of the mask cost, and the tradeoff is visible in finished products. A scope built on FPGAs rather than an ASIC can in principle have its acquisition behaviour changed in firmware or by reloading the fabric, precisely because the device is field programmable.[1311] The penalty is that heavy fixed-function processing — a 128 K-point FFT, for instance — needs an FPGA or an ASIC either way, because a general-purpose processor grinds to a halt on it.[845]

## Acquisition ASICs in oscilloscopes

The traditional scope architecture puts a converter into an FPGA or ASIC, funnels the data through dual-port or external memory, and has the CPU read that memory and update the display — at which point the CPU becomes the bottleneck and the dead time between acquisitions grows.[148] A full acquisition ASIC removes the CPU from that path: the display plotter is coupled directly onto the LCD, a GUI controller inside the same chip handles cursors and graphical overlay, and the processor is relegated to Ethernet, USB, file handling and similar secondary work.[148] Keysight's MegaZoom line is the reference implementation of this, designed and manufactured in-house rather than sourced,[143] and its practical result was a million waveform updates per second in an instrument priced around $1,230 for the entry model, where that update rate had previously required a $10,000-plus instrument.[148][143]

The same architecture is what keeps processing free at the point of use. Serial decoding runs in the ASIC in real time, so enabling it costs no update rate,[149] and math functions execute in hardware rather than as software post-processing, which is the difference between a scope that stays fast with math on and one that slows visibly.[594] Instruments without that hardware show the opposite behaviour plainly: turning on digital channels, FFT or serial decode collapses the update rate because there is no ASIC to absorb the work.[701] Hardware acceleration is not unlimited, though — a modern acquisition ASIC given peak-detect processing can still drop from tens of thousands of updates per second to sixty.[1529]

Acquisition silicon is commonly built per channel pair rather than per instrument, which produces an exploitable asymmetry. Where a scope carries one ASIC for every two channels, running channels one and three keeps full sample rate and memory on both, while adding the paired channel halves them.[792][149] A design that instead spreads a single acquisition path across four channels offers no such trick, and the limit applies to any combination of three or more channels.[704] The channel-pair structure is visible in teardowns as duplicated devices: a four-channel model carrying two acquisition ASICs plus two further custom devices against a single set on its two-channel sibling.[148][144][199]

Later generations push integration further, combining the acquisition ASIC with dual 14-bit converters on one die, which reduces a scope to that chip plus an application processor — effectively "an oscilloscope in one chip".[1639] The competing approach is a discrete high-performance ASIC paired with separate converters, as in the R&S MXO4, whose acquisition chip is responsible for four and a half million waveform updates per second as well as the digital trigger.[1545]

Custom silicon is now a general marker of tier rather than a single vendor's advantage: Rigol's Phoenix chipset comprises a front-end ASIC and an acquisition ASIC of its own design and carries down into low-cost 12-bit models sharing the same front-end chip,[1218][1146][1566] Uni-T develops its own ASICs for its upper series,[1625] and Tektronix reserves a custom acquisition engine for its higher-end models while lower ones use off-the-shelf converters.[1477] Where a manufacturer has FPGAs rather than ASICs, a JTAG header daisy-chained across the devices gives it away.[864] The dependency cuts both ways: a fixed function set frozen into an ASIC can limit what a later instrument built around it is allowed to do.[383]

## Development cost and timescale

Chipset development runs in multiples of years. A generational scope chipset took over five years even with a working predecessor to build from, and amounted to a complete architectural redesign with all new ASICs rather than an incremental revision.[1032] The resulting part then earns for a long time — one such ASIC was still the fastest-updating acquisition engine in its class more than a decade after introduction.[1478][701] Individual engineers can spend years on the VHDL or Verilog for a single chip.[1079] Attribution within a team that size is unusual but not impossible: Matt Holcomb is named as the engineer responsible for the MegaZoom 4.[150]

The support burden is easy to underestimate. High-power ASICs need supplies clean enough not to inject spurs into the signal path — on a 120 MHz function generator, switching supply noise reaching the output required redesigning the supplies rather than the ASIC.[1032] In regulated products the chip is the smaller part of the effort: for an implantable device, designing the ASIC sits alongside qualifying every single part that goes into the product, with five to eight years from concept to market.[1027]

## Identifying custom silicon in hardware

A house-branded chip is weak evidence on its own. Manufacturers routinely order standard microcontrollers with their own logo printed on them, so a branded part in a low-volume consumer product is more likely a custom-marked off-the-shelf device than custom silicon.[330][1018] Lasered-off markings likewise usually conceal commercial parts rather than ASICs, and the underlying device can often be narrowed down from the block diagram, package and pin count in a few hours.[391] Chip-on-board construction — bare die glob-topped to the PCB — says nothing either way about whether the die is custom.[633]

Positive indicators are more structural. A single very large BGA with heavy local bypassing and its own low core rail is characteristic of custom processing silicon,[1289][1145] as is a part number that returns nothing useful in a search: vendors who roll their own silicon produce families of similar, undocumented numbers.[598][917] Where a number does resolve, it may resolve only to a foundry's gate-array family rather than to a datasheet.[591] Debug artefacts are also telling — an emulator interface next to a custom cell device implies headers were soldered on during prototyping to emulate that chip.[619]

## Power, thermal behaviour and failure

Acquisition and processing ASICs dominate the power budget of the boards they sit on and are usually the hottest devices present, sometimes reaching around 100 °C on the die,[565] though a merely warm figure around 55 °C is unremarkable.[593] Cooling is frequently designed around them specifically, with large heatsinks or forced air over the main ASIC, and blocking that airflow can trigger a thermal shutdown within minutes.[1185][622] Core rails are correspondingly low and heavily decoupled — 1.8 V on mid-2010s display processors, 1.2 V on later high-density parts — and a clean, ripple-free core rail is quick evidence that the fault lies elsewhere.[780][1145] Below roughly 0.8 V a processor-class node stops working, which sets the practical floor for anything running directly from a decaying cell.[1383]

Failure diagnosis is unusually hard because the normal localisation techniques fail on these parts. In a scope whose 3.3 V rail measured about a tenth of an ohm, the only four devices remaining on that rail were its ASICs; thermal imaging found no localised hot spot anywhere on the board, with all the excess power spread uniformly across all four devices, and removing one shifted supply current by only about 1.2 A out of roughly 10 A.[401][405] Four identically dead ASICs at essentially uniform temperature leaves no repair path.[401] Two traps recur in this kind of work: infrared imaging of shiny surfaces such as BNC bodies and solder joints shows reflected heat rather than device temperature,[401] and freeze spray does not reliably provoke a marginal BGA joint under a large ASIC, so a negative result is not exculpatory.[781]

## Custom silicon in older equipment

The practice long predates the term's current usage. A 1973 desktop calculator split its processor across four separate ASICs and needed two more for the display processor, an architecture forced by how little logic fit in one device.[663] A calculator generation later, a memory upgrade was implemented by respinning the ASIC itself to integrate five times the register count rather than adding external memory chips.[582] Notepad computers of the 1980s paired a Z80 with a single large LSI device that ran continuously from a 12.2 MHz ceramic resonator, absorbed the LCD controller and nearly everything else, and left the CPU doing very little.[385] Some products of the era deliberately went the other way: a 1984 portable computer used no custom devices at all, entirely off-the-shelf parts on one double-sided board.[116]

Audio, video and instrument makers all rolled their own. Yamaha's YSS910 large-scale integration parts are custom DSP silicon, ten to a board each with local memory, claimed as the "world's first 44bit DSPs".[738] Sony populated video effects processors almost entirely with in-house silicon for the specific transform tasks involved,[598] motion-JPEG compression in a professional video editor ran on large NEC ASICs alongside a general DSP for effects,[969] and a plasma television concentrated its video processing into three large ASICs under a die-cast bar.[725] Analog instruments used ASICs too — hybrid preamplifier substrates for differential front ends — and part numbers within a product family often stayed frozen across model revisions, making devices salvageable between generations.[803][695] A multimeter ASIC designed decades earlier was still being manufactured and fitted in 2005.[372]

The long-run consequence is that functions once built from discrete logic are now bought as single parts. Recovering and displaying a VGA signal formerly took a board full of programmable logic; the same capability later arrived as a couple of chips available from a distributor, laid out in a day.[694] The same integration is why current instrument front ends reach 800 MHz with flat response in essentially one chip, and why the major scope manufacturers now roll their own analog front-end silicon as well as their acquisition engines.[1582]
