# dropout voltage

Dropout voltage is the minimum differential a linear voltage regulator needs between its input and output to keep the output in regulation.[158] If the input drops too close to the output, the regulator can no longer sustain the set output voltage and falls out of regulation, producing a sagging, unregulated output rather than a clean cutoff.[158][1147] The specification is central to any linear-regulator design because it sets a hard floor on how low the input rail can go, or equivalently how much margin above the target output the input supply must provide.[221][1701]

## Definition and measurement

On a data sheet, dropout voltage is formally defined as the input-to-output differential at which the output has sagged by 100 mV from its nominal regulated value.[158] Manufacturers publish it as a parametric graph of input-to-output differential voltage against temperature, with separate load lines for different output currents, since the value changes with both load current and temperature.[158] For an LM317 at 500 mA and 25 °C, the graph reads roughly 1.75-1.8 V; at 20 mA and the same temperature it drops to about 1.5 V.[158] Bench measurement of the dropout point is done by winding the input voltage down while watching the output start to sag, or, more rigorously, by finding the input voltage at which the output has fallen exactly 100 mV from its set point.[158][224]

## Regulator topology drives the number

Standard linear regulators built around a Darlington NPN pass transistor, such as the 7805, incur two base-emitter drops plus a saturation voltage, giving a typical dropout of roughly 1.5-2 V.[90][722] The LM317 is explicitly not a low-dropout part; it is a standard linear regulator with a comparatively high dropout, commonly taken as a 2 V minimum rule of thumb at full current, and its data sheet gives an input-to-output differential of around 3 V minimum, more than the 7805.[158][1438] Low-dropout regulators (LDOs) instead use a PNP or P-channel pass element, which lets the input sit much closer to the output — down to a few hundred millivolts — but at the cost of greater loop instability and sensitivity to output capacitor ESR.[90][1147][158] A cheap SOT-23 "one-cent" jellybean LDO, for example, is specified at 0.2 V dropout at 90 mA rising to 0.4 V at 150 mA, and measures at roughly 150 mV at 100 mA output and around 300 mV near its full rated current of 250 mA.[1147]

Some parts, such as the LT3080, specify dropout two ways: a V-control-pin dropout and a VIN dropout, because the internal control circuitry can be powered separately from the main pass path.[222] Tying the two pins together, which some smaller packages force by internal connection, gives the worst-case figure, around 1.2 V typical and up to 1.6 V at full current; feeding the control pin from a point downstream of a current-shunt resistor instead reduces the effective dropout seen by the main path.[222][260] A breadboarded LT3080 regulator measured about 1.25-1.4 V dropout across no-load to 1 A, consistent with its roughly 1.6 V absolute-maximum spec.[224][260]

## Consequence for battery-powered products

Dropout voltage directly limits how much of a battery's capacity a product can actually use. Powering a 3.3 V LDO with 50 mV of dropout from a single-cell lithium-polymer battery moves the effective cutoff point up the discharge curve enough to waste roughly 45-50% of the battery's rated capacity, since that lost region is the area under the voltage-versus-capacity curve below the cutoff.[1331] For a 9 V alkaline battery feeding a 7805 (nominal 2 V dropout), the battery is effectively cut off around 7 V rather than being run down toward the 0.8 V-per-cell point where most of its usable capacity lies; a switch-mode converter, which can regulate down to a much lower effective dropout, is recommended instead to reclaim that capacity.[722] The same logic applies to any battery-powered chip: the chip's supply-voltage limit combined with the regulator's dropout sets where the low-battery detector threshold has to sit, and a battery cut off well above that point wastes capacity.[972] Any product intended to boost or extend battery runtime by exploiting a small residual voltage is similarly bounded by the dropout voltage of the device it powers, since gains available below that threshold are offset by efficiency losses at higher currents.[751]

## Switch-mode and buck converters

Switching converters are not immune to an equivalent constraint. A buck converter must be fed an input voltage above its output plus its own dropout-like headroom; it cannot regulate an output higher than, or too close to, its input.[1298][1030][1031] A $20 DIY buck-based bench supply rated 0-40 V input needs at least the target output voltage plus its dropout to reach that output, so a 32 V output requires more than 32 V in.[1030] A cheap buck PSU module measured about 1 V dropout at no load (32 V in yielding 31 V out).[1031] A high-power 2000 W buck supply exhibited an unusually large dropout of roughly 14 V (needing at least 20 V in for a 96 V output), which is large by ordinary regulator standards.[1298] Separately, a non-isolated buck-based switchmode converter can also "drop out" of regulation at excessive load current when its component values are not sized for that current, a distinct failure mode from input-output headroom exhaustion: one converter began dropping its output around 170 mA against a 100 mA design target, and a redesigned inductor value shifted that current-limited dropout point to about 100-120 mA.[110]

## Ripple rejection tradeoff

A regulator's ability to attenuate input ripple depends on its dropout voltage as well as its topology and output current: as the dropout margin gets smaller, ripple attenuation can get worse rather than better, making a very-low-dropout regulator a poor choice for ripple cleanup despite its otherwise tight regulation.[1116] The same effect applies to post-regulation, for example filtering the output of a charge pump like the 7660: if the post-regulator is run with only a low dropout voltage across it, incoming noise passes through it more readily rather than being filtered out.[1115]

## Practical and diagnostic use

Dropout voltage is used as a repair and diagnostic reference as well as a design constraint. In oscilloscope power-supply troubleshooting, a pass transistor's rated maximum dropout (for example, 2 V) sets the minimum input voltage the rest of the supply must deliver for the output rail to regulate correctly, so a measured input below that threshold points to an upstream fault.[804] Similarly, confirming that input ripple stays above a regulator's minimum dropout voltage is a standard check when validating that a repaired supply will hold regulation.[621] A cheap multimeter was shown losing display brightness and failing outright as its battery voltage fell to around 2.5 V per pack, well above its claimed operating floor, illustrating a design with inadequate dropout margin built into its regulation.[1095] Electronic loads used for battery discharge testing can likewise be set to cut off testing at a specified voltage, referred to on the instrument as a dropout voltage.[862]

## Terminology

The term "dropout voltage" is specific to voltage regulators and is not used on voltage-reference data sheets, even though references specify an equivalent input-to-output margin (commonly around 2 V above the output) needed to hold the reference in regulation.[500]
