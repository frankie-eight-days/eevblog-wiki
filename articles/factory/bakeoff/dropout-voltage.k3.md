# dropout voltage

Dropout voltage is the minimum voltage differential required between the input and output of a voltage regulator for the device to remain in regulation: "That means the dropout voltage is the voltage differential between the input."[158] If the input falls below the output voltage plus the dropout voltage, the regulator "drops out" of regulation and the output sags or becomes unstable.[158][1147] The figure matters because it sets the minimum input headroom in any design, and in battery-powered equipment it directly determines how much of the battery's capacity can actually be used.[1331][972]

## Definition and measurement

The standard definition of dropout is the input-to-output differential at which the output voltage has fallen by 100 mV from its regulated value.[158] The practical bench measurement follows directly from this: hold the load current constant, wind the input voltage down, and note the input voltage at which the output has dropped 100 mV; the difference is the dropout voltage.[158] A measurement of an LM317 at a 20 mA load gave 4.803 V in against 3.26 V out, or 1.54 V dropout, matching the datasheet graph for that load point.[158] An LT3080 measured about 1.25 V dropout at no load and 1.35–1.4 V at 1 A, again agreeing with the datasheet curves.[224]

Dropout is not a single number. It varies with load current and with temperature, which is why datasheets present it as a family of parametric curves — input-to-output differential on the Y axis against temperature, with separate load lines for each output current.[158] For the LM317 those curves run from 20 mA up to the maximum 1.5 A; at 500 mA and 25 °C the dropout is about 1.75–1.8 V.[158] Worst-case design requires the datasheet's maximum figure at the intended current, not the typical figure: the LT3080, for example, specifies 1.2 V at 100 mA but as much as 1.6 V at full current, so a 5 V output demands at least 6.6 V at the input pin.[222]

## Standard linear regulators versus LDOs

Classic linear regulators such as the 7805 and LM317 use an NPN Darlington pass transistor, so their dropout is inherently two VBE drops plus the transistor's saturation voltage — typically 1.5 to 2 V.[90][158] A rule of thumb for the LM317 at full current is a 2 V minimum drop, meaning a 5 V output needs at least 7 V at the input.[158] The 7805's dropout is also around 2 V, though it depends on the specific variant and the load current.[722] Some jellybean parts are worse still: the LM317-class device cited in one reference design shows a 3 V minimum input-to-output differential, explicitly not a low-dropout part.[1438]

Low-dropout (LDO) regulators use a PNP or P-channel pass element and reduce the differential to a few hundred millivolts or less.[158][1147] Typical figures encountered in practice include 50–100 mV for the regulators inside a handheld meter[972], 0.2 V at 90 mA rising to 0.4 V at 150 mA on a one-cent SOT-23 regulator, with a measured 300 mV dropout at its full rated 250 mA[1147], and 350 mV at full load on the LT3080 when its control pin is driven separately.[222] An LDO with 0.2 V dropout in place of an LM317's 1.5–2 V provides a large margin in a 5 V-USB-powered 3.3 V design that would otherwise be marginal.[158]

The trade-off is stability. LDOs are inherently less stable than standard linear regulators because of the pass-element topology, so the ESR and value of the output capacitor must be chosen carefully, and some parts remain fussy about capacitance even when stable.[90][1147] LDO ground-pin current can also rise with output current, wasting power.[90] A further trap is the minimum load current: a regulator that is not loaded to its specified minimum may not regulate, or may exhibit degraded behaviour, which defeats the purpose of the supply.[222][1438]

## Design consequences

Headroom must be budgeted at the input pin of the regulator, not at the upstream supply. Any series element between the source and the regulator — for instance a 1 Ω current-shunt resistor dropping a further volt at 1 A — adds to the required input: a 5 V output with 1.6 V regulator dropout plus 1 V of shunt drop needs at least 7.6 V in.[222] The same logic applies when diagnosing faults: a 5 V rail fed through a pass transistor with an assumed 2 V maximum dropout needs at least 7 V, and roughly 9 V in practice, on the reservoir capacitor; less than that indicates the primary side cannot supply enough power.[804] Ripple on the reservoir must similarly stay above the regulators' minimum dropout across the whole ripple cycle.[621]

Some regulators expose the internal control circuitry on a separate pin (V control on the LT3080). Tying this pin to a voltage higher than the main input, or to the other side of a current shunt, yields a lower effective input-to-output dropout than tying the two pins together; however, some packages bond the pins internally, so the worst-case tied-together specification must be used when the separate pin is unavailable.[222][260]

In battery-powered design, dropout voltage converts directly into wasted capacity. A single-cell lithium-polymer product with a 3 V cutoff and a 3.3 V rail fed through an LDO with 50 mV dropout effectively moves the cutoff up by that margin, and with a less favourable regulator the product can throw away 45–50% of the battery's capacity.[1331] The same arithmetic applies to a 9 V battery (six alkaline cells): a 7805's ~2 V dropout means the battery is treated as flat at 7 V, or about 1.15 V per cell, whereas extracting most of the capacity requires working down to 0.8 V per cell — something a switch-mode converter achieves easily but a linear regulator cannot.[722] For this reason a switching converter is the recommended choice when the battery cutoff must be pushed low.[722][1331]

Dropout also degrades ripple rejection. An LDO's attenuation of input ripple depends on the input-to-output differential: the lower the dropout margin, the worse the rejection, and higher output current makes it worse again.[1116] This is a specific trap when post-regulating a charge-pump inverter such as the 7660 — with only a small voltage dropped across the post-regulator, noise passes largely straight through.[1115]

## Dropout beyond linear regulators

The concept applies to switching converters as well. A buck converter cannot produce an output at or above its input, so the input must exceed the desired output by the converter's dropout figure.[1030][1031][1298] Measured examples range from about 1 V at no load on a DPS3003 module fed 32 V[1031] to at least 14 V on a 2 kW buck module that requires a 20–110 V input for a 96 V output.[1298] A linear pass element in a programmable supply similarly prevents the output reaching the input rail.[1701] Switchmode converters also exhibit dropout in the current domain: a converter designed for 100 mA falls out of regulation at about 170 mA if its component values are not optimised for the higher current, and a change of inductor alone can move the dropout point.[110]

The terminology itself does not transfer everywhere. Voltage reference datasheets generally do not use the term "dropout voltage"; instead they specify a minimum input voltage — typically 2 V above the nominal output across the family — which is functionally the same requirement and must be read out of the input-voltage or line-regulation specifications.[500]
