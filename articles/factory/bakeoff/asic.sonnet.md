# asic

An application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) is a chip designed and fabricated for one particular task, in contrast to a general-purpose processor or a field-programmable gate array (FPGA), whose function is configured after manufacture rather than fixed at fabrication.[496] Moving a function into custom silicon converts what would otherwise be a CPU- or FPGA-bound processing bottleneck into dedicated, real-time hardware, and the technique recurs across test-and-measurement instruments, consumer electronics, and industrial equipment wherever volume or margin justifies the investment.[148][149]

## Economics and volume thresholds
Developing an ASIC carries a large non-recurring engineering (NRE) cost, but once that cost is absorbed the marginal cost per chip becomes low relative to buying equivalent capability as discrete ADCs and FPGAs from vendors such as Texas Instruments, Analog Devices, or Xilinx.[1639] Developing a single generation of Keysight's MegaZoom oscilloscope acquisition ASIC took over five years.[1032] Volume determines whether the NRE is worth it at all: a product selling in the low thousands of units, such as a niche logic analyzer, would not justify rolling a custom ASIC and is instead built around an FPGA.[1018] Rigol's and Keysight's respective oscilloscope chipsets are described as still paying back years of NRE only once fielded across a full product range.[1146]

Semiconductor vendors will also spin a customized variant of an existing chip for a single large customer -- adding or removing features, or reducing power -- if the customer's order quantity is large enough, as Samsung reportedly did for TomTom's GPS receivers.[1229]

## ASIC, gate array, and ULA are distinct terms
A full-custom ASIC is not the same thing as a gate array or an uncommitted logic array (ULA). The Sinclair ZX81 used a ULA, described as "kind of like" an ASIC, to cut the chip count and cost relative to the earlier ZX80.[872] A gate array lets a vendor implement custom logic within a fixed, pre-fabricated array of logic cells rather than spinning a full-custom ASIC, which is cheaper and simpler to implement when volume does not justify a full design.[969] The Compucorp 322G calculator's processor was split across four separate devices that may be a gate array rather than a genuine custom ASIC, consistent with a design constrained by a limited amount of available logic per chip.[663]

## Oscilloscope acquisition ASICs
The best-documented application is the digital storage oscilloscope acquisition-and-display pipeline. In a traditional design, an ADC feeds a large FPGA or custom ASIC, the data funnels through a dual-port memory into external memory, and a CPU has to read that memory and update the display -- with the CPU becoming the bottleneck.[148] Agilent's MegaZoom ASIC line was designed in-house and, for the 2000/3000 X-Series, manufactured in Agilent's own plant in Malaysia.[143] By routing waveform data through a display plotter directly coupled to the LCD, the ASIC relegates the CPU to a secondary role handling math, measurement, and search, while the ASIC itself delivers up to a million waveform updates per second -- a rate that previously required $10,000-$20,000 instruments built the traditional CPU-bound way.[148][143] The 3000 series board carries two MegaZoom 4 ASICs against the 2000 series board's one, roughly doubling throughput.[148] A teardown of the 2000 series board found two separate ASICs where a "one big wonderful ASIC" doing everything had been expected from the instrument's own power-on splash screen.[144]

Once the NRE on such an ASIC is paid off, the same silicon, or a scaled-down version, can be pushed into lower-end models, bringing million-waveform-per-second update rates to instruments costing little over a thousand dollars that previously required $10,000-$15,000 scopes to match.[143][148] The MegaZoom 4 ASIC remained in service for well over a decade, still shipping in current Keysight scopes roughly thirteen years after its introduction.[1478]

Because the acquisition ASIC performs math, FFTs, cursor measurements, and serial decoding in dedicated hardware, enabling those functions carries no update-rate penalty on scopes built around one.[1478][149][594][701][845] Instruments that instead do this processing in software or on a general CPU show substantial update-rate dead time when such functions are switched on, as on the Tektronix MDO 3000, which lacks ASIC hardware for FFT or serial decoding.[701] Even ASIC-equipped scopes are not immune: enabling enough simultaneous processing functions can still measurably cut the waveform update rate on the Rohde & Schwarz MXO4.[1529] Acquisition ASICs are also commonly paired one per channel pair, so selecting channels one and three rather than one and two on such a scope can double the effective sample rate and memory depth by drawing on two separate ASIC channel pairs instead of contending for one.[792][149]

Other manufacturers have followed the same path. Rigol developed its own "Phoenix" front-end and acquisition ASIC chipset, used across the MSO5000, MSO7000, and DHO-series scopes.[1218][1146][1566] Rohde & Schwarz's MXO4 uses a proprietary acquisition ASIC responsible for 4.5 million waveform updates per second and its digital triggering system.[1545][1529] Uni-T's UTE310 power meter integrates real-time hardware voltage and current integration into its own ASIC.[1693]

## Physical identification and thermal behavior
Acquisition and processor ASICs are typically the largest, hottest devices on a board, and thermal imaging is a standard tool for locating them and diagnosing failures around them.[565][622] In one LeCroy 9384C oscilloscope repair, four identical ASICs sharing a 3.3 V rail were found to be uniformly hot with no single device standing out as the failure, and a thermal camera failed to find any localized hot spot elsewhere on the board even though current draw exceeded the expected value by more than an amp -- pointing to the four ASICs collectively rather than one discrete failed part as the source of a rail-level short.[401][405] More generally, a processor ASIC operating at the silicon level will typically continue to function down to around 0.8 V before performance starts to degrade.[1383]

## Where custom silicon shows up
Custom ASICs recur wherever a manufacturer has the volume or margin to justify one: LCD and plasma TV main processor boards[781][725], calculators[582][1727], video-effects processors[598], digital mixers[738], VoIP processing boards[1019], implantable medical devices[1027], and cryptocurrency mining hardware, where a single mining blade can carry forty ASICs dedicated to the hashing algorithm alongside a small ARM Cortex-M3 used only for control.[993] Low-margin consumer goods without that volume -- a breast pump, for instance -- are more likely to use an off-the-shelf microcontroller than a custom ASIC, since the NRE cannot be justified.[330]

Designing an ASIC is a multi-year undertaking. Certifying every part in an implantable device's ASIC and the rest of its bill of materials can take five to eight years from concept to market.[1027] Engineers can spend years of a career writing the VHDL or Verilog for a single ASIC design.[1079]
