# voltage rail

A voltage rail is one of the regulated supply voltages distributed through a piece of equipment — the 3.3 V, 5 V, 12 V or ±15 V nets that every active device on a board hangs off. A modern product rarely has one: high-density logic such as FPGAs demands a spread of low-voltage supplies, not just 3.3 V but 2.5 V, 1.8 V, 1.2 V, and 0.8 V or 1 V cores, so half a dozen distinct rails inside a single product is unremarkable.[1147] Because the rails are the common dependency of everything else on the board, they are simultaneously the first thing a designer must plan and the first thing a troubleshooter must measure.[65][1216]

## Rails in system design

Instrument and computer designs carry rails for different purposes at different voltages. Analog sections typically want a bipolar pair — ±12 V, ±15 V, ±20 V — while the digital side runs from 3.3 V and 5 V, which is the usual reason a supply is built with multiple taps in the first place.[497][607][593][1489] Where a product runs from a single battery at much lower voltage than its analog signal range demands, DC-DC converters are the mechanism that manufactures the required rails.[1090] Processors of any complexity may need a dedicated power management controller that generates all the various rails and handles the power-up sequences, rather than being hooked directly to 3.3 V the way a small microcontroller can be.[334]

The count of rails costs money as well as board area. With three or even five voltage regulators per product, the per-unit price of a jelly-bean regulator becomes a real line in the bill of materials at volume.[1147] Setting a rail's value also interacts with BOM consolidation: a feedback divider that needs an odd value such as 4.99 K to hit the tolerance on a 1.2 V rail can often be built from two 10 K resistors in parallel instead, avoiding another reel, another BOM line, and another pick-and-place slot.[1307] Layout for bleeding-edge parts pulling substantial power means holding many different rails to tight tolerances under dynamic, AC and static power conditions at once.[1216] At the package level, a device's multiple power pins may belong to different internal cores rather than one rail, and manufacturers commonly specify a bypass capacitor per pin.[1512]

## Sequencing

Rails do not exist independently at power-up. An FPGA datasheet may carry a recommended or even a strict power-up rail sequence — bringing up the 1.2 V core first and the 3.3 V I/O some 10 ms later, with deliberate delays in between.[995]

Powering rails independently from bench supplies exposes the coupling between them. With four rails brought up one at a time, switching on the 3.3 V rail first forced the unpowered 5 V rail up to roughly 1.5 V through back-diode protection paths and similar mechanisms, and simultaneously dragged the −5.2 V rail high by about 500 mV; the 5 V rail only snapped to its real value when its own supply was switched on and presented a low-impedance output.[995] A separate 15.7 V rail in the same setup stayed at 0 V until its own supply was enabled, showing no such coupling.[995] Rails held at intermediate voltages by these paths can prevent circuitry from starting up correctly in ways that are difficult to anticipate from the schematic.[995]

## Rails as the limit on analog signals

A rail sets a hard ceiling on what the circuitry between the rails can do. A typical op amp cannot swing its output closer than about 1.5 V to the positive rail, so a 10 V peak output requires V+ of at least 11.5 V; lowering V+ toward that figure brings on clipping.[600] The same constraint applies to inputs: the inputs of an LM3914 will not operate all the way up to the rail, which is why a battery voltage used as the measured signal is divided down rather than applied directly.[204] In comparator operation the output saturates hard at V+ for the smallest positive input difference and slams to the negative rail when the difference reverses.[600] Rails also fix the arithmetic of threshold circuits — ±10 V rails with a 9 K and 1 K divider give the hysteresis of a Schmitt trigger — and of simple DC analysis, where a midpoint sitting at half rail is a useful sanity check.[941][1427] A logic-level output likewise swings to whatever rail powers it: PWM from a microcontroller on a 3.3 V rail switches between 0 V and 3.3 V.[225]

## Troubleshooting by the rails

The first rule of troubleshooting is "thou shalt test voltages": check the voltages and check the voltage rails before anything else, then work down from there.[65][400] Rails are checked early for the same reason on any repair — a unit with a blown resistor and an open bridge rectifier gets its regulators and rails verified as a matter of course.[777] Checking that the rails are present at the supply is not sufficient; the rails must also be confirmed at the pins of the chip that consumes them, which is easily done at a device with known power pins such as an EEPROM.[777][710] A rail measured on an intercom board at 5.21 V is high but within specification, and can be taken as good.[710]

Symptoms often point at a rail indirectly. A display that dims can only be dimming because the rail feeding it is dipping, since a rising rail would brighten it.[621] A signal generator whose output amplitude is low under load suggests insufficient current drive and a rail sagging, though a board with no test points for the rail makes this awkward to confirm.[1525] A power supply that refuses to bring the system up may be reporting a fault because a single rail — a −5 V rail, for example — is dead, which is what a properly designed system does rather than half-starting.[1348] Where a driver chip is not working, tracing its VCC back to the 3.9 V rail that should feed it, and finding that rail absent, localises the fault to the circuitry deriving it.[1394]

Measuring every rail rather than the obvious one is the discipline that makes the method work. On a universal programmer showing signs of shutdown, the 5 V, 12 V, 48 V, −5 V and 2.1 V rails were all measured before conclusions were drawn; the 12 V, 48 V, −5 V and 2.1 V rails were fine and the 5 V rail was dead.[1061] On a Tektronix 475 the unregulated 50 V section read 67 V and the 5 volt rail read 8.6 V, with plus 5, plus 8, minus 8, plus 50 and further rails all needing to be swept.[jmb9ICnI8xI] A 3.3 V rail that failed during operation measured 18 ohms — effectively a short — which is a far easier fault to chase than a vague misbehaviour.[400]

Rails can also be exonerated. On a dead LCD television all the measurable rails checked out fine, with 5.3 V and around 10 mV of ripple on the supplies, which is grounds to stop suspecting the electrolytics and look at the panel instead; reflexively replacing capacitors is not diagnosis.[780] On an oscilloscope channel fault, verifying the voltage rails and the signals out of the hybrid was what built confidence that the hybrid module was not at fault.[565]

### Intermittent and coupled faults

Faults that appear only after warm-up require several rails to be watched simultaneously. Four or five multimeters attached to the rails at once, with the probes fitted before power is applied, catch a rail that drops out after five or ten minutes without anyone probing live circuitry — which matters where dangerous voltages such as a 58 V rail and a 2.2 kV EHT supply are present.[1452][803] Instrumenting the rails first and then going hands-off is the safe approach on high-voltage equipment.[803]

Rails that share a reference fail together in a distinctive pattern. In an HP1740A oscilloscope every voltage rail reference was tied to the 15 V reference, so a failure on the 5 V rail dragged the 15 V rail down, and the 15 V rail dragged all the remaining rails with it; the giveaway was that every rail dropped by exactly the same percentage.[804] A shorted rail can equally be pulled down by a fault on a different board fed from the same supply, so the board holding the fault is not necessarily the board showing it.[1061] The usual failure mode on a rail is over-voltage, and a rail that has gone over-voltage tends to take multiple parts with it — a failed −15 V rail took out at least one op amp and three voltage regulators, which is a reason to suspect the other devices on that rail after the first faulty part is found.[593] Not every rail is ground-referenced: inside a multimeter the rails are shifted rather than sitting at a common reference, which makes probing them without a schematic misleading.[1520]

## Design for serviceability

Access to the rails is a design decision. Some equipment fits labelled links in series with each rail so the rail can be broken and its current measured, or the voltage checked at a marked test point such as one labelled plus 8.6 — links placed deliberately for ease of servicing and troubleshooting.[208] A power-on link on a television supply serves the same purpose.[1145] Rail voltages silkscreened on the board, as 1V0, 1V8 and 1V2 were on one oscilloscope revision, and pinouts printed next to a supply connector, make rails identifiable without a schematic.[1510][1145][1388] The opposite practice is a legitimate criticism: connectors labelled only with reference designators rather than their system function, with no test point voltages anywhere on the board, make a supply unfriendly to service.[814]

Rails are exercised deliberately during development and production too. A prototype loom loaded with simulated loads on the different voltage rails is used to test power supply modules before they go into a final board.[614] Old-school universal programmers went further, driving the voltage rails to their extremes during verification so a device was checked over a much wider range than its nominal supply.[1060]
