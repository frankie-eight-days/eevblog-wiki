# linear power supply

A linear power supply derives a regulated DC output by rectifying and filtering a mains transformer's secondary, then dropping the surplus voltage across a series pass element operated in its linear region.[90][742][715] Its defining characteristic is that the excess voltage is not recovered but dissipated as heat, which makes it heavy, bulky and inefficient — and, in exchange, gives it noise and ripple performance a switch-mode supply is very hard pressed to match.[166][107] It remains the preferred topology for bench supplies and for the internal rails of precision instruments, where output cleanliness matters more than efficiency.[166][828][1173]

## Topology and construction

The canonical arrangement is a mains transformer, a bridge rectifier, bulk filter capacitance, and a regulator.[742][715] At its simplest the regulator is a three-terminal device with only an input and an output plus a couple of filter capacitors, stepping something like 9 V down to 5 V in the manner of a 7805.[90] Historic instrument supplies follow exactly this pattern: a TO-3 LM309K fed by a 4700 µF filter capacitor generating the 5 V rail for the digital section of a bench multimeter, with smaller local linear regulation distributed around the boards.[613] Larger examples scale the same blocks up — a dual bridge rectifier built from four panel-mounted diodes feeding three 15,000 µF 40 V filter capacitors.[574] Multiple electrolytic capacitors in parallel after the bridge are a standard sight in such supplies.[742] The bulk capacitance at the supply output stores charge for low-frequency events, notably smoothing the 50 or 100 Hz mains-derived ripple of a bridge-rectified linear supply.[1085]

Rectifier diodes in these supplies are unremarkable parts — a nominal 1 A bridge such as a DF02 is typical of a basic linear supply, though its single half-sine peak forward surge rating can reach 50 A.[376] The forward drop is the cost: roughly 0.6 V per diode, rising toward a volt at higher currents, so two bridges in a supply throw away about two volts.[711] MOSFET-based synchronous rectifier controllers such as the LT4320 substitute low on-resistance for that drop and recover the loss, making an already-linear supply more efficient still.[711]

Not every linear supply is regulated. The external brick that powered the Apple IIc was a transformer, a full-wave bridge rectifier and output capacitors with a nominal 15 V DC at 1.2 A rating and no regulation at all, with regulation handled downstream by a DC-to-DC converter inside the machine.[788] A homebrew 25 A linear supply on a homebrew chassis is a recognised amateur construction project, and a bench supply built around an LM317 and a couple of knobs is one of the classic build-it-yourself projects for a new lab.[428][168]

## Dissipation, heat sinking and transformer taps

The central design constraint is the power lost in the pass element, equal to the input-to-output differential times the load current. A supply rated 30 V at 3 A set to deliver 1 V from a 30 V rail must drop 30 V at 3 A, an enormous dissipation in the output transistor and heat sink.[828] The same arithmetic applies at 3.3 V out from a 40 V transformer rail, and to battery-powered equipment, where a linear regulator dropping 12 V to 3.3 V wastes a large fraction of the battery's capacity as heat in the heat sink.[512][258] A 34 V maximum output at 5 A output current would likewise mean dissipating a huge amount of power.[440]

The standard mitigation in variable bench supplies is to tap the transformer secondary and switch taps as the output voltage setting rises, keeping the differential across the pass element bounded. An Atten triple-output supply brings out 7.6 V, 19 V and 34 V taps switched by two relays, which is why its heat sink can be far smaller than the roughly 150 W per channel rating would otherwise demand.[440] The Korad KA3005P uses three taps and two relays on the same principle, switching in taps above certain voltages to reduce heat sink dissipation in a 30 V 5 A supply whose heat sink is not large by the standards of that rating.[314] A Siglent bench supply switches taps according to the selected voltage range.[828] The Rigol DP832 does the same job with triacs and triac drivers instead of the more traditional relays.[512][509] Range switching is also what gives such supplies their split specifications — 0 to 8 V at 3 A per channel and 0 to 20 V at 1.5 A per channel in a roughly 60 W two-output unit.[655]

Even with tapping, the topology costs size and mass. A linear supply needs a big heat sink on the back and is wasteful in energy for a given output power.[166] Weight is a reliable tell: a 40 A switch-mode supply weighing three and a half to four kilograms would weigh a ton as a linear, and the presence of a linear supply inside an instrument can be identified by its weight alone.[272][314][1173] Delivering 60 W per module, or 180 W total, linearly demands a big thumping transformer, which is precisely why high-density instrument supplies go switch-mode.[755] Where a 7.5-digit multimeter has no room for a large linear transformer, a switch-mode supply becomes mandatory and the design effort shifts to keeping its switching noise out of the analog section, using an electrostatically shielded transformer to lower primary-to-secondary capacitance.[731] A 360 W bench supply is a switching design for the same reason.[1691]

## Ripple and noise behaviour

Ripple is correctly understood as the charge-discharge cycle of the storage element in any supply, linear or switch-mode; it is not exclusively mains hum.[594] In a linear supply, however, that ripple does appear at the mains-derived frequency — 50 or 60 Hz, or 100 or 120 Hz after full-wave rectification.[594] This has a practical consequence for measurement: an oscilloscope can be triggered from the AC line to hold the ripple stationary on screen, a technique that works only for a linear supply and fails on a switch-mode converter with a free-running switching frequency.[594]

Because a linear supply has no switching stage, any high-frequency content observed on its output is diagnostic rather than intrinsic. Measuring a linear bench supply and seeing high-frequency peaks in the trace indicates common-mode noise entering the measurement system from elsewhere, not noise generated by the supply.[441][594] The absence of switching components is also the basis for using a linear bench supply as a clean reference source when characterising the noise of some other converter under test.[1606]

## Applications and design judgment

As a class, the best bench power supplies are linear rather than switch-mode, because the noise and ripple performance is much better than a switching design can readily achieve.[166] The consistent engineering position is that linear is heaps better than switch mode on simplicity and noise grounds, and that switch-mode should be adopted only where it is required — to step up or boost a voltage, or to obtain higher efficiency.[107] Instrument teardowns bear this out: linear supplies are standard inside older laboratory gear, and the internals of a linear bench supply are typically undramatic, with commodity op-amps such as the LF442, LM393 and LF411 and ten-turn pots to trim the output range.[620][166] A ten-turn potentiometer is the recommended adjustment element for a self-built linear supply without microcontroller control; coarse-and-fine dual controls on a commercial supply are a giveaway that a cheaper pot was used instead.[225]

Linear supplies are also chosen where isolation or output quality is the point — a linear mains adapter supplied with an insulation resistance meter for its higher isolation voltage, or a linear rail feeding an audio DAC specifically to avoid switching components in the signal path.[468][1613] Instrument-internal linear supplies show up wherever the digital and analog sections need only modest power, such as the small linear supply inside an electronic load, which powers only its own circuitry rather than a load output.[1023] Commercially, a 150 W linear programmable supply with USB serial control sold for AUD 199, and a completely linear laboratory supply offering 0 to 100 V with 1 µA current resolution and 40 W per channel, 80 W total, represents the high end of the topology.[315][1402] Advertised linear supplies are not always purely linear: a linear output stage may be preceded by a switching tracking pre-regulator, an arrangement that yields more output power in a smaller form factor at the cost of a switching element in the chain.[1402]

One failure scenario is specific to the topology: feeding voltage back into the supply from the output, as when a charged battery remains connected while the input voltage is switched off, is a common situation for traditional linear supplies and raises the question of whether the regulator survives it.[233] For characterising any supply, linear or switch-mode, a precision DC electronic load is the appropriate instrument, and is among the most overlooked items in a lab.[281]

The rectifier arrangements learned in linear supplies generalise. The half-wave and full-wave rectifiers of a linear supply are the reference point for understanding Cockcroft-Walton multipliers, where a full-wave version is built by mirroring the circuit and adding a transformer tap exactly as in a linear supply.[469] The diode-and-capacitor output filter of a microcontroller voltage inverter is the familiar linear-supply filter with the diode reversed for negative rails.[483] A precision peak detector behaves like the ordinary diode rectifier of a linear supply, except that the op-amp forces the output to equal the input exactly.[490] A linear power supply is itself one of the everyday realisations of an ideal voltage source, which in practice must be modelled with a series resistance.[1397]
