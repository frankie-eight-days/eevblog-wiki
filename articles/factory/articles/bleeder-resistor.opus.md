# bleeder resistor

A bleeder resistor is a resistor placed permanently across a capacitor so that the stored charge drains away once power is removed.[268][1482] Its purpose is safety and serviceability: a large filter capacitor left with no discharge path can hold hundreds of volts for a long time after the equipment is unplugged, and the energy is sufficient to injure whoever opens the case.[631][1388] Because the resistor is always connected, it also appears in circuit during resistance measurements and can make surrounding components look shorted when they are not.[1364]

## What it does and why it is fitted

A well-designed supply puts bleed resistors on the main filter capacitors so the reservoir energy is gone shortly after shutdown.[725] The effect can be quick: on one instrument the front-panel LED extinguished only a second or two after the plug was pulled, which is the visible signature of bleeders draining the rails.[452] On a switchmode supply with a 1 kV cap on the output, a 330 ohm 1 W bleeder drains the output filter capacitors very fast at switch-off, and details of that kind separate the better supplies from the cheap ones.[272]

The same reasoning applies at the other end of the scale. A 10 kW argon-ion laser supply carries a bleed resistor across its main capacitor bank, so a board that had sat in a dumpster for a decade or more could be handled without concern about residual charge.[1381] A 600 V, 5100 W supply that had been powered off for a long period was likewise expected to have bleeders, though the working habit is still to probe the bus bars and confirm both the secondary high-voltage DC side and the primary side are dead before touching anything.[814]

## Values and typical implementations

Bleeder resistors are high in value relative to the load, since they dissipate power continuously while the equipment runs. Across mains input filter capacitors a 1 meg half-watt axial part is representative.[268] In an X-class dropper supply, two 750 k resistors in series give 1.5 meg total across a 220 nF cap, which is enough to remove the charge from the pins after the appliance is unplugged.[1481][1482] Where a high-voltage rail is built from a series stack of capacitors, resistors across each element serve two functions at once — bleeding and voltage balancing; a source measure unit using four series banks of three paralleled ceramics used 10 meg parts for the job.[607] The same dual role appears on supplies where the resistors across the two main capacitors provide sharing as well as discharge.[1301]

Bleeders are also found on smaller and less obvious nodes: across the DC output rail of a bench supply to discharge the output capacitor,[861] across a mains input filter cap in parallel with the X-class part,[272] across the MOV in a console supply to remove residual charge when the cord is pulled,[1164] and in linear supplies where the resistors sit across the reservoir caps that protrude through the chassis.[709] A single design may carry several, for instance one large bleeder on the pass-transistor side and another across the bank of 2,200 µF 63 V filter caps.[512]

## When they are absent

Omission is common, and it is the case that matters practically. A vintage oscilloscope's 5,300 µF capacitor retained charge with the regulator pass transistor switched off simply because no bleeder was fitted.[804] A 4K television's main filter capacitor was still at 150 V DC with the set unplugged, decaying only through the 4 meg loading of the probe attached to it.[1388] An LCD television's main capacitor held a couple of hundred volts and would take tens of minutes to decay on its own, a case complicated further by dielectric absorption returning charge to a capacitor that has been briefly shorted.[631] Consumer gear built without serviceability in mind frequently has none — a solar monitoring unit's DC input caps sat charged to mains potential and delivered a shock during disassembly.[1511] Capacitor-based mains products sold on dubious energy-saving claims are another category where a bleeder is often simply not present.[1191]

Some equipment discharges itself through the rest of the circuit even without a dedicated bleeder; a vintage computer's 35 V rail was found already at zero for that reason, which is fortunate rather than by design.[905]

## Bench practice

The engineering rule is that the presence of a bleeder is assumed but never trusted: always measure before touching.[365][780] The low-impedance (low-Z) voltage function on a multimeter, nominally intended to kill ghost voltages, is well suited to this because it places a couple of kilohms across the capacitor while measuring it, both reading and discharging in one operation.[631][8blgmbXfDEc] If no voltage is present the meter drops to a megohm resistance reading, confirming the node is safe.[780] Where the meter lacks the function, an appropriately calculated high-power dummy resistor across the capacitor serves the same purpose,[630] and on a set with no bleeder at all the main filter cap can be shorted out once removed.[1388] For measurements that must be made with the supply live, a high-voltage differential probe is the correct instrument on the primary side of a mains switcher.[630][1388]

Bleeders also complicate diagnosis. Diodes that appear shorted or that read the same in both directions may simply have a bleed resistor of a couple of hundred ohms in parallel with them, combining with the secondary-side winding resistance to produce a misleading in-circuit reading.[1364] In a dropper-supply repair, the bleeder and dropper resistor both measured correctly while the actual fault lay in the capacitor value, a reminder that a component measuring fine in circuit does not narrow the fault as much as measuring the rail voltage would.[1481]
