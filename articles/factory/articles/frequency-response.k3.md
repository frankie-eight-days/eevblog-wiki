# frequency response

Frequency response is the measure of a system's output amplitude (and often phase) as a function of input frequency, typically presented as a Bode plot of magnitude in decibels versus frequency on a logarithmic axis.[396][49] It characterises how a circuit, instrument, transducer, or acoustic space treats signals of different frequencies, and it is the standard way to specify bandwidth, flatness, and roll-off behaviour of amplifiers, probes, filters, microphones, and loudspeakers.[49][602][453] An idealised amplifier response rolls off at some low frequency and some high frequency, with gain essentially constant in between, and such responses can span many decades — for example 1 Hz to 1 MHz is six decades — which is why logarithmic frequency axes and decibel magnitude scales are used: the log axis converts the logarithmic response into a linear slope and fits wide spans on one graph, while dB scaling lets small and large signals be viewed in the same space.[49][225]

## Interpreting a response plot

Bandwidth is conventionally defined at the −3 dB point, where output amplitude has fallen to 0.707 of its midband value; the measurement is made by increasing input frequency until the output drops to that level.[572][972] Real responses are not always textbook-flat with a clean 6 dB-per-octave roll-off: peaking near the band edge is a common deviation caused by the characteristics of the particular circuit.[572] Responses may also undulate — falling and rising again with frequency — which points to effects such as transmission-line reflections rather than ordinary roll-off, since cable loss simply increases monotonically with frequency.[652]

When characterising devices, spot figures at single frequencies can be misleading. A common mode rejection ratio, for example, varies strongly with frequency, so a response curve is more informative than isolated values; a differential probe specified at −40 dB at 10 MHz may degrade sharply above that point, which is why datasheet figures often stop there.[1521][Dez9KG6whb0] A measured CMRR curve for one high-voltage differential probe began falling off at a couple of megahertz, an effect attributed to the input divider resistors being difficult to compensate at high frequency.[Dez9KG6whb0]

## Measurement methods

A spectrum analyser with a tracking generator produces a frequency response plot directly, but such analysers are RF instruments — for example 9 kHz to 1.5 GHz — and are unsuitable for audio and lower-frequency work.[396] The manual alternative needs only a function generator and a multimeter (or oscilloscope) of sufficient bandwidth: the input frequency is stepped up, the amplitude at each spot frequency is recorded, and the table of values is plotted in a spreadsheet.[396]

A swept function generator can produce a real-time response plot on an ordinary oscilloscope. The generator's sweep gate or trigger pulse defines one complete frequency sweep — say 1 Hz to 100 kHz — and triggering the scope from that pulse maps the horizontal axis onto frequency, displaying the filter's amplitude response as a repetitive trace; disabling the device under test yields a straight line because the generator's output is flat across the sweep.[396] The limitation is the oscilloscope's typically 8-bit ADC, which restricts dynamic range compared with a dedicated dynamic signal analyser or FFT analyser with a much higher-resolution converter.[396] In simulation, the equivalent is AC analysis, with the sweep type (for example linear) chosen to match the physical function generator's sweep.[396]

Dedicated frequency response analysers sweep over ranges such as 10 Hz to 20 MHz and beyond, with sweeps running noticeably slower when started at very low frequencies.[1103] The same measurement can be displayed as a polar plot, a magnitude-versus-frequency plot, or an impedance plot — these are different presentations of identical underlying data.[1103] Dynamic signal analysers intended for physical measurements extend down to DC and cover the DC-to-tens-of-kilohertz range typical of vibration and sound phenomena, adding mathematical functions such as coherence that operate on two input channels.[1443]

## Test setup pitfalls

The measurement chain must be validated before the device under test. In a capacitor impedance measurement, for instance, the capacitor is first replaced with a shunt resistor to confirm the response is flat and that cabling or the fixture introduces no artefacts.[859] Missing 50-ohm termination produces obvious errors — magnitude shooting up past 1 MHz — and an unterminated coax run driven by a function generator shows amplitude that undulates with frequency rather than rolling off consistently, dropping from about 1 V to under 900 mV across the sweep before recovering.[1103][652]

The physical environment of the device also alters its response. A high-voltage probe that measured near-unity in free air dropped to a scale factor of 0.4 at the top of its range once potted, because stray capacitances not present in free air were exaggerated by the dielectric effect of the potting resin.[85] Conversely, the coax and compensation networks in quality ×10 oscilloscope probes are specifically engineered — with low-dielectric-loss insulation and matched compensation — to hold a flat, usable response beyond 500 MHz in some cases.[453]

## Frequency response of components

Capacitors depart from ideal behaviour through equivalent series inductance, producing impedance-versus-frequency curves with resonant dips whose sharpness depends on construction — a ceramic part shows a sharper dip than a film part of similar capacitance, because the resonance is set by the lead frame and package rather than capacitance value.[1117] Paralleling capacitors with different resonant frequencies combines their responses into a broader low-impedance region, and feedthrough-style configurations can yield a very low-Q broadband filter 30 dB down across a gigahertz, starting around 20 MHz and reaching 10 GHz depending on part choice.[1103][9V99J22aiLE] Mounting a surface-mount capacitor vertically on the board reduces its inductance and improves its high-frequency response — a long-standing hobbyist trick for cheap parts.[9V99J22aiLE]

Op-amp circuits have their response shaped deliberately and incidentally. A small bypass capacitor of 10 pF to 100 pF in the feedback network rolls off the frequency response, and bandwidth always trades against gain through the gain-bandwidth product.[600] Op-amp input noise also has a characteristic frequency dependence: white Gaussian noise dominates from around 10–100 Hz upward, while 1/f noise tails up at lower frequencies, which is why peak-to-peak input noise is specified over a low-frequency band such as 0.1–10 Hz.[528]

Active probes show their own roll-off: a 3 GHz active probe's gain stays flat to about 1 GHz, then droops, with a small kink in the response appearing after 2 GHz — acceptable for general use but a limitation for precision work at the top of the range.[1715] Spectrum analysers likewise differ in amplitude flatness across their range, with one instrument measurably flatter over a 1.5 GHz span than another, and about 10 dB more sensitivity with its preamplifier engaged.[891]

## Transducers and acoustic response

Laboratory measurement microphones achieve a ruler-flat response from about 10–20 Hz up to roughly 15 kHz, with a characterised (not perfectly flat) region from 15 kHz to 40 kHz deviating by about a decibel to a decibel and a half.[602] Loudspeakers used as microphones convert sound pressure to electrical energy surprisingly effectively but have poor frequency response in that role.[602] Microphone construction matters: back-electret types are markedly more stable than cheaper constructions in both frequency response and sensitivity.[608] Circuit topology matters too — a valve microphone's high output impedance means a heavily capacitive load makes the frequency response droop.[609] Ruggedising a microphone for vandal-prone public address service can cost response directly, turning a once roughly flat capsule into one rolling off at a couple of hundred hertz.[354]

Consumer specifications illustrate typical figures: a vintage cassette Walkman quoted 40 Hz to 12 kHz, studio monitors 65 Hz to 23 kHz, and a well-designed two-way speaker can be essentially flat down to 20 Hz with no obvious peaks or bumps across the range.[752][169][adSk6suM29E] Room acoustics and microphone placement dominate loudspeaker measurements: absorption panels change the midrange decay while leaving the low end (40–50 Hz) and the top octave essentially identical, and moving the microphone can introduce dramatic dips, such as one near 13 kHz and another at 8.3 kHz, that are positional artefacts rather than speaker behaviour.[IVWhoGFJQAY]

## Weighting and deliberately non-flat responses

Not every instrument should be flat. Sound level weightings filter the response for purpose: A-weighting approximates the nonlinear frequency response of human hearing and is used for loudness-type specifications such as fan noise; C-weighting suits loud measurements; and Z-weighting (flat, unfiltered) is the correct choice for instrument measurement calculations.[1745] Ambient low-frequency content, such as air-conditioning duct noise around 60–115 Hz, contaminates acoustic measurements and motivates high-pass filtering, for example a 60 Hz high-pass in a microphone's DSP chain to reject stand-borne rumble.[1745][f7jPJek3XuQ] Similarly, a light meter must not have a flat spectral response; its response must follow CIE recommendations approximating the human eye's response to light.[362]

## Instrument bandwidth specifications

AC bandwidth specifications deserve scrutiny. A handheld multimeter specified to 20 kHz may keep responding past 100 kHz before dropping off several hundred kilohertz later.[249] A bench meter's 300 kHz AC bandwidth applies only to sinusoids; complex waveforms begin rolling off much earlier, around 160 kHz in one measurement.[489] Other instruments quote frequency response to 10 kHz for true RMS or to 60 MHz.[1378][986] Devices operated outside their specified supply may retain AC performance well beyond expectations — one reference held within a few percent to 36 kHz and reached its 0.707 point near 120 kHz even under reduced supply voltage.[972]
