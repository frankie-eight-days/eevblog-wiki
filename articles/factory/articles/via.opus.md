# via

A via is a plated hole through a printed circuit board that carries a net from one copper layer to another. Structurally it is nothing special: on a bare board a via and a component pad are constructed in exactly the same way, and there is no practical electrical difference between them — the two are simply used for different purposes.[543] The plating is applied during manufacture by passing a large current through the panel in an electrolysis bath, coating the inside of every drilled hole with copper so that it connects the top and bottom layers.[939]

Vias are the currency of PCB routing. Every one of them costs board area, splits a plane, adds inductance, and adds a drill hole to the fabrication file, which is why the number of vias on a layout is treated as a proxy for how well that layout was thought through.[1323][410]

## Types and finishes

The designer, not the board house, decides what happens to the top of a via.[500] A **tented** via has solder mask flowed over the hole, which is the default on most prototype boards.[566][1306] Tenting is not the same as plugging: solder mask over the opening leaves the barrel hollow, whereas plugged vias are a separate process.[1306] **Via-in-pad** places the hole inside a component land and is normally filled with resin.[1259] **Blind and buried** vias connect only a subset of layers rather than passing through the whole stack.[500][1216] Solder-filled vias are not produced at the PCB fabrication stage at all — the fill happens during assembly, either by wave soldering so the wave bubbles up into the untented holes, or by opening the solder paste mask over them.[500] Vias filled with solder carry considerably more current than open ones.[500]

Tenting control matters most on fine-pitch parts. On a BGA fan-out where the via sits a fraction of a millimetre from a ball, solder paste that bridges to an untented via produces a short underneath a package that cannot be inspected, and the fault will not be discovered until power-up.[193] Forcing tenting on those vias removes the solder mask expansion and eliminates the bridging path.[193] Some tools make this awkward: KiCad's tenting control is board-wide, with no per-via override, which is a real limitation on designs that need some vias tented and others open.[1193]

## Current and thermal duty

A single via has finite current capacity. A working rule of thumb is roughly one via for every half amp, and any trace carrying serious current should use a minimum of two.[244] Boards built to carry tens of amps show this directly — a multimeter's 10 A path may be stitched with a very large number of vias in parallel to keep the path impedance down.[1447] Vias are also added purely to increase current capacity, sometimes as a retrofit with extra traces on the bottom layer tied through.[813] A high-current, low-inductance feed to a large processor typically appears as a dense field of vias directly beneath the die.[491]

The same hole conducts heat. A power device with its heatsink on the opposite side of the board relies on a via array underneath the package to move heat through the laminate — nine vias under a thermal pad is a classic arrangement.[895][326] The thermal path then has to be accounted for as a chain: junction to case, case to PCB, top side to bottom side through the vias, contact resistance to the heatsink, and finally the heatsink itself.[895] Hundreds of small vias used to conduct power and heat through to the other side of a board is not the most efficient solution, but it is often good enough within a fixed case size.[607] Exposed copper areas dotted with via arrays under a chip are a reliable signature of a thermal pad.[443]

## Effect on planes and power integrity

Vias are not free from a power-delivery standpoint. Every via dropped through a ground or power plane chops that plane up, so a BGA fan-out that pushes a large number of vias through the stack fragments the very plane that was supposed to be one solid low-impedance block.[1216] The resulting DC drop is measurable: a rail feeding 8 A across a board can easily lose 40 mV before it reaches the load, with a further couple of millivolts of variation across the plane depending on how many vias are breaking it up.[1216] Boards without a solid ground plane end up with split planes and meandering return paths dictated entirely by wherever vias could be dropped.[1081]

Via inductance is the dominant term in high-frequency bypassing, and FPGA power-delivery application notes treat it as such alongside plane inductance and capacitance per square.[1117] The best achievable bypassing puts the capacitors on the underside of the package, directly beneath the pins, with vias running straight up to the balls, so that individual pins are bypassed rather than the rail as a whole.[1512] Where capacitors sit on the top side, they are via-stitched straight down to the ground and power layers.[1512] Vias placed for bypass and ground should be committed early — putting them in as part of placing the capacitors reveals exactly how much room is left to route signals out of the area.[1323] A layout that leaves the ground side unstitched while the signal routing proceeds has the order backwards.[1323]

Stitching also serves RF containment: vias run around the perimeter of a section keep return current local and hold RF impedance down.[1315][1416] Conversely, the deliberate absence of vias along an RF path is itself a design decision, visible on high-frequency interconnects where a signal jumps through a series termination without dropping layers.[470]

## Routing practice

The governing goal for a surface-mount design is to route as much as possible on the top layer and drop to another layer only when forced.[1323] The target is a single-sided layout every time — the holy grail — with vias treated as an admission that the placement did not quite work.[1323] On a typical review board, several dozen vias might be reduced to three or four once the power traces are moved to the bottom layer and brought up only where needed.[1323] The related discipline is directionality: routing traces horizontally on one layer and vertically on the other keeps the board tractable, whereas letting traces run in every direction fills the space and forces a large number of vias to recover.[1535]

Dropping vias immediately underneath surface-mount parts to reach the inner layers destroys routing density, which is why the traditional four-layer stackup keeps signals on the outer layers and ground and power in the middle.[1193] Jumps to the opposite layer should be kept as short as possible.[244] Component and pin choices feed back into via count — flipping parts so a bus runs along the correct side avoids a column of vias, and on programmable devices pin swapping can be used specifically to minimise the number of vias on the board.[974] A single well-placed via can also be additive rather than costly: taking a trace to the opposite side for a short run frees a clear routing corridor that was previously blocked.[974] Where a copper pour ends up broken into isolated islands, extra vias and jumpers are used to tie the regions back together.[974][1193]

Autorouters handle vias poorly. A router will get caught on an existing via and abandon a solution a human would reach by nudging the via a short distance, and rip-up-and-retry algorithms will place a via, route past a constraint, then tear the whole thing out again.[975][1535] A board with only a handful of vias in a dense layout is strong evidence of hand routing.[410]

## Manufacturing constraints

Via size drives cost and process class. Fanning out a fine-pitch BGA on individual layers forces drill sizes down to around 0.1–0.2 mm, which pushes the board into a much more expensive process class alongside 4 thou trace and space rules.[193] More ordinary designs use 0.6 mm plated holes for vias, and because fabricators charge per tool change, drill sizes should be consolidated rather than inherited piecemeal from mixed component libraries.[990] Annular ring is the other constraint: drill wander and drill tolerance mean a hole can skid off centre, so minimum annular rings are specified on both vias and pads, and a via sitting only a fraction of a millimetre from a track risks breaking it if the drill wanders.[1327] Where a fabricator's rules forbid routing between pads, the board may simply be rejected unless a via is added to drop the trace out.[1327]

Fabrication houses occasionally alter a design in ways that break it. Expanding the clearance around pads and vias beyond what the submitted Gerbers specified can sever a ground plane, leaving dead copper and an input ground with no electrical connection to the rest of the circuit.[155] Vias also appear in panelisation: several small holes drilled along a mouse bite let a board be broken out of its panel, though cutting through them with side cutters risks shorting adjacent tracks.[552] Plated edge vias are used for mechanical reinforcement of a board edge.[1336] Inspection of a finished board focuses on hole-to-annular-ring registration and the consistency of the barrel plating, both of which can be assessed optically, though a definitive answer requires cross-sectioning.[997][1306][817]

At the extreme end, a ceramic multi-chip module from the early 1990s used 100-micron vias in tens of thousands, and a comparable module packed over two million vias into 63 layers.[1341][1358]

## Failure modes

Vias fail in service. Alkaline electrolyte that reaches a board wicks into the barrels and eats them out from the inside; affected vias turn visibly black and the copper is consumed, and the leakage path a via provides is often how electrolyte gets underneath a chip in the first place.[1687][VZuebPVrzI8][1497][1527][qmcSEJehqZM] Old wave-soldered assemblies present a different problem: where the solder never flowed through the hole, the joint depends entirely on the through-hole plating, and after several decades of thermal cycling on a board with nearly all its copper on one side, that is an intermittent waiting to happen.[804][1572] Marginal vias are also a candidate for mechanical intermittents — board flex acting on a via with very little annular ring is a known, if obscure, defect mode.[9u8TEJdAVdQ] A short inside a via or pad on an eight- or ten-layer board is notoriously difficult to locate without a current-tracing probe.[296]

## Vias in repair and reverse engineering

Vias are the main obstacle to tracing a multilayer board optically, because a trace that drops into an inner layer disappears from both the top and bottom plots and has to be recovered with a fast continuity tester.[675] They are also the main aid: printing each layer onto overhead transparency and using the vias as alignment markers lets the sheets be overlaid so signals can be followed from one side to the other.[675] Where a via must be probed, the annular ring is scraped clear first; tented vias cannot be probed at all without breaking the mask.[1415][1428]

Vias give mod wires somewhere to go. Large pads and large vias make it straightforward to run a wire from a trace on one side of the board to the other, which is a common requirement when modifying a board, and a wire fed down a via rather than tacked to its surface has far more mechanical strength.[738][1215][879] On multilayer boards with blind and buried vias, that option disappears.[1215] Vias on membrane-keypad boards are usually covered in conductive carbon ink, which also coats the inside of the hole — soldering into such a via is a mistake, and the correct approach is to gently scrape the top clear and lay the wire flat.[962] Single-sided boards may carry via-like holes that do not actually connect to the other side, ruling out a back-side solder joint entirely.[505] For repairing broken traces or filling vias on home-made boards, conductive ink pens are effective.[1244]
