# ohms law

Ohm's law is the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in a conductor: voltage equals current multiplied by resistance, and the three quantities have a direct influence on each other so that changing any one changes the others.[27aG9xhfk6s][1216] It is named after George Ohm, who discovered it.[27aG9xhfk6s] It is the single most-used piece of arithmetic in practical electronics — the tool for sizing a dropper resistor, predicting a voltage drop along a PCB trace, working out how much power a pass transistor must dissipate, and explaining why a measurement instrument disturbs the circuit it is measuring.[1401][1216][440][1604] Along with Kirchhoff's laws it is the foundation on which every other DC and AC analysis technique is built, and it holds in every practical circuit: "Ohm's law always must be obeyed."[819][600][1439]

## Statement and forms

The three algebraic forms — V = I × R, I = V / R, and R = V / I — are rearrangements of one equation, and are conventionally memorised through the Ohm's law triangle, a visual device in which covering the quantity to be solved for leaves the correct expression of the other two.[1688][1401] Given current and resistance, the voltage across a component is the product of the two; given resistance and voltage, the current through it is the voltage divided by the resistance.[27aG9xhfk6s] A higher voltage across a given resistor forces more current through it than a lower voltage does.[27aG9xhfk6s] Ohm's law together with the power equations belongs to a small set of formulas that has to be committed to memory before any circuit work is possible at all.[1401]

Power follows immediately from it: P = V × I, and by substitution P = I² × R and P = V² / R.[1009][140][1730] The squared forms are what make Ohm's law a thermal tool as much as an electrical one — a 1.6 V drop at 250 mA across a 6.4 Ω LED dropper resistor works out to a quarter of a watt by I²R, and 3.66 V across a 5 Ω multimeter current shunt is 2.5 W by V²/R.[RT3godBXkOg][376]

## Resistance is everywhere

The resistance in the equation need not be a resistor. It can be the internal resistance of a battery or power supply, a PCB trace, a length of wiring, transformer winding resistance, or the effective output resistance of a logic gate's output MOSFET; whenever current passes through any of them a voltage drop appears and power is dissipated.[1401][sr1DOHnJi8I] Copper on a PCB is a conductor with a non-zero resistance, so a trace carrying current drops voltage, which is one of the constraints on trace current rating.[1559] Even a large ground plane is not zero ohms: at eight amps flowing across a board, a measurable drop develops over the distance travelled, which is why lowering trace resistance is the standard remedy for excessive DC voltage drop.[1216] Splitting a supply's bulk capacitance into three parallel electrolytics divides the current between three traces, and each carries a third of the current and therefore develops a smaller drop — a real consideration in high-power systems even with 1 oz copper.[742]

The same logic sets the scale of what can be ignored. Test leads of a milliohm or so drop essentially nothing into a 10 MΩ multimeter load, so remote sensing is unnecessary there while a heavy load would demand it.[ZYC763Vx9O8] A resistor that runs hot cannot be a kilohm-range part, because kilohms would require thousands of volts to dissipate appreciable power — visible overheating therefore identifies a low-value resistor before it is even measured.[1423] A transformer primary reading 0.2 Ω rather than the expected tens or hundreds of ohms implies a catastrophic power figure across mains voltage, and hence shorted turns with the enamel insulation burnt off.[1152]

A battery's internal resistance produces the difference between its loaded and unloaded terminal voltage, which is why an open-circuit measurement is meaningless for characterising discharge curves or cut-off voltages.[779]

## Consequence: zero volts means zero current

Because current through a resistance is proportional to the voltage across it, forcing the two ends of a resistor to the same potential guarantees no current, whatever the resistance value is. This is exploited directly in guard-ring design: a buffer amplifier actively drives the guard trace to the same voltage as the high-impedance input node, so no voltage difference exists across the board's surface leakage paths and no leakage current flows, regardless of contamination, fingerprint oils, or moisture on the PCB.[1755] The same reasoning explains why an op-amp input bias current cannot flow through a resistor that has zero volts across it and must instead come entirely through the feedback resistor,[479] and why a current-limit control node with the same 10 V on both sides of its resistor passes zero current no matter how hard the load demands more.[221]

## Design calculations

Series dropper resistors are the canonical application. For an LED string, the resistor value is the supply voltage minus the LED forward voltage, divided by the desired current: 1.6 V at 250 mA gives 6.4 Ω, rounded to the nearest E12 value of 6.8 Ω;[RT3godBXkOg] 3.3 V minus a 1.8 V LED at 20 mA gives 75 Ω;[1491] 4 V minus a 2 V LED across 100 Ω gives 20 mA.[1427] Where the LED drop is an order of magnitude smaller than the supply it can simply be dropped from the calculation.[286] The extreme case is a 1 MΩ LED dropper in an electric fence controller, where ignoring the diode drop against 1,000 V yields a convenient rule of thumb of 1 mA per kilovolt, so a 20 mA LED maximum corresponds to 20 kV.[1277] The position of the series resistor within a string is irrelevant to the result.[1617]

Zener shunt regulator design is the same arithmetic twice over. The dropper value is the input-to-Zener voltage difference divided by the test current — 12 V in, 5.1 V out, 50 mA, giving 138 Ω.[908] Regulation performance then falls out of the Zener's dynamic resistance: raising the input to 15 V pushes the current to 71.7 mA, and the resulting current change of 21.7 mA across 5 Ω of dynamic resistance shifts the output by about 0.11 V, from 5.1 V to 5.21 V, roughly 2%.[908]

Precision current sources are built by imposing a known voltage across a known resistor. A 1.25 V bandgap across 10 kΩ sets 125 µA;[329] a 1.25 V reference across a 1.25 Ω four-terminal precision resistor sets exactly 1 A;[577] a 10 V reference lifted by an op amp across a precision resistor gives a calculable load current, measured at 1.008 µA against prediction;[579] and 1 V across 1 Ω in an op-amp dummy load gives 1 A.[102] For an LM317-style regulator, the adjust-pin current flowing through the lower divider resistor adds a voltage-drop term to the output by Ohm's law, which is the origin of the I_adj × R2 term in the datasheet equation.[158][204]

Small currents through large resistances are the dominant error source in precision analog design. A 100 pA input bias current through a 10 kΩ source resistance produces a 1 µV offset error;[479] 30 pA through a 100 kΩ resistor produces 3 µV, which the op amp's gain of 100 multiplies to 300 µV at the output;[479] and 10 µA through a 2 kΩ divider resistor produces 20 mV, a 2% error when the intended output is 1 V.[222] In each case the remedy begins with recognising which resistance the stray current is developing a voltage across.

Ohm's law also underpins the conversions between equivalent circuits: the Norton current of a Thevenin source is simply V_TH divided by R_TH, and the reverse conversion is V_TH = I_N × R_N.[1399] Shorting a 10 V source through 10 kΩ gives 1 mA, and dividing the 5 V Thevenin voltage by that short-circuit current gives a 5 kΩ Thevenin resistance.[1399] Nodal analysis solves for node voltages, after which the branch currents are recovered by a single division — V_A divided by R2.[820] Maximum power transfer is derived the same way, expressing load power as I²R with I calculated from the source voltage and the total series resistance.[1401]

## Instrumentation and measurement

Every ammeter inserts a shunt resistance into the circuit under test, and Ohm's law governs the resulting error. A 1 V source across 100 Ω should read 10 mA, but with a meter in series the total resistance becomes 100 Ω plus the shunt resistance R_S, so the reading is necessarily lower.[1604] The voltage the shunt develops is the burden voltage: 500 µA full scale through a 100 Ω shunt is 50 mV, the figure datasheets typically quote as maximum burden voltage.[929] Shunt values are chosen to hold that figure constant across ranges — 10 mΩ at 5 A and 100 mΩ at 500 mA both give 50 mV, an order of magnitude less current against an order of magnitude more resistance.[931] The same relation sets the lower limit of usefulness: 5 mA through a 10 mΩ shunt is only 50 µV full scale, which is not enough signal to resolve, so a low current range cannot reuse the high-current shunt.[1693] More generally, 1 mV per amp requires 1 mΩ, 1 mV per milliamp requires 1 Ω, 1 mV per microamp requires 1 kΩ, and 1 mV per nanoamp requires 1 MΩ.[72] Burden voltage is the reason a battery-life measurement setup cannot simply trust the supply voltage: the external meter's shunt drops voltage that must be accounted for at the device under test.[1533]

A voltmeter's input impedance is a resistor too, and Ohm's law converts it between the voltage and current domains. A 10 MΩ input in series with a reverse-biased Schottky diode carrying 473 nA — calculated as 4.73 V divided by 10 MΩ — is enough to invalidate a reverse-leakage measurement; at 2.2 µA the resistor would need 22 V across it, so a 5 V supply simply clamps the reading at 5 V.[1747] Run in reverse, the same calculation converts a measured leakage current into an equivalent resistance: 10 µA at 100 V is 10 MΩ, and a worst-case 5 µA at 1000 V corresponds to 200 MΩ of MOV leakage, high enough not to disturb a 10 MΩ input divider.[rT0g1QmKE5E] A femtoammeter's minimum full-scale range follows the same product: a 10 GΩ feedback resistor at 20 pA full scale gives 0.2 V, a 200 mV full-scale front end.[1755]

Where a four-terminal ohmmeter is unavailable, the measurement can be reconstructed from Ohm's law using two multimeters, one reading voltage and one reading current.[133] Input protection design uses it in the other direction: clamping diodes limit the voltage across the current shunt, and the maximum power in that shunt during the time the fuse takes to blow is then calculable.[373] The output resistance of a logic output is obtained the same way, from a 0.2 V drop at 5.2 mA of output current.[sr1DOHnJi8I]

## AC form

Ohm's law applies to AC exactly as it does to DC, with the substitution of impedance Z for resistance R.[1729][1660] Impedance is measured in ohms just as DC resistance is, and the AC equation states that impedance equals AC voltage divided by AC current.[1728] Strictly the DC form applies at a single instant in time, since both voltage and current are changing; in practice the instantaneous formulation can be set aside and the law applied directly to maxima or to RMS values, since I_max = V_max / R and I_RMS = V_RMS / R.[1660] Resistors are linear and introduce no phase shift, so their current-voltage relationship is unaffected by the change to AC.[1660]

For reactive components the resistance term becomes the reactance: inductive reactance X_L is ωL, or 2πfL, and an inductor can be treated exactly like a resistor if the phase relationship is not of interest, giving I_max = V_max / ωL.[1660] Capacitive reactance X_C is 1/ωC, carrying a −90° angle against the inductor's +90°, for pure components.[1470] Once phase is retained, voltages, currents, and impedances all acquire real and imaginary parts, expressed in rectangular or polar form, and the law itself is unchanged — only the arithmetic becomes complex rather than real.[1470][1729] Solving an AC circuit is therefore the same procedure as solving a DC one: total the impedances and divide the source voltage by the result, as with a total impedance of 10 − j75 Ω where the current is the voltage divided by that impedance, most easily handled in polar form.[1729][1661] Every DC technique — Kirchhoff's laws, nodal and mesh analysis, superposition — carries over intact.[1729] For a purely resistive AC load the power equations also survive unchanged as P = I²R and P = V²/R.[1730]

## Thermal analogy

Thermal design is mathematically identical to DC circuit theory, with power playing the role of current, thermal resistance the role of resistance, and temperature the role of voltage.[361][105] The case temperature rise of a transistor is therefore the dissipated power multiplied by the case thermal resistance, and temperature accumulates stage by stage from ambient up to the junction, which might be rated for an absolute maximum of around 120 °C.[744] Understanding Ohm's law and series circuit theory is essentially sufficient for the whole of practical heatsink design, which in most cases needs no more than back-of-the-envelope calculation followed by real measurements and a safety factor.[105]

## Limits and misapplications

The law describes linear resistances, and several common situations are not linear. A short circuit measured at 0.11 Ω with a milliamp-level ohmmeter should not be expected to match Ohm's law at 10 or 11 amps, because the short is non-linear, has a temperature coefficient, and heats up under load.[401] A PTC thermistor is deliberately non-linear for the same reason: a 2.25 kΩ device across 240 V mains would dissipate an unacceptable amount of power if its resistance stayed put, so the resistance must rise with temperature while remaining at its nominal value at low voltages.[1667] Battery discharge cannot be modelled by Ohm's law either, because the terminal voltage falls throughout the discharge; a product containing a DC-DC converter behaves as a constant-power load and must be assessed against constant-power discharge curves rather than constant-current ones.[1533] Where a supply's current limit interacts with an over-voltage condition, the interaction is again a simple division: 3.3 V into a 2 Ω load exceeds a 1 A limit.[1402]

Because the law is inescapable, it also functions as a falsification tool. Free-energy and over-unity claims collapse under a couple of simple calculations against datasheet values and nothing more complicated than Ohm's law.[708] A wireless power demonstration showing 0.52 V across 100 Ω delivers about 5.2 mA, not the 52 mA claimed;[1408] and a stated smartphone charging requirement of 4.2 V at 62 mA is 260 mW, against the 26 mW the same document admits to receiving.[MCyLO-1grEk] Practical engineering has built up Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, maximum power transfer, transmission line theory, and signal theory precisely to give usable insight above the level of the underlying physics, and none of it can be evaded — if energy did not flow through the conductor, megawatts could be carried on 30 gauge wire, which they cannot.[1439]

## Place in practice

Ohm's law is the entry point of the discipline and, in Dave Jones's assessment, close to its practical ceiling as well: everyday engineering work rarely requires mathematics much above Ohm's law, with the calculus taught at university going largely unused outside specialised roles.[131] Troubleshooting in particular depends on it, alongside Kirchhoff's current law, basic building-block circuits, and concepts such as power supply decoupling.[zyuRcsM0gjI] The scope is wide enough that even a mousetrap trigger reduces to a resistor and a voltage source,[191] a heating element that measures good will heat up when voltage is switched across it,[1481] a resistor cube can be attacked with Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law before symmetry simplifies it,[1472] and a microcontroller doing Ohm's law arithmetic on the fly can turn an electronic load into a constant-resistance or constant-power load.[140]
