# dc dc converter

A DC-to-DC converter takes one DC voltage and produces another, using a switching element, an inductor or transformer as the energy storage element, and a rectifier and smoothing capacitor on the output.[110][67][594] Because the switch chops the input into an alternating waveform before it is stored and rectified again, a DC-to-DC converter is an AC circuit internally, however DC its terminals appear.[1417] It is the near-universal way of generating supply rails in modern equipment: almost any product with a battery, a processor, or more than one voltage rail contains at least one, and often a dozen.[15][1124][731][851]

## Topologies

The three basic arrangements are step-up (boost), step-down (buck), and inverting; they differ only slightly in circuit configuration, and the design formulas for each differ only slightly as well.[110] A SEPIC converter accepts an input either above or below its output, which makes it attractive where a battery voltage crosses the target voltage during discharge — for example charging a three-cell lithium-ion pack of over 12 V from a 5 V USB input, where a plain boost would not cover the whole range.[259] The penalty is part cost: a SEPIC constant-current constant-voltage charger chip such as the LT1512 ran at $3.85 even in hundred-off quantity, which made it the most expensive semiconductor in the design it was considered for.[259]

At its simplest the whole converter is a single transistor switch driving an inductor, a diode, and a smoothing capacitor, with the IC controlling the switch.[67] Constant-current output — as required to drive an LED — is obtained by having the controller regulate against a current sense shunt rather than an output voltage divider, and the sense resistor is usually identifiable by its physical size.[67] Output voltage in a voltage-mode part is set by a feedback divider against an internal reference; for the MC34063 the relationship is 1.25 V times one plus R2 over R1, rearranged to R2 equals Vout over 1.25 minus 1, times R1.[110] Other parts use different references — 1.245 V in one commonly used chip — and the same divider ratio therefore gives a different output.[366] The upper feedback resistor can also be chosen to bound the maximum output a converter can reach, which is a useful safety feature when something else is modulating the feedback node.[260]

## Switching frequency

Frequency is a direct trade against inductor size: the higher the switching frequency, the smaller the inductance required and therefore the physically smaller the inductor, at the cost of higher switching losses.[110] The MC34063 tops out at 100 kHz.[110] Modern single-chip solutions switch up around a megahertz or more.[116][321] Converters are normally kept above the audible band, above 20 kHz and typically a couple of hundred kHz or into the megahertz region, precisely so they do not whine.[855] Some do sit low enough to be heard: a faint high-pitched noise on power-up is audible evidence that a converter is running, and its absence is a diagnostic clue.[382]

Not all converters run at a fixed frequency. Many vary switching frequency with output power in order to hold efficiency up at very low currents, and a converter may change conduction mode — from pulse width modulation to pulse frequency modulation — as the load changes, which shows up as a visible change in the shape and amplitude of the output ripple with load.[957][110]

## Efficiency

Efficiency is output power divided by input power, and everything not delivered to the load is dissipated as heat.[1031][1401] For ordinary designs the ceiling is around 90%: 85 to 90% is common, a good one reaches the 92 to 93% range, and even the best converters barely push 90% at a spot figure.[1557][772][734] A wide-input-range brick measured at 69.7 W in and 52.4 W out — about 75% — which left 17.3 W to be dissipated by a very small heatsink; at roughly 6 °C per watt of thermal resistance the implications for temperature rise are immediate.[895] Efficiency is optimised for one particular input voltage and one particular output power range, and outside that window whatever efficiency results has to be lived with.[1031] In a product this is not a serious constraint, because the designer knows the load and sizes the inductor and switching transistors for it; on a universal supply module it is largely unavoidable without great complexity and expense.[1031]

Gallium nitride devices change these numbers. Their very fast rise and fall times combined with high breakdown voltage allow extremely efficient converters, and high-power dense brick designs using them can exceed 99% efficient — which is why very small mains adapters can deliver 60 W.[1557][o2NxHu5Bsnk]

The switching element matters. A MOSFET with a couple of ohms of on-resistance, such as the 2N7002, is not suitable for a converter because too much power is dissipated in the switch; parts with far lower RDS(on) in the same SOT-23 package are the appropriate choice.[1736] MOSFETs are generally preferred over BJTs as the switching element, and the advantage grows at high frequencies into the megahertz region.[1736]

## Characterising a converter

Characterising a converter means measuring input voltage, input current, output voltage, and output current simultaneously, which is the standing argument for keeping four multimeters in a lab.[1031][110][67][107] An electronic load substitutes for a bank of power resistors and allows the output to be swept from near zero up to full rating.[102][862] Some loads add rise and fall time measurement, so the transient response of a converter can be captured without an oscilloscope.[862]

The output current axis should be logarithmic, because the interesting behaviour is spread over several decades.[957] Sweeping one converter from 100 µA to about 320 mA produced a curve above 80% from roughly 20 mA up to its rated 100 mA, never exceeding 90% at any point.[957] Below about 100 µA there is no point continuing, because the measurement has reached the quiescent current of the converter itself and that particular part is simply not optimised for low currents.[957] Deliberately driving past the rated output shows how the curve tails off.[957] Alongside efficiency, switching frequency can be recorded by probing the inductor.[957]

When testing an unknown converter, setting the supplying power supply's current limit conservatively low can itself cause trouble: power-on surge current pushes the supply into current limit, the supply voltage collapses, and the converter misbehaves as a result.[895] Characterising one brick properly, over every input voltage and every output current, is a matter of days or weeks, not an afternoon.[895]

Two measurements illustrate what the data sheet does and does not tell. One boost part reached 96% maximum efficiency and operated down to 0.35 V input, but would not start below 0.65 V.[320] An isolated 2 W brick specified for a 4.5 V minimum input continued to deliver full output current down to about 2 V input, though once the output had collapsed it required the load to be removed and a minimum threshold to be re-established before it would restart.[324]

## Ripple, output capacitors, and stability

Ripple on a converter output is at the switching frequency — tens to hundreds of kilohertz, sometimes into the megahertz region — and tens to hundreds of millivolts is typical.[1116] A worked design produced almost 200 mV peak-to-peak, roughly double the calculated target, which an added LC output filter reduced to around 20 mV per division on the screen.[110]

Output capacitors are not free choices. Converters commonly specify a maximum ESR — 0.1 ohm is a typical figure — and sometimes a minimum or a window, because the loop stability depends on it.[742] Peak ripple currents in converters can be very high, and a single capacitor may not cut it; parallel capacitors spread the ripple current and the resulting temperature rise, and provide redundancy against a defective part.[742] The redundancy argument is real: an EV charger failed with one of two paralleled output capacitors measuring 260 µF against a 470 µF rating, the design margin having not been there.[1726] A frequency response analyser with a voltage injection transformer measures control-loop stability directly and lets the required output capacitance be calculated rather than guessed, for in-house converters and off-the-shelf regulators alike.[1103]

## Selecting a part

Selecting a converter IC begins with the switching current the topology demands. As a rough rule of thumb the switch current is approximately Vout divided by Vin, times the output current — 5 V out from a 0.8 V minimum input at 0.5 A implies about 3.1 A, and relaxing the cutoff to 1 V input brings it to about 2.5 A.[139] That number then drives a parametric search through a distributor's switching-converter category, which runs to many thousands of parts, most of which operate at low voltage.[139] The graphs in the data sheet, not the headline numbers, are where the selection is actually made.[139] Parts with the diode built in lower the component count and cost.[139] Working through this for a single simple step-up converter takes far longer than the compressed account suggests, and it is what a design engineer spends a great deal of time doing.[139]

The cheap end of the market is dominated by long-lived jellybean parts: the Motorola MC34063 is a classic and is how cost is driven out of low-end products.[411][110] At the other end, a step-up such as the Maxim MAX629 can on its own cost more from regular sources than an entire finished instrument built around surplus stock.[1248] Integration continues to compress the physical solution — a buck IC can be embedded underneath the inductor so that the entire converter becomes a single pick-and-placeable part.[1525]

## Modules and bricks

Off-the-shelf converter modules take the guesswork out and save a great deal of design time, and the settled engineering judgment is not to roll your own where a module will do.[308][1019][1639] A Texas Instruments PTH08T240 brick appears in a function generator whose designers chose to spend their engineering time elsewhere.[308] A source measure unit uses TDK Lambda 9 V in, 5 V out isolated bricks at around $16 each in tens, which is rational when a few tens of units are built at a time.[607] The same logic explains Linear Technology power modules in a high-end oscilloscope and up to nine converter bricks inside a logic analyser.[1639][1018] Boards needing very large currents on many low-voltage rails almost always use bought-in power modules.[1019] Bricks also solve retrofit problems: a 12 W module taking 5 V in and giving 12 V out, set by a single resistor, provides a rail that a product never had.[1515]

Isolated single-in-line bricks are a commodity item in industry-standard footprints from countless manufacturers.[324] A representative 2 W part offers 1000 V isolation between input and output, with higher ratings available.[324] Many such parts are unregulated, so the output voltage follows the input — perfectly acceptable when a regulator follows the converter.[324] Isolation is the reason they appear in handheld multimeters and oscilloscopes to separate the input section from the processor ground, and in RS-485 interface designs.[810][1313][932]

## Batteries and constant-power loads

A DC-to-DC converter presents a constant-power load to the battery driving it, not a constant current or constant resistance one.[393][140][141][772] This is why battery capacity is more usefully specified in watt-hours, and why the constant-power discharge curve is the right one to consult when estimating product battery life.[393][140][1533] It also has an unpleasant consequence at low battery voltage: as the cell voltage falls the current must rise to maintain power, which snowballs the lower the voltage gets.[774]

The distinction underlies a common specification abuse. A battery bank's advertised capacity is often input-referred — the internal cell voltage multiplied by the cell capacity — and therefore excludes the converter's losses.[1648][1649] One 77 Wh-rated bank delivered 61.8 Wh at its output, implying an 80% efficient converter; what matters to the user is the output-referred figure, and only output-referred figures can be compared between products.[1648][1649]

The same arithmetic disposes of clip-on boost converters sold to extract residual energy from primary cells. A product that cuts out at 1 V has already used about 90% of the cell's energy, and the best efficiency obtainable from an optimised converter is around 90%.[751] Measured properly, a product with a 1.1 V cutoff drawing 100 mA gains at best just over 10% extra capacity before converter losses, which over the whole range can net out at zero or worse.[772] Drawing the real curve on real axes showed the available extra to be about 4% at that current, which is not worth the extra bill-of-materials cost.[772] A converter small enough to fit inside an AA form factor is additionally constrained in output capacitance and peak current capability, which matters for loads that draw current in bursts.[751]

Where a converter is genuinely necessary it is accepted as a cost. A multimeter running from AA cells needs a boost converter to generate a diode-test voltage high enough for white LEDs or a 15 V diode mode, whereas a 9 V battery supplies it directly at the price of poorer capacity; two cells in series would sag below 15 V under load and would still need a boost converter.[97][ILIO5b1BliE] Some meters have no converter at all and run everything from four AA cells directly.[868]

## Noise, layout, and system interaction

A converter is a noise source, and precision analog circuitry has to be protected from it. Switch-mode power supply sections are shielded in full metal cans in portable products specifically to stop high-frequency garbage escaping.[321] Placing an analog input connector in the middle of a group of converters forces extra board layers to route the signal through an internal channel away from the switching noise.[810] Instrumentation with wide dynamic range needs many rails and therefore many converters, with the switching kept away from the measurement path.[1090] A converter running a Peltier element in a thermal chamber raised the noise floor of a resistance measurement noticeably.[835]

Post-regulation does not automatically fix this. A linear regulator's power supply rejection falls with frequency; one part offering 75 dB at 120 Hz manages only 20 dB at 1 MHz, which is exactly where a high-efficiency converter's switching noise sits.[222] Where the converter's noise cannot be rejected, it can sometimes be placed: some converter and inverter chips accept an external clock so their switching can be synchronised to an ADC's sampling clock, letting the sampling system reject those frequencies.[1115] Alternatively the switcher can be used as a tracking pre-regulator that follows the linear regulator's output a fraction of a volt above it, so the linear stage stays efficient and needs only a small heatsink regardless of the output voltage set.[1174][260] Such a tracking loop can be closed in software by measuring the output and adjusting a digital potentiometer in the converter's feedback path, or in analog with no software at all.[260]

Every additional rail costs money, board space, and bill-of-materials complexity — an FPGA requiring a separate core voltage forces extra converters onto the board, and large FPGAs can need three, four, or half a dozen I/O voltages.[193] The converter's output voltage can also be made switchable: a MOSFET altering the feedback divider can raise the FPGA core rail to 1.5 V for JTAG programming and drop it to 1.2 V for operation to minimise power consumption.[193]

## Failure modes and troubleshooting

The first check on a suspected converter is whether it is switching at all. Identical DC voltages on both ends of the inductor mean the switch pins are not pulling to ground and the converter is off — a conclusion reachable with a multimeter, without an oscilloscope.[366] The same signature identifies a converter held off by an upstream MOSFET whose gate drive is missing.[1672] Where a converter is a self-contained module, mains present on its input pins with nothing on its output condemns the module outright.[1726]

Thermal imaging finds converters in distress quickly: on a failed compute module the converter was the hottest part on the board at over 100 °C, too hot to touch, alongside an overloaded Ethernet chip, and the fault in the load is a plausible cause of the converter's destruction.[fpDnVSKfhew][E1IqcGcZKHE][RjfStZa4Si8]

Mechanically, the converter's inductor is the largest and heaviest component on many boards and is the part most likely to shear off its pads in a drop test.[66][868] Assembly errors have their own class: a missing inductor guarantees a dead converter, and a tall converter module can foul a display that the mechanical layer of the CAD drawing said would clear it.[1306][357]
