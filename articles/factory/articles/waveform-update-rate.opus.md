# waveform update rate

Waveform update rate is the number of separate acquisitions a digital oscilloscope captures and presents per second, quoted in waveforms per second.[1478][199] It is not the rate at which the screen is redrawn: a scope claiming a million waveforms per second is not refreshing its LCD a million times a second, it combines many captures into a single displayed image periodically.[1478][3jDRH-6IvZc][674] The figure matters because catching an infrequent event is a statistical process — a scope capturing a couple of hundred thousand waveforms per second stands a far better chance of landing on a rare glitch than one capturing a couple of hundred.[3jDRH-6IvZc][1583] It is a specification that must be compared alongside bandwidth, sample rate and memory depth rather than treated as a footnote.[149]

## Why it decides whether a fault is found

A scope displaying a 10 microsecond capture window, looking for glitches that recur every 15 milliseconds, will only see one roughly every 1,500 acquisitions.[1583] At about 720 to 730 waveform updates per second, that works out to one capture every couple of seconds — a number that can be predicted in advance from the update rate and the window length.[1583] The same arithmetic run against a one-in-50,000 runt pulse shows why 50,000 waveform updates per second catches it about once a second while a scope an order of magnitude slower may take 30 seconds or more.[149][143] Against a one-in-a-million glitch, a 50,000 waveform per second instrument would be expected to need around 20 seconds per capture where a million waveform per second instrument sees it roughly once a second.[199] For a fault that appears once a day, even 50,000 waveforms per second is not enough.[149]

The consequence is that update rate, not analog performance, is often what separates a scope that shows a problem immediately from one that shows nothing at all.[1478] It is also what makes a digital scope superior to an analog one for glitch hunting, since the analog instrument's only equivalent control is turning up the intensity to lengthen persistence.[3jDRH-6IvZc]

## Measuring it

Almost every scope with a trigger output or pass/fail output can be made to report its own update rate: set the auxiliary output to emit a pulse on each trigger, and measure that output's frequency on a counter or a second scope.[199][383][1478] The frequency read directly in hertz is the waveform update rate.[199] The input signal must be at or above the anticipated update rate, so a 1 MHz square or sine wave is the standard stimulus.[383][797] The de facto bench reference condition used for cross-brand comparison is a 10 MHz input at 10 nanoseconds per division with 250 points of memory.[1478] Where the update rate falls below the resolution of this method, an alternative is to count acquisitions over a fixed interval — 43,000 acquisitions in 30 seconds works out to about 1,400 waveforms per second.[1478] Instruments with no pass/fail or trigger output cannot be measured this way and must be compared indirectly against another scope on the same runt-pulse signal.[487] A few scopes, notably the Rohde & Schwarz MXO, report acquisition speed, average blind time and percentage of time captured on screen directly.[1583][1529]

## What the number depends on

The banner specification is a maximum, usually reachable on only one or two optimised time base settings for a given sample rate and memory depth.[1218] A full comparison requires a whole table of update rates, not one figure.[1218]

Memory depth is the dominant variable: the update rate falls as the amount of memory the scope must process rises.[1478] On one instrument 1,000 points of record length gave 250,000 waveforms per second, 100k points gave about 22 kHz, and 1 meg gave 380 to 390 Hz.[617] Another dropped from 4 kHz to 20 waveforms per second going to 20 megapoints, at which setting a one-in-a-million pulse remained uncaptured after 20 minutes.[199] The practical rule is to select the lowest memory depth that still covers the time base of interest when hunting infrequent events, rather than defaulting to maximum memory.[1478] The high-update-rate fast-acquisition modes of some instruments enforce this themselves by dropping the record length to 1,000 samples, which trades away any detail on zoom-in.[970]

Channel count matters on architectures that share acquisition hardware. Turning on a third channel halved the update rate on one four-channel instrument, indicating one chip serving two channels.[199] On others the rate halves with the second channel, and in one case fell by close to an order of magnitude.[797]

Processing load is where architectures diverge most sharply. Scopes that do decoding, masking and measurement in the acquisition ASIC show no drop at all when those functions are enabled.[149][701] Scopes that do not fall apart: enabling digital channels, FFT or serial decode on one instrument collapsed its update rate entirely.[701] A single AC RMS measurement took one high-end scope from 400,000 down to 2.5k waveforms per second while a competitor alongside it stayed at a million.[1638] Turning on a spectrum analyser function dropped another from its fastest rate to 50k.[1529] Trigger holdoff also throttles the rate, to roughly the reciprocal of the holdoff value, which makes it a deliberate means of slowing a scope down.[617]

## Architecture

High update rates come from mapping acquisition data directly to the display in hardware, bypassing the CPU.[148][475] The Keysight MegaZoom 4 ASIC wrote samples and display information straight to the screen to reach a million waveforms per second, a rate that would previously have cost ten to twenty thousand dollars.[148][792] The ASIC's 4 megasample memory is built in, and the technique of filling one 2 meg block while reading out and displaying the other is what produces the speed.[383][1311] The cost is a fixed display layout: because the ASIC writes to a designated screen area, the menu cannot be collapsed to reclaim waveform space.[383] The same silicon was reused for years across the 1000, 2000, 3000 and 4000 series, with capability deliberately disabled on the lower models.[976][383] Its successor raised the figure to 1.3 million.[1638] The Rohde & Schwarz MXO4 ASIC is responsible for 4.5 million waveform updates per second alongside the digital trigger system.[1545] Where display data instead passes through a general-purpose DSP or applications processor, that path becomes the bottleneck and intensity grading typically suffers.[475][674]

## Dead time and the limits of the number

A high waveform-per-second figure is not automatically better; what matters is dead time.[797] Segmented acquisition modes inflate the headline number by capturing bursts to memory without updating the screen, and one instrument reaching 400,000 to 446,000 waveforms per second this way still failed to find a one-in-a-million pulse because of the dead time between bursts — switching the mode off, with a far lower nominal rate, found it quickly.[797] Update rates measured on a burst-mode scope are also visibly bursty rather than continuous.[1478] A scope with zero dead time acquires continuously and decimates the result for display.[1478]

Claims frequently fail to survive measurement. One instrument specified at 60,000 waveforms per second delivered 10,000 in the normal vector mode everyone uses, reaching 71 kHz only in dot mode at a single time base setting.[797] A 4.5 million per second claim measured 2.3 million in the automatic memory depth mode most users leave selected, reaching the claim only under manual tweaking — described as marketing wankery, while the same instrument still ran two to three times faster than the reference against which it was compared.[1529] A 1.5 million figure applies solely to a segmented ultra acquire mode, with normal operation limited to around 50,000.[1501] Conversely, one instrument was measured at 1.038 MHz against a claimed 1 million.[610]

Update rate also does not predict how responsive a scope feels. An instrument with a poor waveform update rate can still have a quick, responsive user interface, and the two should not be conflated.[1478][3jDRH-6IvZc] A perceived difference in display speed between two models can turn out to be screen updating rather than waveform updating, which are different things.[YkgCcYG29nk] There is also a case where the fast scope misleads: a very high update rate paints so many acquisitions into the trace that the waveform appears thick and noisy, and slowing the trigger frequency to a few hundred hertz reveals a clean line with nothing about the signal having changed.[610]

## Anomalies

Tektronix digital scopes drop to an extremely low update rate — on the order of 10 to 20 Hz — when there is no trigger, whether the trigger level is moved off the signal or the input is removed entirely, the result of a timeout of roughly 50 to 60 milliseconds in the acquisition engine.[617] The behaviour is long-standing and specific to that manufacturer; comparable instruments maintain essentially the same update rate whether or not they are triggering.[617] One four-series architecture is specified at 100 waveform updates per second in normal mode, with the greater-than-500,000 figure in the manual applying only to a super acquisition mode that must be switched on deliberately and which leaves very little sample memory.[1218]

## Representative figures

Instruments built around direct-to-screen ASICs cluster at the top: 1 million waveforms per second for the MegaZoom 4 generation, 1.3 million for its successor, and 4.5 million claimed for the MXO4.[148][1638][1529] The mid-market sits between roughly 30,000 and 600,000 — 30,000 for entry-level Rigol models, 50,000 for the GDS-1000B and the Agilent 2000 X, 80,000 for the GDS-2000A, 100,000 to 140,000 across the Siglent SDS1104X-E, SDS2000X and SDS2000X Plus, 110,000 for the SDS5000X, 120,000 for the SDS1000X HD, 75,000 for the Owon XDS3202A, and 600,000 for the Rigol MSO7000.[674][824][143][475][1042][864][1309][1220][1612][ByUiOk00K0U][CMoBGGqojqs] Slow instruments include a 500 MHz four-channel scope managing only 4,000 to 6,000 despite substantial hardware, a 300 MHz instrument at 10,000, and one that fell to 35 Hz and dropped further still when a menu was opened.[792][790][842][480] The Tektronix 2 Series reached a best of 18.8k waveforms per second at 10 nanoseconds per division with 250 points, falling to 7.5k at 100 ns/div and 760 Hz at 1 microsecond per division, against 280,000 for the model above it.[1478] An older instrument marketed on 25 million vectors per second — a figure never translated into real update rate or dead time — measured about 520 Hz.[591]
