# oscilloscope bandwidth

Oscilloscope bandwidth is the frequency at which an instrument's analog front end has fallen 3 dB below its low-frequency response, and it is the single most important specification of a scope.[13] The figure printed on the front panel is not a limit beyond which nothing is seen and below which the response is flat; it is the point at which a sine wave of constant amplitude at the input reads 0.707 of its true value on screen.[306][1260] Everything else about an acquisition system — sample rate, memory depth, vertical sensitivity, and the probe on the end of the cable — is either subordinate to the analog bandwidth or capable of quietly destroying it.

## Definition and measurement

Because the specification is a minus-3 dB point, the practical test is to feed in a constant-amplitude sine wave, sweep the frequency upward, and find where the displayed amplitude reaches 0.707 of the low-frequency reading.[306][1260] A 4 V peak-to-peak signal should therefore read about 2.83 V at the rated bandwidth, and a 5 V peak-to-peak signal about 3.53 V.[1260] The measurement is only meaningful when the signal path is properly terminated: a 50 ohm feedthrough at the input, a correctly compensated ×10 probe, or a direct 50 ohm load.[1260][1317][978]

Where no sufficiently fast generator is available, bandwidth can be inferred from step response instead. Feeding a 1 kHz square wave with a fast edge and measuring the displayed rise time gives the bandwidth as roughly 0.35 divided by the rise time, a method that works on analog instruments where no frequency source of the right range exists.[196] The relationship inverts freely, so a measured rise time also yields a bandwidth figure: a 520 ps edge corresponds to about 769 MHz.[306] Different constants apply depending on the front-end response — 0.4 and 0.45 are both in use — and the result is a lower bound rather than an exact value, because the cabling and the finite rise time of the source both contribute.[306][1133]

A rise-time check is also the standard confirmation that a bandwidth change has actually taken effect in hardware rather than only in a menu. On the Rigol DS1000E series, a 3.5 ns displayed rise time and a 2 ns/div minimum time base indicate the 100 MHz mode, while the 50 MHz mode returns a slower edge and a 5 ns/div floor.[77]

## Bandwidth against sample rate

Analog bandwidth alone does not make a signal usable. For single-shot work the governing rule of thumb is that the real-time sample rate should be about ten times the analog bandwidth, so a 300 MHz instrument running at 500 MS/s has a usable single-shot bandwidth closer to 50 MHz.[107] That ratio became the industry norm with the Tektronix TDS200 series, which paired 100 MHz of bandwidth with 1 GS/s of real-time sampling and established the 10-to-1 expectation for the digital scopes that followed; earlier instruments sampled at around 20 MS/s and relied on equivalent-time sampling to fake the rest.[690][PcxEO3fA_Ls] Where sine(x)/x interpolation is applied, a lower multiplier of roughly four times is treated as the minimum acceptable.[800]

The mismatch appears most often in low-cost four-channel instruments, where a single ADC is shared and the aggregate sample rate divides down as channels are enabled. A 1 GS/s converter shared four ways yields 250 MS/s per channel — technically acceptable for a 100 MHz model with interpolation, and inadequate for the 200 MHz version of the same chassis.[1038][824] At 500 MS/s a 200 MHz front end is effectively reduced to around 125 MHz of usable bandwidth.[800][1042] The same reasoning applies at the top of the market: a 500 MHz four-channel instrument dropping to 1 GS/s per channel meets Nyquist and still fails to perform as a genuine 500 MHz scope.[792] Where the advertised sample rate cannot support the advertised bandwidth at all — 200 MS/s behind a claimed 110 MHz front end — the specification does not hold together.[1375]

Not every distorted waveform is a bandwidth problem. A square wave reconstructed from too few memory points can be badly wrong at very low frequencies, and reducing the bandwidth limit to 20 MHz changes nothing, because the fault lies in memory depth and interpolation rather than in the front end.[1213]

## Bandwidth and noise

The higher the bandwidth, the greater the inherent noise of the input amplifier and the rest of the front-end circuitry.[601][369] This is the mechanism behind the long-standing impression that analog scopes are quieter than digital ones: an analog instrument of modest bandwidth and short phosphor persistence simply does not display the high-frequency content that a wideband digital scope with deep memory captures and paints on screen.[601]

The effect is directly observable. Engaging a 20 MHz limit on a 100 MHz instrument visibly drops the trace thickness, and stepping a 1 GHz scope down through 250 MHz to 20 MHz reduces it further at each step.[601] On a 200 MHz scope the noise floor at the most sensitive range falls to under one division once the 20 MHz limit is applied.[369] Comparative noise figures track bandwidth closely: at 1 mV/div, roughly 94 microvolts RMS from an instrument restricted to 200 MHz against 96 microvolts from a 350 MHz instrument running at full bandwidth, and around 323 microvolts from a wider front end under otherwise identical settings.[1223]

Critically, the noise penalty is baked into the hardware and cannot be undone in software. A 350 MHz front end software-limited to 200 MHz is still measurably noisier than a native 200 MHz front end at the same setting, because a bandwidth limit filters the noise rather than preventing it from being generated.[1612][nO09bc5ozng] The same logic underlies the near-universal presence of a 20 MHz bandwidth limit on modern scopes, and the convention that power-supply output ripple and noise is specified over a 20 MHz bandwidth.[324][1266][1309] Measuring supply noise at the full 200 or 400 MHz bandwidth of the instrument is not the industry standard and produces figures that cannot be compared against a datasheet.[1266] Reduced bandwidth also buys resolution in high-definition acquisition modes, where dropping from 500 MHz to 200, 100 and finally 10 MHz raises the effective resolution from 14 bits to roughly 18.[1529]

## How bandwidth is set in hardware

In instruments up to about 500 MHz, both the switchable gain ranges and the bandwidth limiting are typically handled inside a programmable gain amplifier — commonly the TI/National LMH family — which then drives a differential output to the ADC.[1761] Register writes over an SPI bus to that amplifier select the bandwidth, and the 20 MHz limit is implemented there with no other hardware involved.[879] On one instrument family the base unit ships at 50 MHz and is raised as far as 300 MHz entirely by SPI commands to the front-end amplifier.[wWymcaiaQXM] Probing that bus reveals settings well above the model's rating — a 750 MHz code on a 300 MHz unit — with the actual ceiling imposed elsewhere in the front end.[879]

Above that range the front end becomes a custom part. A single-chip ASIC covers 70 MHz to 500 MHz in one Tektronix design, and a comparable custom ASIC provides a 1 GHz front end at the sub-$1000 price point, drawing enough power to require heat-sink contact.[1477][1717] Not every manufacturer shares hardware across a model range: some series use genuinely different analog front ends at 200/350, 500, 1000 and 1500 MHz, which requires a board swap at the factory to change bandwidth.[384] Analog bandwidth is generally expensive to obtain, and pricing reflects it — a four-channel 200 MHz instrument at around $4,000 against $7,500 at 350 MHz and $10,000 at 500 MHz within one series.[148][149]

## Software-limited models and bandwidth hacking

A large proportion of the market ships the full-bandwidth front end in every model of a series and restricts it by licence key or configuration setting.[879] One compact instrument carries no bandwidth marking on the front panel at all, precisely because the figure is a software property; the base 100 MHz unit contains the same 300 MHz vertical amplifiers as the top model.[879] The pattern repeats across manufacturers: a 70 MHz model containing a 300 MHz front end, a 50 MHz model containing the full 100 MHz hardware, a 70 MHz model containing a 300 MHz front end with no upgrade offered for sale, and a series spanning 70 to 500 MHz in five field-upgradeable steps.[864][976][474][PcxEO3fA_Ls] Where upgrades are sold, the path may be constrained by the model purchased, or may allow a jump from 100 MHz directly to 1 GHz.[701] Some series are deliberately not upgradeable, with separate 70 and 100 MHz models and no licence route between them.[522] More recent designs push the software span further, expanding a base 350 MHz model to 800 MHz or 1 GHz.[1718][1717]

Because the hardware is present, the restriction is frequently defeatable. Changing the model number over the remote interface converts a 50 MHz instrument to its 100 MHz sibling, and the change survives a firmware upgrade provided the modification is performed on a sufficiently old firmware version first.[77] On another instrument, setting two product-configuration resistors to zero changed the reported bandwidth; measurement then showed the front end was not 3 dB down even at 200 MHz, implying an actual analog bandwidth beyond 200 MHz on a unit sold at 70 MHz.[978] Where the setting lives in an SPI register, a small microcontroller intercepting the bus can rewrite it in transit.[879]

A software-set bandwidth is also useful in its own right. Being able to set a 500 MHz instrument back to 200 MHz allows side-by-side comparison against a 200 MHz unit without the extra noise of the wider setting.[nO09bc5ozng][1612]

## Where the rated bandwidth is not available

Full bandwidth is commonly withheld at the most sensitive vertical ranges. One manufacturer's datasheet carries a double-asterisk footnote stating that below 1 mV/div the bandwidth is limited to 200 MHz — so the 500 microvolts per division range advertised alongside the bandwidth figures cannot deliver it — and on the 500 MHz and 1 GHz models the restriction applies below 2.45 mV/div, meaning four or five mV/div is required before the purchased bandwidth appears.[1218][1220] The same trap appears elsewhere: a 300 MHz instrument that cannot use its 1 mV/div range at full bandwidth, a 300 MHz instrument that automatically engages its bandwidth limit at 2 mV/div, a 12-bit instrument that forces a 20 MHz limit on its 500 microvolt range, and a 1 GHz instrument on which a 1 GHz signal simply is not present at low volts-per-division settings.[474][480][1501][701] The consequence is that a noise figure quoted at 1 mV/div may have been taken at 200 MHz on an instrument sold as 1 GHz — "a bit of a trap for young players".[1223] Instruments that provide a true, non-zoomed 1 mV/div range at full bandwidth are the exception rather than the rule.[207] Analog instruments have an equivalent behaviour: engaging ×10 vertical magnification on a 50 MHz scope drops the bandwidth to roughly 5 MHz, an order of magnitude.[196]

The probe is the other common ceiling. A switchable ×1/×10 passive probe in its ×1 position has a bandwidth of about six and a half megahertz, so the ×1 setting reduces a 100 or 200 MHz instrument to that figure regardless of the front end behind it.[1445][1260] Passive probe technology runs out at around 500 MHz; above that, fixed 10:1 and then active probes are required, and the probing solution can cost more than the scope.[ln_XJDPKJlc] A 1 GHz passive probe is essentially unobtainable, which caps what a 1 GHz instrument can usefully be pointed at.[1735] Supplying 500 MHz passive probes across an entire model range avoids the probe becoming the limiting element and preserves a later bandwidth upgrade.[701] When a signal on screen looks smoothly sinusoidal because sine(x)/x interpolation is filling in between too few samples, the signal is beyond the scope's bandwidth regardless of how good the probe is.[Y7t6BIhBZhc]

## Claimed against measured bandwidth

Low-cost portable instruments are where claimed and measured bandwidth diverge most sharply. A tablet scope advertised at 100 MHz measured about 22 MHz at the 3 dB point with a direct 50 ohm input, and its own manual conceded 5 MHz on the ×1 profile; the claimed 1 GS/s sample rate was likewise not delivered, so the 100 MHz figure could not have been met in any case.[1317] Restricting such an instrument to 20 or 30 MHz makes it usable.[1317] A $123 pocket instrument technically reached its claimed 180 MHz, but behind only 500 MS/s falling to 250 MS/s with two channels — a well-designed 50 MHz front end would have suited the price class better.[1582] An $80 instrument claiming 50 MHz visibly falls apart by 40 MHz.[1597] Claims are not always inflated: one $70 single-channel instrument measured 107 megahertz with proper 50 ohm termination and a compensated ×10 probe, after an initially poor result was traced to the physical ×1 attenuator in the supplied probe.[1260]

## Choosing bandwidth

For general-purpose bench work the practical floor has been around 20 MHz for a secondhand dual-channel analog instrument, with 50 MHz preferable and 100 MHz comfortable.[1022][86] For modern digital work a couple of hundred megahertz covers fast edges, serial decoding and mixed-signal analysis, and 200 to 350 MHz is regarded as sufficient for almost all general-purpose requirements.[ln_XJDPKJlc] Above roughly 500 MHz the probing problem dominates, and buying additional bandwidth that will never be usefully probed is treated as wasted money for anything short of a specific niche requirement.[ln_XJDPKJlc] Bandwidth requirements are also application-dependent in the other direction: power-conversion work at hundreds of kilohertz to a few megahertz was well served by 7.5 MHz instruments, and field service work is well served by 5 to 10 MHz.[1724][D2PANd9Hu3U][1723]

## Historical and high-end range

The bandwidth available at a given price has moved by orders of magnitude. A 1965 dual-channel instrument offered 0 to 10 MHz, a 1975 portable combining scope and multimeter offered 1 MHz, and 10 MHz was a respectable bench figure in that era.[416][628][813] The 300 MHz analog instruments of the mid-1980s were raised to 400 MHz in their revised versions.[695][1203] At the far end, real-time instruments reach 2 GHz at 3.2 GS/s, 13 GHz at 40 GS/s, and 32 GHz halving to 16 GHz when all four channels are enabled at 80 GS/s.[1709][311][984] Above a few gigahertz the input impedance becomes 50 ohms as a matter of necessity.[311]
