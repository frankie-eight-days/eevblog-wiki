# oscilloscope timebase

The timebase of an oscilloscope is the horizontal axis control: it sets how much time each screen division represents, and so decides what fraction of a signal's behaviour is visible at once.[1311][1751] Setting it is one of the two fundamental acts of using a scope, alongside setting the vertical scale, with the trigger configuration following from both.[1311][1723] Almost every other property of a digital scope — capture window, achievable measurement resolution, waveform update rate, even whether a fault is visible at all — is downstream of where the timebase is set.[1583][1478][1183]

## Divisions, sequences and steps

Timebase settings are conventionally quantised into a 1-2-5-10 sequence, so the coarse horizontal control jumps in large steps rather than moving continuously.[879][AVuVCrYMBwk] A fine or vernier horizontal control exists to fill in between those steps, allowing the sweep speed to be trimmed to line features up against the graticule instead of accepting the nearest coarse setting.[879][487]

Departures from the standard sequence occur. The Rohde & Schwarz RTB2004 follows 1-2-5-10 at 1 ns per division and again from 500 ns per division upward to the region of 500 seconds per division, but between them substitutes 20, 40, 80 and 200 nanoseconds per division, with no 100 nanoseconds per division setting available at all; the cause is presumed to lie in the acquisition architecture and sampling memory depth.[AVuVCrYMBwk] Some low-cost instruments abandon the convention entirely and step in a 1-2-4-8 sequence.[1127] The Zoyi ZT-703S corrected an over-range step of 1.25 microseconds per division present on the earlier 702, moving to conventional steps, and also fixed a timebase accuracy defect the earlier model showed at 500 nanoseconds per division.[1597]

Timebase accuracy is straightforward to verify against a known frequency: ten divisions at 1 microsecond per division against a 1 MHz reference, or a 1 Hz signal spanning the screen at 200 milliseconds per division, confirms the sweep is calibrated.[502][540]

## Range

Fast-end limits vary enormously by class of instrument. Pocket and budget scopes reach 5 or 1 nanoseconds per division, though the displayed waveform at those settings may be worthless.[359][1582] Mid-range bench instruments reach a few nanoseconds per division in real time,[1478] and high-bandwidth instruments go to 500 picoseconds per division, at which point a 500 MHz sine occupies roughly four divisions.[fM4bbKVw2og] Equivalent-time sampling extends the effective sample rate beyond the converter's real-time capability at fast settings, at the cost of no longer acquiring in real time.[474]

The slow end runs to tens or hundreds of seconds per division — 50 seconds per division on some instruments, with others slower still, and 2.5 seconds per division on handheld units.[1678][1723][D2PANd9Hu3U]

## Reading measurements from the timebase

With the timebase known, the graticule becomes a ruler. Counting divisions against a horizontal setting of 20 microseconds per division yields a time interval directly — around 95 microseconds in one phase measurement, converting to roughly 34 degrees against a cursor-derived 33 degrees, so a graticule reading lands within about a percent of a cursor measurement.[1751] Where a measurement is limited by screen resolution, the remedy is a faster timebase: a 0.2 microsecond resolution improves once the two features of interest are expanded across more of the screen, provided both remain on screen.[1751]

The timebase also fixes the width of the capture window: 1 microsecond per division across ten divisions gives a 10 microsecond window, which against events recurring every 15 milliseconds makes the probability of catching one on any given sweep very low.[1583] The same arithmetic drives search strategy when hunting a narrow event. A 100 nanosecond pulse is invisible at 5 milliseconds per division and resolves properly at 20 nanoseconds per division.[1183] Conversely, measuring a roughly 10 millisecond idle period at 10 milliseconds per division supplies the number needed to set up a pulse-width trigger.[297]

## Interaction with memory depth and update rate

On a digital scope the timebase, the record length and the waveform update rate are coupled. The slower the timebase, the longer the instrument must acquire to fill its memory, and the lower the update rate falls: one instrument sustained a 52 kHz update rate at 2 nanoseconds per division, 22.7 kHz at 2 microseconds per division and 1.8 kHz at 50 microseconds per division.[1478] At 50 milliseconds per division the dead time between acquisitions reached roughly 150 milliseconds.[1478] Headline figures of a million waveform updates per second are therefore meaningless at a slow timebase, where filling memory dominates.[1478] Manufacturers' update-rate claims generally hold only in a particular window of timebase settings.[824] Anomalous behaviour exists too: one Tektronix instrument produced the same trigger output regardless of time per division, right up to 10 nanoseconds per division, suggesting the limit lay in the display update rather than in acquisition.[617]

Deep memory allows a capture taken at one timebase to be zoomed into afterwards, which is why maximum memory depth is worth selecting before a single-shot capture of an event that may need later examination.[1311]

## Dual timebase and delayed sweep

A dual timebase, also described as a delayed sweep or B timebase, runs a second, faster sweep within a window of the main sweep, so a region of interest can be expanded without losing the overall context.[1022][792] On analog instruments the horizontal mode switch selects A, B or mixed operation, with a separate B trigger; the resulting split traces on a powered-up but untested instrument are normal and not a fault.[1022] Where a full dual timebase was unavailable, sweep magnification of ×5, ×10 or ×50 provided a partial substitute that behaves similarly without the full capability.[196] A four-channel analog instrument with expanded dual timebase could put eight traces on screen from four inputs.[1194]

Most modern digital scopes implement only a zoom window sharing the main timebase, which is not a true dual timebase; the Rigol DS1054Z is an example, and genuinely independent dual timebases remain rare on the market.[704][1235] Where they exist, each channel gets its own timebase and its own independent trigger, which is more capable than the older alternate-trigger arrangement because a single-shot capture returns both channels simultaneously.[1235] The implementation cost is why manufacturers avoid it.[1235] Two cautions apply: functions may be disabled in the mode — one instrument refuses single-shot capture while dual timebase is active — and measurements read visually off screen can be misread if the two channels are on different timebases.[1235] Entry-level Tektronix TBS models offer dual timebase.[1235] Despite the capability of high-end analog instruments with dual timebases and high bandwidth, single-shot capture on even a low-end digital scope is the more useful facility in practice.[1536]

## Slow-timebase pitfalls

Several traps appear only at slow sweep speeds. Acquisition modes such as high-resolution averaging can completely conceal an oscillation at 20 milliseconds per division, with no on-screen indication that the display is being filtered; speeding the timebase up reveals the signal.[710] Aliasing likewise announces itself through an implausible timebase setting rather than through anything obviously wrong with the trace.[1127] Pre-trigger or pre-arm time becomes significant at slow settings, because the instrument must fill its pre-trigger buffer before it will accept a trigger at all; some instruments provide a front-panel ready indicator for exactly this reason.[1678] Roll mode, where the trace scrolls continuously rather than sweeping, is the appropriate choice for watching slow events unfold in real time.[771] A single notch of timebase change can also alter whether an event triggers reliably.[771]

## Timebase-dependent faults and bugs

Because so much of a digital scope's signal path is reconfigured by the horizontal setting, a large class of defects is timebase-dependent and identified by sweeping the setting. Display artifacts on one instrument appeared only at settings faster than 50 nanoseconds per division — at 20, 10 and 5 nanoseconds per division — and were traced to the display engine rather than to sampling, since dot mode showed a clean trace.[824] A faulty Tektronix TDS3054 misbehaved below a certain sweep speed and was stable above it.[564] The Rigol MSO5000 showed captured data present at one timebase setting and absent at the next, and failed to expand about the centred point.[UDGsZcAWgL8] The Rigol DHO800 lost a single-shot capture taken at 500 milliseconds per division with 25 megapoints of memory as soon as the waveform was moved horizontally, with or without the vernier engaged, suggesting a memory allocation problem.[EVRdTY4LNhg] An RTB2004 acquisition defect appeared at 100 milliseconds per division and was not expected at faster settings.[Ott9syzNLuE] The Siglent SDS5000X, driven down through the timebase by pinch-zoom, reached 200 picoseconds per division and then folded back around to the slowest setting of 1,000 seconds per division — a fault the horizontal knob does not exhibit, since it correctly stops at 200 picoseconds.[1220]

A dual timebase zoom window that fails to redraw its waveform until the display is otherwise updated is a firmware defect rather than intended behaviour.[369]

## Controls and usability

The horizontal control is the most-used control on the instrument after the vertical, and burying it costs real time at the bench.[1723] Pushable horizontal knobs that re-centre the trace or swap the active window between main and zoom displays are a worthwhile convenience.[704][487] Touchscreen-only instruments are frequently worse: pinch-zoom on the timebase may be absent entirely, leaving only horizontal panning,[1317] or the timebase adjustment may be hidden behind an undocumented tap or press-and-hold gesture.[1723][D2PANd9Hu3U]

Turning the timebase up while moving the horizontal position is also a standard technique for exposing detail that a slower sweep smears out, such as common-mode switching noise riding on a supply.[442]
