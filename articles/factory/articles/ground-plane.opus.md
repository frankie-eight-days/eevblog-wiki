# ground plane

A ground plane is a large uninterrupted area of copper on a printed circuit board dedicated to the ground net, either flood-filled across an outer layer or given an entire internal layer of a multi-layer stackup.[244][193][1216] Its purpose is to give every return current a short, low-impedance, low-inductance path back to its source, and in doing so it governs radiated emissions, signal integrity, DC voltage drop and the thermal behaviour of the board during assembly.[1176][500][1216] As a first approximation the correct action on almost any layout is to flood fill the ground: a big low-impedance, low-inductance plane over the whole thing generally does the business, and a star point should only be used where there is a specific reason for it.[500]

## Return current and loop area

Every current forms a loop, and the ground plane is where the return half of that loop lives.[812] Current leaving a supply through a MOSFET and into ground must come back through the plane to close the loop, which is why measured current direction reverses between charge and discharge in a battery circuit.[812] The plane can be modelled as a grid of resistors, and the path the return current takes through that grid depends on frequency.[1216] At low frequencies bulk decoupling current spreads out and its position barely matters.[1085] As frequency rises, the return current stops spreading across the copper and concentrates directly beneath the trace it is returning for, even though the plane is one big continuous sheet of copper — the rest of the copper ceases to matter.[1439][1216]

That concentration is what makes the plane an EMC tool: it minimises the area enclosed by the outgoing trace and its return, and loop area sets the radiated field for a given switching current.[1176] Probing directly on the leg of a bypass capacitor shows the current flowing through that lead, having crossed the ground plane to get there, so moving the capacitor closer to the load shrinks the loop.[1085] The corollary is that anything obstructing the return path is destructive. A gap in the flood fill — not a routed slot, merely an unfilled region — forces the return current the long way around, wrecking the timing budget and the signal integrity of a memory interface at once.[1247] The rule follows directly: do not split ground planes under high-speed signals.[1247]

## Two layers versus four

The effect has been measured directly on an identical layout produced as both a two-layer and a four-layer board, with no trace changed, the same clock, and the same bypass capacitors — the only difference being an internal ground plane and a 5 V plane.[1176] The two-layer version has no internal plane at all, and its returns run over flood fills on top and bottom joined by via stitching, with paths of high inductance wandering across the board.[1176] On the four-layer version the flood fills are unnecessary because the whole board is one plane, so a ground pin connects to the bypass capacitor over a path of the same length but far lower inductance, and the ground and power planes add shielding against magnetic and electric fields.[1176] The measured reduction in near-field radiated emissions was 15 dB — enough to be the difference between passing and failing compliance, and between shipping and respinning the product at a cost of five or ten thousand dollars.[1176]

Solid ground and power planes across a board are without competition; via stitching on a two-layer board improves matters but does not equal them.[1176] Where two layers are mandated, the practices that help are keeping ground and power on top of each other wherever possible, not splitting the ground, and stitching and flood filling aggressively; a well-laid-out two-layer board can radiate less than a poorly laid out four-layer one.[1176] A board with no plane at all, such as the single-sided phenolic construction found in cheap USB hubs, has neither a return path nor controlled impedance for high-speed signalling.[12] Early home computers built on double-sided board without a plane failed FCC certification outright, with long parallel buses, memory interfaces and unshielded ribbon cables to the expansion box acting as antennas.[645][955]

## The plane is not an ideal conductor

A large plane has non-zero resistance, non-zero spread inductance and is not the same node everywhere.[1216][1117] Carrying 8 A across a board, a plane can easily drop 40 mV, and that figure is for the power side alone, before the ground connection is added; where a design has only 120 mV of margin, this is not a rounding error.[1216] Measuring across a board from one bypass capacitor to another gives readings in the tens of milliohms — 67 mΩ in one case — comprising the plane itself plus the thermal relief spokes and joints at each pad, which is enough to defeat low-resistance short-hunting until a meter with milliohm resolution is used.[1061][717ampROkvc] The same shared impedance produces ground bounce and power bounce when a switching section shares copper with sensitive digital parts, an effect that a big plane reduces but does not eliminate.[1365] On an oscilloscope, energising a channel relay pulls extra current through the plane and shifts the DC offset of a neighbouring channel by a couple of hundred microvolts.[704] Every conductor, including a plane, has inductance, and lowering the inductance from pad to via to plane makes a measurable difference to bypassing.[1117][1081]

## Splitting

Splitting a ground plane is the most consequential decision in plane design and the one most often taken wrongly. For low-power, non-fast digital work, one plane for the entire board is normal and analog and digital may share it; with 8-bit converters one big plane is fine.[107] Separation becomes necessary when the performance demands it — 16-bit dynamic range with high-speed digital on one side of the board and analog on the other.[107] A split is really a star ground: separate planes for separate domains, joined at a single point, with the intervening space left deliberately bare so that current flowing to an LCD connector cannot disturb an FPGA and the FPGA's switching cannot disturb the LCD supply.[1216] Grounds may overlap freely on different layers.[1216] Against this, splitting power planes is dangerous for EMC, and a plane must remain continuous over the whole of the power track loop regardless of any split — break it and EMC problems follow.[1216][104] Splitting grounds is generally more of a problem than splitting power.[1323]

Deliberate splits appear throughout galvanically isolated equipment: totally separate planes between the channels of a power analyser, a visible break under a digital isolator, split copper across an isolated USB-to-RS232 interface, a gap between current and voltage measurement sections of a 600 V power meter, and a plane that stops at the mains-isolated boundary of an instrument.[589][875][680][1693][164] Splits are also made for signal integrity by removing plane copper: the bottom plane taken out beneath two scope input channels, plane removed under a metal shield covering low-noise op amps, and copper cleared from beneath antennas and sensor pads.[360][529][1190][171] Conversely, on a fast comparator input the plane was carried straight through rather than split.[452]

## Stackup and layer assignment

The conventional four-layer order is signal, ground, power, signal.[1323] Putting signal, power, then ground is workable but inferior, because having ground directly beneath the signal layer is what sets the loop area and bypassing behaviour.[1323] Inner layers should be reserved for solid power and ground planes rather than routed with traces — both for signal integrity and EMC, and because traces buried on inner layers cannot be inspected, cut or modified.[1323] The dielectric spacing matters: for lowest emissions the core should be thick and the prepregs between the planes and the outer signal layers as thin as possible, bringing each signal layer close to its reference.[1193] Ground and VCC are effectively the same node for this purpose, which the bypass capacitors ensure.[1193] Copper weight can be specified per layer, with 2 oz on power and ground layers carrying real current and 0.25 oz sufficient for shield layers that carry none.[1216]

A plane on an outer layer over a trace on top is a microstrip; a signal sandwiched between two planes is a stripline.[1323][1530] Boards routed as a single-sided surface-mount layout preserve the most plane area, and shooting for that is the goal on every SMD layout.[1323] Blind and buried vias matter here because every through via punches a hole in every plane it passes: breaking out a BGA without them chops the planes severely, whereas blind and buried vias leave a solid plane and buy routing density as well.[1259] Boards for high-density work go to six, eight or ten layers precisely to put a ground layer between everything.[598][444][VnrtneFP_6A]

## Distributed capacitance

An internal ground plane and an adjacent power plane form a capacitor — two sheets of copper separated by a dielectric, in this case the epoxy-glass of the board.[1117][1081] Measured against discrete parts, the plane pair on a four-layer board showed roughly 2 nF with a broad, flat impedance response, in contrast to the sharp resonant peak of a 100 nF film capacitor.[1117] The broadness is the point: a plane does not present the peaky response that can cause LC resonance trouble.[1117] Position on the board makes only a small difference, since the plane is essentially one big capacitor with edge effects.[1117] Bulk distributed plane capacitance is a real mechanism, sufficient to keep a board running with individual bypass capacitors lifted, and there is published work and practical demonstration showing it can outperform discrete bypassing.[1081] It is not, however, a substitute for bypass capacitors in general, and where a board has only split planes with scattered via paths rather than a solid one, the mechanism is largely absent.[1081] Even on a two-layer board where the top is a positive rail and the bottom is all ground, the two layers act as one big bypass capacitor.[341] Plane capacitance can also work against a design: ferrite beads fitted between two grounds in a multimeter resonated with the plane capacitance and had to be shorted out in a later revision.[987]

## Flood filling in practice

Ground is normally the last net left unrouted, and flood filling it is the easiest way to finish a layout.[974] Filling need not cover the whole board — a high-voltage Nixie section is a reason to keep the pour to one half.[974] Saving a few millimetres of trace length on the plane layer to avoid cutting up the copper matters little on a low-speed board but becomes significant on a critical one.[244] Star points are created by placing keepouts that stop the polygon joining, then bringing the two pours together at the chosen point.[244]

Several mechanical rules recur. Planes should be pulled back from the board edge rather than taken to it or beyond, and the tool's automatic trimming should not be relied on.[Ep4r-wD7PPs] Pour clearance need not inherit the trace design rules — an 8 thou routing clearance can be relaxed to 10 or 15 thou for the pour, and a plane crowding every pad is a sign the designer never changed the rule.[759] Clearance around a meter's voltage input jack was judged too tight where the fill had been brought close in.[1096] A pour that breaks into isolated islands leaves dead copper.[155] Filled zones must sometimes be poured manually rather than existing as true planes, depending on the tool.[Ep4r-wD7PPs] Via stitching joins pours on opposite layers, shortens returns instead of creating large loops, and lowers plane inductance; one via is a rough rule of thumb for around half an amp.[1034][856][643] Edge plating, where used, is almost always connected to ground.[1193] On flex circuits solid planes should be avoided and crosshatched fill used instead.[1262]

Errors in plane definition are expensive and easy to make. Assigning two zones the same net incorrectly shorts the planes together, and on a board carrying thousands of dollars of parts an unconnected or shorted plane found after assembly is a costly mistake.[1193] A manufacturer that expanded the ground pour beyond the submitted Gerbers ate away an 8 thou trace entirely, breaking the board; the clearance submitted already met the 6 thou specification, and manufacturers should not modify Gerbers without written permission.[155][1193]

## Thermal behaviour

Copper plane is thermal mass, and this dominates both assembly and rework. A pad tied to a large plane sucks heat away from the joint; if the other pad of the same component goes to a thin trace, the two ends reflow at different times and the part tombstones.[127][415] The same differential can thermally stress and crack a ceramic capacitor.[1037] Thermal reliefs — a pad connected to the pour through four spurs rather than solidly — exist to let the pad heat faster than the surrounding copper.[183] Whole-board effects are just as real: an 8-layer, 3.2 mm board full of planes retains so much heat that a reflow profile written for 1.6 mm material caused widespread tombstoning and unsoldered joints, and heavily planed BGA boards demand a soak profile and cool slowly.[VnrtneFP_6A][104][860]

At the bench, the plane sets the demand on a soldering iron. Irons with the tip loosely coupled to the element lag when heat drains into a plane, and because the tip is outside the control loop the sensor reaches setpoint before the tip does.[1064][1065] A high-thermal-capacity iron and a large chisel tip are what make plane soldering practical; an 8 W USB iron cannot do it, and a nano iron with almost no tip mass is not intended to.[183][1113][QbeTv41l2EQ] Temperature is raised to 370–400 °C for plane work such as soldering a wire to a plane or an RF can, while delicate parts are done as cool and as briefly as possible.[1106] Hot-air rework on motherboards with large planes and large components can exceed a small station's air flow and heat capacity, and a preheater raising the whole board's temperature reduces the thermal shock when the hot air arrives.[1058][1154] Solder on a vertically mounted board with large exposed planes and no solder mask runs downhill under gravity, so such boards must be laid flat.[1183]

The same mass is exploited deliberately. A TO-220 tab or a QFN thermal pad is soldered down to the plane so the board acts as the heat sink, with thermal vias carrying heat from the pad into the internal plane.[183][E1IqcGcZKHE][1322] Thermal jumpers route device heat through copper, through the jumper, down vias and into the plane, using the whole board as a heat sink while occupying very little area.[1347] Plane copper spreads heat far enough that a pad remote from a hot component measured 35 °C and climbing.[401]

## Reading and probing planes

On a finished board the plane is visible and diagnostic. A dark patch under illumination indicates internal power and ground layers, and the number of visible layers with no corresponding surface traces establishes a minimum layer count.[452][145][1415] A large solid pour on an unmarked board is almost certainly ground and is the safest place to take a reference.[932] Extensive via stitching together with flood fill and shielding indicates high-frequency circuitry.[687] A solid internal plane also defeats reverse engineering by backlighting, since inner traces cannot be seen through it.[675]

For measurement, the plane is the ground return, and the quality of the connection to it sets probe bandwidth. Where no convenient ground point exists, the options are scraping back solder mask and soldering to the plane, soldering in a contact loop pin, or applying copper tape.[GS0WqUKZ-3c][1718][689] A short spring tip with a ground blade reaches around 2 GHz; a soldered copper pad gives a flexible ground point.[Y7t6BIhBZhc] A shorter inductive path to the plane gives higher probe bandwidth.[1718] Where a board's designer has provided ground test points on the digital side but none on an isolated section, probing that section requires soldering something in.[660] A current probe traced over a plane shows current flowing under some regions and nothing at all under others, confirming that return current does not spread uniformly.[296]

## Ground planes in EMC testing

The term also denotes the large metal sheet used as a reference in emissions testing rather than a feature of a board. For conducted-emissions pre-compliance, the device under test sits on a 5 cm polystyrene block above a ground plane, with the LISN connected to the same plane and a spectrum analyser also sitting on it — everything referenced to one plane, because a cable leaving the plane invalidates the setup.[546][993] A metal plate is required; an antistatic or static-dissipative mat is not adequate.[993] The standards specify a horizontal and a vertical plane with defined dimensions and separations, the planes serving to stop capacitive coupling to other equipment in the room; at minimum the horizontal plane is used, and the vertical one is often omitted.[548] Such bench testing gives a ballpark indication of margin for a few hours' work rather than thousands of dollars of laboratory time, but the plane does not shield the setup from ambient fields such as LED lighting.[548] In an anechoic chamber the receive antenna is raised to 4 m precisely because of reflections off the ground plane.[202]

## Historical practice

Before ground planes were routine, ground and power were run as traces between chips, with a bus down each side of the board branching to each device, and chips deliberately aligned in rows so the two traces could be routed underneath them.[410] Boards of that era were laid out with one big plane and no controlled impedance because signal integrity at PCB level was not the concern it later became.[410] Double-sided boards were sometimes built specifically to obtain a plane on the top layer, even without plated-through holes, with component leads used as the through connections.[256] The crinkled appearance of large pours on old boards comes from tin plating the bare copper before solder mask, rather than the modern practice of solder mask over bare copper.[557][1258]
