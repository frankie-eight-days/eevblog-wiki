# flat flex cable

A flat flex cable is a set of printed conductors on a thin flexible carrier, used to carry signals between the boards, modules, displays and mechanisms inside an assembled product [1262][454]. It is the dominant interconnect in consumer electronics because it is effectively the only viable way to design a product into a usable form factor: a camcorder of ordinary specification contains half a dozen to a dozen separate flat flex cables and assemblies [454]. The same technology appears at the other end of the market as well, joining the digital and acquisition boards of bench instruments, the head assemblies of hard drives, and the driver boards of LCD panels [829][1398][465].

## Termination methods

Flat flex is terminated in three broadly different ways, and the choice governs both cost and repairability. The cheapest and most common is a connector on the board — either a simple press-fit clip, or a lever-and-bar type where the cable is inserted and a hinged bar is folded down to clamp it [370][661][1255]. Some connectors are locking types [295]. A retention clip pressing the cable against pads on the board is also used, and it is not always obvious by inspection whether such a joint is soldered or merely a pressure contact [830]. In one design the board carries only pads and no connector at all, with a bracket and a screw pushing the flex down onto them, the screw doing double duty as a board hold-down [775].

The second method is direct soldering of the flex to the board, either by hand or as a separate production process [271][863]. The third is hot bar attachment, in which a heated bar comes down across the end of the flex and reflows solder to bond the conductors directly onto the pads beneath [465][651]. Hot bar joints are also known as tape automated bonding, or TAB connections [1202]. They are common where a driver chip is mounted directly on the flex — chip-on-flex — with the die flipped onto the flexible carrier and the whole assembly bonded down to the PCB [651][914][1398]. A hot-bar-attached flex is not reusable in salvage, whereas a press-fit one is [547].

## Attachment to display glass

For an LCD, flat flex is one of three ways to get signals onto the glass, alongside through-hole pins and elastomeric zebra strips; the flex is bonded to the glass with a conductive glue and runs straight out to pins [1044]. It does not reduce the pin count in any way — all three are simply different interface styles to the same glass [1044]. Where the display is chip-on-glass, the flex tail is the only interface, and its pin field is documented in the panel's datasheet [1699][413]. Without an on-glass driver, every pin of the display must be driven individually, which for a 40-pin panel becomes a design problem in its own right [1055].

The conductive adhesive bond is a known failure point. A photocopier fault traced to the adhesive holding the flat flex strip to the LCD glass was recovered simply by heating each bond in turn [1100].

## Pitch, density and electrical limits

Pitches run from coarse, low-density cables to modern fine-pitch parts, and both can appear in the same product [809]. Fine pitch is fiddly to work with: 0.5 mm pitch on a 36-way display tail is small enough to be an obstacle in prototyping, to the point that a header connector may be wired on instead [1699][8]. A common pattern is a very dense trace pitch at the panel end that fans out to a wider-pitch connector at the board end [454]. Some sizes have become de facto standards — a generic 50-pin flat flex header on a 7-inch LCD makes many different panels interchangeable [g2kCBfUa9H0].

Electrically, flat flex and its connectors have little current capacity per pin and per conductor, so power is routed through several conductors paralleled up [631]. Powering a module entirely through its flat flex, with no separate supply terminals, is a design weakness in a part sold as a self-contained module [913]. An ordinary flexible flat cable cannot provide controlled impedance — the conductors are simply tracks running across it — and while some suppliers now sell flex with EMI shielding, shielding and controlled impedance are separate properties [1694]. Where an application demands many connections, shielding and controlled impedance together, rigid-flex is required, and its cost has to be justified by that requirement rather than by appearance or convenience [1694]. Shielding may also be added over an ordinary flex carrying differential pairs to a screen [842].

## Mechanical handling and strain

The flexible portion is the reliable part; the terminations and the transitions are not. In a repair context the ribbon itself virtually never breaks, while the connectors can be troublesome, particularly if the cable has been pulled out at the wrong angle [631]. A flex that steps from a thicker section to a thinner one will shear at that transition if the assembly is opened carelessly [687]. Large traces on a hard drive flex showed chunks missing around a bend, consistent with mechanical stress rather than any impedance feature [1398]. When a panel is folded out of its bezel for service, keeping stress off the flat flexes is the first concern, because the whole screen hinges on the flex drivers [1758][OdovWOP7ik4].

Manufacturers add stiffeners and restraints for the same reason. A clear fiberglass stiffening plate glued to the back of a camcorder flex gives it rigidity over its unsupported length [454]. Tape is routinely used to hold cables and connectors down so they cannot flap around [497][321][860]. One design goes as far as a dedicated PCB carrying no traces and no components whose sole purpose is to retain a flat flex and adjacent wiring with clips [378].

## Failure modes and repair

Fine cracks in the conductors are a common fault, and they can be small enough to need magnification to see [736]. With enough spare length, a broken flex can be cut across with scissors, the white covering scraped away to expose fresh conductor, and solder applied at very low temperature to rejoin the breaks — a delicate operation in which excess heat burns straight through the carrier [736]. Attempts to repair individual traces in place, without that care, tend to fail outright [661]. Hot bar attached flexes are the hardest case: there is no good way to fix them, and TAB connections are a known failure mode in instruments such as Fluke scopemeters [1202].

Standard diagnostic practice on a dead display is to reseat the flex and inspect it for broken conductors before looking further [918], and to flex, tap or press on the cables and their hot bar joints with an insulated poker while the unit runs, watching for any change in the fault [631][781]. A fault that gets steadily worse with time, and is unaffected by pressure on the flex, is not indicative of a flex problem, and attention moves to the T-con board [781]. Suspicion of a damaged flex is not always right — one intermittent failure after a user upgrade proved to be a missing screw rather than flex damage [1739]. Contamination is another mode: a leaking battery corroded the contacts of a keyboard flex to the point of destroying them [507].

Replacement is often the only option, and depends on obtaining the part by number or by cannibalising a junk unit with a smashed screen [o4S-TkTuHZs]. Recovered flat flex cables have limited salvage value, though a couple are worth keeping [488].

## Cost and design effort

Each flat flex in a product is a custom part that must be designed, and each generally implies a separate soldering or assembly production step [454]. A moderately complex camera or camcorder therefore carries a large hidden engineering cost in its flex alone, alongside the mechanical parts, brackets and moldings it interfaces with [454][816]. The connector-based approach also adds bill-of-materials items and additional things that can go wrong, which is one argument for integrating multiple rigid sections into a single flex PCB instead [1262]. At the margins the calculation is fine-grained: a dedicated flex carrying a single tactile dome switch to the main board is a cost that a right-angle switch on the board might have avoided [370][514].

## Historical use

Flat flex has been used in cameras since the Polaroid instant cameras of the 1970s, which were among the first consumer items to use the technology for joining things together [625][454]. It appears in a 1972 printing terminal running to the printhead [1169], in a 1970s programmable calculator [1153], and in an HP calculator of 1979 design where it forms the battery contacts and the expansion module contacts [582]. A vintage HP computer uses a flex ribbon roughly an inch wide as a single large copper strap between boards, in a machine otherwise wired discretely [903]. An early 1980s portable CRT television has a flat flex made up and glued onto the back of one board to join it to another, an unusual level of effort for the period given the discrete cable looms used elsewhere in the same set [830].
