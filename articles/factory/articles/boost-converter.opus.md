# boost converter

A boost converter is a switch-mode DC-to-DC converter that produces an output voltage higher than its input, storing energy in an inductor during each switch-on period and dumping that energy into the output capacitor when the switch opens.[139][273] It is the standard answer whenever a circuit needs a rail above whatever the supply or battery can provide — a vacuum fluorescent display running from 5 V logic, white LEDs whose forward voltage exceeds a flat cell, or a 5 V USB output taken from a single lithium cell.[1665][855][RasOXxxEhCk] Because it cannot step down, its inability to regulate below the input voltage is the single most consequential limitation in practice.[1710][1606]

## Topology and parts

The canonical circuit is a series inductor, a MOSFET pulling the inductor's far end to ground, and an output diode feeding the output capacitor.[273] The FET stores energy in the inductor and the inductor then dumps that energy into the output capacitor.[139] The configuration is general enough that a mains power factor correction pre-converter such as the MC34262 is exactly the same arrangement, operated to shape the input current into a half sinusoid rather than to produce a fixed step-up.[273]

Integrated controllers vary in how much of this they absorb. Most single-chip solutions contain the switching FET but still require an external inductor, diode and capacitors.[139][1] An AVR ATtiny variant advertised as having a built-in boost converter still needed all the usual external parts — an inductor, a diode and some caps — so the parts-count saving was modest.[1] Some converter chips do integrate the diode, which lowers both parts count and component cost.[139] Others, such as the LM2623, explicitly require an external one.[139]

Output voltage is set by a feedback resistor divider, and this is the standard access point for making the output adjustable: replacing or trimming the lower feedback resistor moves the regulation point.[259][260][1596] In one supply design the lower feedback resistor of a Micrel 2253 boost converter was an I2C-interfaced 5 kΩ digital potentiometer, letting firmware set the pre-regulator voltage.[260]

Switching frequencies run from tens of kilohertz through hundreds of kilohertz and up into the megahertz region, with output ripple of tens of millivolts and sometimes hundreds.[1116] A 3 MHz part such as the LTC3424 sits at the extreme high end of that range.[139] Electrolytic capacitors are commonly placed in parallel on the output of such converters rather than as a single bulk part.[742]

## The step-up-only constraint

A boost converter that is not switching does not block — it passes the input straight through to the output, minus a diode drop.[1710] A module with a 12 V input sitting at 11.5 V out is therefore behaving exactly as expected when its enable pin has not been driven; it needs an external 3.3 V or 5 V logic signal before it will boost at all.[1710]

The same asymmetry shows up as a hard ceiling in products that omit a boost stage. A 100 W USB-C bench supply fed 12 V can only reach 11.7 V out, because there is no internal boost converter to bump the input up.[1606] A $53 360 W bench module is step-down only, so reaching higher voltages means feeding in a higher supply.[1265][1030] A $20 supply built from step-down modules is limited to 11.26 V maximum on a 12 V input.[1030] An oscilloscope-and-generator instrument taking 5 V from USB offers only ±4 V rails because no boost or SEPIC converter was fitted.[1056] Where both directions are needed, a SEPIC accepts input voltages above or below the output — but the premium is real: an LT1512 SEPIC constant-current constant-voltage battery charger cost $3.85 even at hundred-piece quantity, making it the most expensive semiconductor in the design it was considered for.[259] Combined buck-boost and SEPIC modules exist as an alternative to plain step-down units.[1265]

## Efficiency and part selection

Efficiency is a strong function of output current, not a headline number. A typical boost converter chip may reach 90% efficiency, but only over a narrow band of output current; at the tens or hundreds of microamps a remote control actually draws, efficiency can fall to around 50%, and some converters are worse.[751]

Selecting a part therefore means reading the graphs rather than the front page. Taking a single cell up to 5 V at 2.5 W is a demanding application, and most candidates fail on one axis or another.[139] Parametric search narrowed to step-up devices, then to output power, is the practical route — output current alone is deceptive because these chips are specified over very wide input voltage ranges.[139] Concrete failures from one such search: the LTC3424 delivers 1 A at 5 V from a 3.6 V input at 90% efficiency, but from low input voltages its curve tails off completely by 250 mA and reaches only 50 mA at a 1 V input; a Maxim 1947 offers 0.7 V minimum input and 94% efficiency but a fixed 3.3 V output that will not reach 5 V at all; a Texas Instruments candidate at 1.2 V input runs out near 100 mA, about five times short.[139]

## Applications

Displays and high-voltage loads are the classic case. A vacuum fluorescent display cannot run from 5 V TTL levels, so a small boost converter generates the display rail; the input side carries a 100 µF cap rated 10 V while the output carries a 10 µF cap rated 50 V, and if that output electrolytic loses its electrolyte its ESR goes through the roof.[1665] A module built to drive old Soviet VFDs takes 12 V in and produces 32 to 54 V adjustable via a trimmer, drawing 4.6 mA quiescent from an 8 to 16 V input and holding up under a 10 W constant-power load.[1710] Valve headphone amplifiers running from a 6 V plug pack likewise need boost circuitry to reach valve operating voltages.[837] An electric fence controller stacks two stages: a first switcher generating perhaps 700–800 V across parallel 900 V caps, then a second switching stage taking energy from those caps to boost to the high output voltage.[1277]

LED drive is another staple: a Texas Instruments 61500 constant-current boost LED driver with dimming runs five LEDs in a series string from 5 V DC.[1010] A TPS61175 3 A high-voltage boost converter with soft start and programmable switching frequency provides the adjustable positive and negative rails in a portable instrument.[1596] A current-mode boost using a TPS40211 with a switching MOSFET and dual inductors produces 180 V out at a claimed 40 W RMS.[1263]

Battery-powered equipment relies on boost conversion to use more of the cell. A multimeter running from a 9 V battery with a 6.5 V or 7 V cut-out throws away a large fraction of the battery's capacity, and a boost front end recovers it.[200] Two 9 V cells in series start at 18 V but drop under load below the 15 V needed for diode mode, so a boost stage is required regardless.[ILIO5b1BliE] An LTC1304 with a 1.5 V minimum input and 10 µA standby current is the sort of device expected in a two-alkaline-cell product, here backing up a 0.1 F 5.5 V supercapacitor.[334] A 34063 serves as the boost converter for a meter's transistor tester.[1580] Backlight whine in an instrument traces to the boost converter driving LEDs that need more voltage than the flat batteries can supply.[855] Energy harvesting takes the same approach at lower power, whether from a solar cell or from the boost stage in an isotope-powered cell concept.[534][1333]

Elsewhere, boost stages appear inside an airbag power management controller alongside a buck, a linear regulator and reset watchdog on one die[517]; in a lithium pack charger that must generate voltages above its 12 V input[397]; in a robot base so a netbook can run from the same battery[429]; in USB-powered modules producing 9 V or 12 V[745]; in a two-AA converter giving selectable 5 V or 3.3 V at up to 350 mA on fresh cells[320]; and inside flash microcontrollers, which generate their own programming voltage internally rather than requiring an external high-voltage pulse.[1060][1539]

## Pre-regulation

A boost converter followed by a linear regulator makes a tracking pre-regulator: the switcher holds its output only a couple of volts above the linear regulator's output so that dissipation in the pass device stays low while the output retains the lower noise inherent in linear regulation.[260][329] The drawback of a firmware-driven implementation is that software must continuously measure the output and compensate the pre-regulator voltage to match.[260] Post-regulation is also the general remedy for the high-frequency ripple a DC-DC converter leaves on its output.[1116]

## Diagnosis and EMC

On the bench a boost stage identifies itself by voltage relationships: a capacitor sitting at 7.7 V when the battery is lower marks a boost output, and finding 5.2 V at one node and 3.5 V at another distinguishes input from regulated output.[1564] Single-shot capture of a converter starting up shows the ramp: one speaker amplifier boosts from just under 4 V of battery to 5 V and then steps deliberately to 12 V.[1605] A failure appears as the absence of that behaviour — a 5.143 V input and 4.98 V after the output diode means the converter is not switching[366], and a battery bank showing 3.3 V and then 4.4 V on its boost node with a live input points at a faulty controller.[V0RWwSw96Sw]

Boost converters are a standard EMC demonstration vehicle, with paired good and bad layouts of the same 300 kHz converter boosting 19 V to 24 V into an electronic load at around 420 mA.[oVkrc3gF7ns] As a design task the topology is comparatively tractable: an LLC resonant converter is vastly more difficult to design than the traditional PWM boost, buck or SEPIC converters.[1294]

## Limits on what boosting can achieve

A boost converter cannot create energy that is not in the source. Products marketed on the premise that a boost sleeve unlocks most of a discarded battery's capacity founder on this point: manufacturers' discharge curves stop at 0.8 V because there is very little energy left below it at ordinary loads, though ultra-low-power boost converters can reach some of it at very small drains.[774] Holding the output at a fixed 1.5 V as the cell sags means drawing progressively more current from the battery, and the converter's own efficiency loss compounds that.[963][789] On passive loads the result was a toy train running faster but dying at 94 minutes against 169 minutes unassisted.[963] Where the cells were genuinely flat, no converter helped at all — it does not matter how good the boost converter is if there is no energy left in the batteries.[789] The realistic gain from adding such a stage to a product that already discharges its cells reasonably far is an extra couple of percent.[789] A converter small enough to fit inside a battery sleeve also carries very limited output capacitance and peak current capability, leaving pulse loads to be handled by the product's own decoupling.[751] The engineering judgment is direct: a boost converter is not magic, and what it can deliver is bounded by the energy remaining in the source.[789]
