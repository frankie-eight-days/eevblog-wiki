# common mode rejection ratio

Common mode rejection ratio (CMRR) is the ratio of a differential amplifier's differential gain to its common mode gain.[1521] Every differential amplifier circuit has one, whether it is built from an op amp or from discrete transistors.[1521] The figure matters because a differential amplifier's job is to amplify the difference between its positive and negative inputs while rejecting whatever signal is applied commonly to both wires, and CMRR states how completely it succeeds.[1521] In theory the rejection would be infinite — the amplifier would measure only the difference and have no gain at all for a common mode signal — but in practice the design of the amplifier itself, and above all the matching of the input resistor network, sets a finite limit.[1521]

## Units and sign convention

CMRR is usually quoted in dB, though as a pure ratio it does not have to be.[1521] Differential gain divided by common mode gain comes out positive, yet data sheets frequently print a negative number, and there is no standard settling which is used.[1521] The consequence is that the sign determines which direction is better: with a negative convention, −60 dB is better rejection than −40 dB, while on a positive convention such as an op amp data sheet might use, a higher number is better.[1521]

A further trap is the reference point. A probe specification may be input referred — stated at the input of the amplifier inside the probe, before the internal gain stage — which makes the number look considerably better than an output-referred figure.[1521] A gain of ×10 inside the probe is 20 dB, ×100 is 40 dB, and ×1000 is 60 dB, and that offset must be accounted for when a bench measurement is compared against the sheet.[1521] A well-written data sheet states which convention it uses; declaring a figure input referred is an easy way for marketing to make rejection sound better than it is.[1521]

## What limits rejection in practice

The dominant limit in a resistively divided differential front end is matching. In the classic single-op-amp difference amplifier the two divider pairs must be equal — the upper resistors to each other and the lower resistors to each other — and they must match precisely to get good rejection.[600] In a high-voltage differential probe the same principle applies to the whole attenuator string: if the string on one input is unbalanced relative to the other, the CMRR is ruined.[1415]

Because matching cannot be achieved by tolerance alone, probes are trimmed. The DP10007 carries two additional trim pots on its positive input that the negative input lacks, one of them a CMRR trimmer used to balance the positive path against the negative, with a separate gain trimmer to match the differential gain.[1415] The same idea appears in older analogue instrumentation, where a CMRR pot sits alongside an input level pot on the board.[574] Matching is also fragile in service: moisture creeping under the input components will unbalance them and degrade CMRR, which is why an O-ring seal and removed solder mask appear around that region of a probe's board.[1744]

Scope channel math is the cheapest differential measurement available — subtracting channel 1 from channel 2 in the math menu gives a differential waveform on analogue and digital scopes alike — but it is a poor man's differential probe precisely because the CMRR is terrible, being set by how well the two probes' input impedances happen to match.[324]

## Frequency dependence

CMRR is not a single number; it varies with frequency, so a product will typically specify it at several spot frequencies, and occasionally as a response curve.[1521] Rejection degrades as frequency rises. On a 70 MHz high-voltage differential probe the degradation becomes visible from around 5 MHz upward.[1521] Measured with a frequency response analyser and corrected for the ×10 setting, that probe gave −51 dB at 1 MHz and −44 dB at 10 MHz against a −40 dB specification at 10 MHz, but only −21 dB at 70 MHz.[1521] The steepness of the roll-off above 10 MHz explains why data sheets commonly stop quoting at 10 MHz rather than at 50 or 70 MHz.[1521] Extending the sweep far enough also shows the phase response reverse, which is ordinary amplifier behaviour rather than anything specific to differential circuits, and the phase plot carries no useful information for the CMRR figure itself.[1521]

Independent confirmation with a vector network analyser reproduced the shape but not the exact value, returning 47.5 dB at 1 MHz where the specification was 50 dB and an earlier oscilloscope-based measurement had given 51 dB.[Dez9KG6whb0]

## Measuring it

The measurement drives both inputs with the same signal and compares the amplitude at the input against the amplitude at the output; the ratio of the two, expressed as 20 log of output over input, is the CMRR.[1521] Test setup is decisive, because the quantity being measured is almost down in the noise floor of the instrument.[1744] Leads should be short, twisted and shielded.[1521] A 12-bit oscilloscope helps, as does high-resolution acquisition mode — with HD mode giving an effective 16 bits — and selecting the probe's highest attenuation range to maximise the available signal level.[1631][1744] Long input leads are a liability: external signals can couple into them and wreck the apparent rejection outright, which argues for direct banana-plug inputs when such measurements are routine.[1744] Dave Jones's view is that a factory CMRR test should not use long leads at all but should crack the probe open and solder directly onto the input points.[1744]

Worked examples follow the same arithmetic. A measured common mode residue of 22.67 mV against an 811 mV input gives −31 dB at 100 MHz.[1744] A residue of 26.2 mV against 804.5 mV works out to −29.7 dB, taken as meeting a −26 dB specification at 100 MHz.[1631]

Measuring the CMRR of a bare op amp is harder than measuring a probe, because the external resistor network built to apply the common mode signal dominates the result.[1521] A technique exists that removes the resistors from the measurement and yields the amplifier's own rejection, using a step difference in the power rail voltage instead.[1521]

## In op amps

Op amp data sheets quote CMRR as a positive dB figure, with precision parts reaching 140 dB typical at a 2.25 V supply and chopper-stabilised parts around 130 dB.[1325][476] Whether the parameter matters depends entirely on the topology: it is not a leading concern for a current-measurement front end whose resistors are not directly coupled into the input, where power supply and common mode rejection enter only indirectly.[1325][1328] CMRR is also one of the specifications watched when a manufacturer re-releases an existing part on new silicon; a revised NE5532 kept it unchanged while other specifications moved.[1752]

## High-voltage differential probes and their ceiling

For mains work and higher-voltage power supply work a high-voltage differential probe is essential kit.[1557] Its limitation is that CMRR falls with frequency exactly where modern switching designs operate.[1557] The problem case is a high-side gate-source measurement: the source terminal itself swings the full rail, so the larger the swinging voltage and the higher the switching frequency, the more rejection the probe needs to keep that swing out of the measurement.[1557] With a 300 V rail switching at 1 kHz a high-voltage differential probe is fine, and the waveform is unaffected even at full voltage; at 1 MHz the rejection has dropped far enough that ringing and switching artefacts dominate the gate drive waveform entirely.[1557] Since the efficiency case for GaN rests on switching fast, the measurement problem is intrinsic to the application rather than incidental.[1557]

Probe CMRR is also where cheaper high-voltage differential probes have failed to deliver. One unit matched every other specification but missed its CMRR specification and was not even close, a result others confirmed independently, and it took years and a hardware revision before the manufacturer addressed it.[1414][1631][1744] The revised model still did not match the benchmark Sapphire-designed HVP70 on CMRR, though its bandwidth was better than that probe's 70 MHz.[1744]

## Optically isolated probes

Optically isolated probes sidestep the matching problem rather than solving it. Power is transferred one way and signal the other entirely in the analogue domain with no sampling and no metallic conductor between probe and oscilloscope, giving galvanic isolation up to 60 kV together with the bandwidth of an active FET probe.[1557] The resulting rejection is over 100 dB — 106 dB for a 200 MHz version, held across the full 200 MHz bandwidth — high enough that measuring it on a normal bench is itself difficult.[1557] Architecturally these are single-ended active high-frequency probes rather than differential ones; the rejection comes from the isolation, not from a matched input network.[1557]

At that level of rejection the high-side gate-source measurement that defeats a differential probe simply stops being a problem.[1557] The same argument drives isolated instrument design generally: an isolated 1 kV oscilloscope front end has two requirements, that it be isolated and floating, and that it have very high CMRR to keep noise from the fast-slewing signal out of the measurement, specified there as 100 dB at 50 MHz.[1119] Beyond power electronics, the combination of roughly 100 dB rejection with tens of kilovolts of isolation is what makes these probes the only option for high-energy physics work.[1557]
