# sample rate

Sample rate is the number of discrete samples a digitising instrument captures per second, expressed in samples per second (S/s) or megasamples/gigasamples per second (MS/s, GS/s). Together with analog bandwidth and memory depth, it determines what a digital oscilloscope, logic analyser, or data logger can faithfully reproduce: insufficient sample rate produces aliasing, distorted waveform reconstruction, and misleadingly precise-looking measurements.[1213][506][1140]

## Sample rate versus bandwidth

The Nyquist criterion sets an absolute floor: the sampled bandwidth can be at most half the sample rate. A 40 MS/s ADC in a spectrum analyser's IF stage, for example, constrains the baseband frequency to below 20 MHz.[892][1109] Nyquist is a floor rather than a target — at 2.5 samples per cycle a signal does not alias, but reconstruction quality is poor.[1355][1213][1038]

For sin(x)/x interpolation to reconstruct a waveform properly, "You need roughly four times the analog bandwidth in sample rate."[797] At five times bandwidth — for instance a 200 MHz sine wave on a 1 GS/s scope — the display shows only five dots per cycle and looks visibly jaggy.[1213] The long-standing industry benchmark is a 10:1 ratio of real-time sample rate to analog bandwidth, established when the Tektronix TDS220 paired 1 GS/s with 100 MHz bandwidth and set the trend for modern scopes.[690][747] A working rule of thumb holds that usable single-shot bandwidth is roughly the sample rate divided by ten; a 300 MHz scope sampling at 500 MS/s has a real single-shot bandwidth of only about 50 MHz.[107] Where a trade-off is forced, sample rate is generally more valuable than headline bandwidth.[107] At the high end, instruments reach 40 GS/s on 13 GHz-class scopes and beyond, with platforms announced at 40 GS/s and 16 GHz.[340][114][5YjS4DHKlQU]

## Aliasing and interpolation artifacts

Signals above half the sample rate fold back into the display as aliases. An 8.1 MHz signal sampled at 5 MS/s aliases immediately, while a slightly offset frequency such as 8.123 MHz can produce a benign beat pattern instead — the distinction between true aliasing and a sample beat frequency depends on the exact relationship between signal and sample clock.[j49T1E4UEv4] Beat artifacts of this kind appear around 250 MHz on a scope sampling near that region.[1582]

Interpolation can conceal insufficient sampling behind a plausible-looking trace. Sin(x)/x interpolation is only valid when the Nyquist requirement is met; with a 200 MHz bandwidth and only a 200 MS/s sample rate, the reconstruction is "completely mathematically invalid".[1213] Because scopes ship with interpolation on by default, a smooth but wrong waveform can send troubleshooting down a false path; switching to dot mode reveals the actual samples and exposes the artifact.[1213][1220][797]

## Channel sharing and interleaving in oscilloscopes

The banner sample rate on a multi-channel oscilloscope is almost always a single-channel figure, because ADCs are shared across channels. Enabling a second channel typically halves the rate; enabling three or four can quarter it. The Rigol DS1054Z drops from 1 GS/s to 500 MS/s with two channels and 250 MS/s with three or four, because a single ADC serves all four channels.[703][704][674] The same single-ADC cost-saving architecture appears in the GW Instek GDS-1000B (one HAD1511 converter, 250 MS/s across four channels), the Siglent SDS1104X-E (500 MS/s with all four channels), and the Rigol DHO800 (a single 1.25 GS/s ADC divided among four channels).[824][1042][1563]

Scopes with two ADCs — one per channel pair — let channels 1 and 3 (or 2 and 4) each retain full rate and full memory, while channels 1 and 2 together halve both.[475][384][792][1530] Interleaving runs the other way: combining converter cores doubles the single-channel rate, so the Agilent 2000 X-Series reaches its advertised 2 GS/s only on one channel and drops to 1 GS/s when a second is enabled, and the 3000 X-Series similarly halves from 4 GS/s to 2 GS/s.[143][149] Some designs are worse than the banner suggests: the Tektronix MDO3000 series delivers only 2.5 GS/s at 1 GHz bandwidth even without additional channels enabled.[1218] Newer architectures can avoid the penalty entirely — the Keysight HD3 sustains 3.2 GS/s on all four channels simultaneously.[1638] At the silicon level, high rates are built from many slower cores: the Rigol DS1052E interleaves ten 100 MHz ADCs to reach 1 GS/s, and the MDO3000's RF input ties the analog-channel ADCs together to sample at 10 GS/s.[19][587]

## Interaction with memory depth and time base

Capture duration equals memory depth divided by sample rate, so a scope must reduce its sample rate at slow time bases once memory is exhausted. A scope with 4 Mpts of memory at 5 GS/s holds only 800 µs of data, forcing the rate down to 2.5 GS/s at 50 µs/div and 1.25 GS/s at 100 µs/div; with 400 Mpts it could sustain 5 GS/s far longer.[1311] The same trade-off appears throughout: the Rigol DS1052E's full 1 Mpt memory is available only at 500 MS/s and below, and the Owon SDS series reaches 3.2 GS/s only at 1K or 10K record lengths, dropping to 1 GS/s at 100K and deeper.[kdjpbWLi7UI][480] Zooming out past the captured record, FFT quality, and phase-measurement resolution are all bounded by this interplay of sample rate, memory depth, and time base.[1311][845][1751]

## Effect on displayed noise and acquisition modes

Higher sample rates admit more high-frequency noise content into the display, so a digital scope appears noisier at maximum sample rate; shortening the record length or limiting bandwidth visibly cleans up the trace.[601][610] Consequently, meaningful noise-floor comparison between two digital scopes requires matched sample rate, memory depth, bandwidth, intensity grading, and update rate.[610] High-resolution acquisition mode exploits oversampling directly, boxcar-averaging samples taken at the scope's full rate to average out noise in real time at slow time bases.[878] Peak-detect mode similarly uses the underlying high sample rate to register narrow pulses that the decimated display rate would otherwise miss.[869]

## Timing resolution in logic analysers and digital capture

In any sampling system, timing resolution is bounded by the sample period. Measuring a 2 MHz signal at 12 MS/s yields incorrect frequency readings because the temporal resolution is inadequate, and even 24 MS/s shows periodic duty-cycle errors.[1140] Analysis software can display a resolution finer than the hardware delivers — pulse widths shown to 1 ns resolution from an 8 MS/s capture are fictitious, and at 500 kS/s the true resolution is only 2 µs despite three-decimal readouts.[506] Logic analysers distinguish asynchronous timing analysis from clock-synchronous state analysis, and the maximum sample rate for state analysis is generally lower than for timing analysis on any given unit.[44][1018] Asynchronous timing capture also quantises edges to the sample period, producing inherent one-sample jitter.[UDGsZcAWgL8]

## Sample rate in other instruments

Power and energy instruments sample continuously at fixed rates: the Voltech PM300 samples near 100 kHz to enable transient capture, the BattLab One's 1 kHz rate is adequate for active-current energy calculations, and the Uni-T UTE310 integrates voltage and current at 1 MS/s so short transmit spikes are not missed.[589][1331][1693] Logging interval choice matters at the slow end too: one sample per minute missed the entire sub-0.8 V knee of a battery discharge curve, where one sample per second resolved it.[774] Digitising multimeters span from the Gossen Metrawatt Xtra's 0.1 s to 9 h per sample up to the Keithley DMM7510's 1 M readings per second.[46][731] In function generators the DAC sample rate sets the output ceiling — 500 MS/s in the Siglent SDG5000, 1.2 GS/s for the 16-bit DAC in the SDG2122X, and 2.5 GS/s in 500 MHz-class Uni-T generators.[497][805][cziiWo6Uh5M] Converter resolution trades against speed: the Owon XDS3202A's 1 GS/s rate falls to about 125 MS/s in its 14-bit mode.[1004][ByUiOk00K0U] At the application level, sensor sample rates are often trivially slow — strain gauges need only a few hertz and accelerometers rarely exceed tens of kilohertz — while ADC source impedance, not just the converter, can cap the achievable sampling rate.[fm13tIe5wSc][232]

## Misleading specifications and verification

Sample-rate claims require scrutiny. The headline figure applies to one channel, and some low-cost handhelds advertise "effective" rates achievable only through equivalent-time sampling: a tablet scope claiming 1 GS/s delivered only 200 MS/s real-time per channel, its two ADCs never interleaved.[1317] Equivalent-time mode is legitimate for repetitive signals — the GW Instek GDS-2000A switches to it at 1 ns/div and indicates 100 GS/s — but it is not real-time capture.[474] Older instruments sometimes concealed the figure entirely; the Fluke 91 Scopemeter manual omitted it on the theory that users did not understand "this sample rate stuff".[430] On the Keysight 1000 X-Series, sample rate is set by configuration resistors and can be altered by modification, though pushing it to 5 GS/s produced broken behaviour at slow time bases.[977][978] Sound bench practice is to keep the current sample rate and memory depth visible on screen — instruments that display both permanently support correct use, while those that hide them invite mismeasurement — and to recheck the rate whenever channels are enabled.[704][703][474][1213][1220]
