# pcb panelization

PCB panelization is the practice of stepping and repeating a single board design across a larger sheet of laminate so that many copies are fabricated, assembled and handled as one unit.[127] The panel is not merely a shipping convenience: it is itself a printed circuit board, carrying the mechanical frame, the tooling holes and the optical fiducials that assembly machinery needs to find the work, and it can carry copper traces and even active circuitry of its own.[552] Getting a single small board manufactured and populated loose is uneconomical, because the pick and place machine processes that one board and ejects it, with handling problems at every stage.[127]

## Why boards are panelized

The dominant reason is assembly throughput. Rather than running one tiny board at a time through the pick and place machine, a panel lets every part on every copy be placed in a single pass.[552] Anyone building in reasonable quantity through a pick and place machine panelizes as a matter of course.[552] On a production line, a six-board nest can clear an inspection or placement station in about 82 seconds, amortising the cycle across all six.[684]

The benefit is not confined to machine assembly. A hand assembler often prefers a large panel, because handling is easier and the same component can be placed across ten boards in succession.[239] Handing a bare-board assembler a tiny loose board invites a large surcharge, and the work will likely end up hand-soldered anyway.[1158]

Bare-board cost also favours panels. The fabricator charges essentially by panel area, so board outlines that pack badly waste money: an odd-shaped board simply does not fit as many times onto a panel.[3_CQKSC1JNE] Designs that square off their outline, using cutouts in the metalwork rather than a contoured PCB edge, fit more copies per panel.[1237] A large four-layer board consumes a great deal of panel space, which is why small prototype-shield-sized boards can be sold five for ten dollars while a big board cannot.[ct9K-EutiIQ] Very long assemblies are split into halves for the same reason, because a metre-long board is impractical for most pick and place machines and PCB panels.[915] Historically the economics were starker still: a custom double-sided board once cost hundreds of dollars for a single piece precisely because the buyer had to purchase the entire panel, so the design was made to fit as many copies as possible.[mW-1FMGpsFg] Early volume production worked the same way, with roughly eight boards to a panel drilled several deep at once on quad drills.[280]

## Anatomy of a panel

In its minimal form a panel is a physical mounting frame: tooling holes to hold the assembly in place inside the pick and place machine, and visual alignment fiducials for the placement head to register against.[552] Breakaway rails run outside the board array to give the machinery something to grip, which matters particularly when several different designs share the sheet.[1259]

A worked example places the individual boards in an array, then adds tooling strips top and bottom carrying 3.2 mm tooling holes, with fiducials formed as pads with the solder mask expanded around them to give high contrast.[127] Fiducials are placed on both sides even when components populate only one, and the panel outline is sized from simple arithmetic on the individual board dimensions — a 79 mm by 50 mm board scaled up by the desired row and column count — then adjusted by dragging the panel corners.[239] Fabricators add their own margin of waste material around the outside of the customer panel for handling and alignment, including alignment holes of their own, since panels are stacked and drilled several at a time.[939]

Spacing between adjacent boards is a deliberate choice rather than a leftover. Setting the pitch 2.4 mm wider than the board itself leaves a 2.4 mm gap, which matches a standard routing bit width and lets the fabricator clear the whole slot in a single pass of the router.[239] Fabricators can route any width requested, but a single-pass path is cheaper and cleaner.[239] Silkscreen outlines are pulled back so that roughly a 1 mm gap remains, rather than pushing artwork to the edge of what the process will hold.[974]

## Separation methods

Three techniques hold a board inside its panel, and they are routinely mixed on the same design.[939]

V-grooving, or V-scoring, cuts a shallow groove from both faces so the boards snap out.[127] It is cheap and simple, but it is destructive to copper: the scoring wheel saws through anything crossing the groove, so no signal can be routed across a V-scored boundary on either face.[552] A V-scored edge is also rough to the touch, which is one of the tells that a finished board was snapped out of a panel rather than routed individually.[127]

Routing cuts the board outline with a drill that plunges and then mills any shape required, leaving small tabs — mouse bites — to retain the board until it is broken out.[3kdYGneg9xI] Routing is what makes non-rectangular outlines possible, including circular boards, which are panelized with breakout tabs like any other shape.[407] Because the router works to a finite bit radius, inside corners come back rounded, which is visible on the finished piece.[3kdYGneg9xI] Mouse bites and break-off tabs are the standard evidence that a board was panelized; they appear on cost-optimised instrument boards where several copies clearly shared a sheet,[985] on older commercial assemblies,[648] and on flex panels.[1262]

Which method is chosen has consequences for the finished appearance. On a board that forms a product's front panel, the separation features are deliberately placed at the corners so that trimming with side cutters leaves clean edges; left to a fabricator's default they may be put on the sides, which looks noticeably worse.[558] For a board hidden inside an enclosure this does not matter.[558]

Break-off tabs are sometimes used for reasons that have nothing to do with fabrication. A section of a larger board may be tabbed off and separated into its own module simply because it is tested on a separate test rig, even though the mouse bites could have been left connected with no electrical penalty.[1101] Conversely, cutting an outline back at the panelization stage buys nothing if the routing has to happen anyway.[148]

## Panelizing yourself versus letting the fabricator do it

For production, the panel should be a controlled design the customer owns and hands to the assembler, so that every parameter is known.[1259] A fabricator left to panelize at will may omit the tooling holes and fiducials the assembly process requires.[1259] Supplying only a single-board file leaves the fabricator guessing, and the default placement of separation features can land in the worst possible position for the product.[558] The recommended practice is therefore to do the panelization in-house: it is quick, and it avoids protracted back-and-forth with the bare-board manufacturer.[239]

Fabricator panelization remains acceptable where the panel exists purely for handling — twenty copies of one design wanted as a single unit, with V-scoring, and no assembly-machine requirements to satisfy.[1259] It is also worth noting that panelizing an existing loose design incurs a fresh tooling charge that a straight reorder would not, though the charge is modest; since retooling is being paid for anyway, it is a natural moment to fold in other artwork changes.[239] Some fabricators charge nothing extra per unit for a panelized board over a loose one, which changes the comparison between suppliers substantially: a supplier several times more expensive on a loose board may come out only modestly more expensive once panelization is priced in.[241] Panelization is sometimes supplied unrequested, along with spare panels from the same run.[241]

## Shared prototype panels

Low-cost prototype services achieve their prices by pooling many customers' designs onto one manufacturing panel, then breaking them out and shipping each customer their own boards.[1259][1306] Because the sheet is shared, every design on it must conform to the same fixed process parameters, and the service will adjust incoming Gerbers where they do not comply — expanding or reducing solder mask, for instance, to meet a 0.2 mm minimum mask bridge between copper pad edges.[1327] A customer paying for a whole panel can dictate such terms; a customer paying a few dollars for five boards on a shared panel cannot.[1327]

Shared panels also constrain what post-processing is available. Selective V-scoring of one customer's boards cannot be done, because the whole panel goes through the scoring machine as a unit; requesting it forces the design onto its own separate process at substantially higher cost.[1306] Where a fabricator does offer a customer-specific panel, the extra setup carries a price premium, and ordering several different designs as separate single pieces can work out cheaper than requesting one customer panel.[1259] Some services also attach a yield disclaimer to shared panels, charging up to 30 percent more if the customer will not accept the possibility of a few bad boards in the batch.[1259]

The pooled-panel process leaves a visible artefact: the fabricator prints an identifier code on each design so that the correct boards can be cut out and shipped to the correct customer once the shared panel is separated.[1259] The mark may land in copper, in silkscreen, or elsewhere, and it is unwelcome on a board whose appearance matters.[1259] Some fabricators offer the option to remove it, and better ones omit it entirely; if a board is a visible front panel, the batch code can be excluded on request.[1471]

Ordering a design that was published as a multi-up panel does not oblige the customer to build all of the copies; a panel of four programmers can be ordered when only one is wanted, though snapping the copies apart afterwards requires V-scoring to have been specified.[1306] Panelization can equally be declined outright — small flexible circuits, for example, may be ordered as single pieces.[1262]

## Panel size limits

The usable panel size is bounded at both ends of the supply chain: the bare-board fabricator states a maximum panel it can process, and the assembler states a maximum the pick and place machine can accept.[255] A panel can be made as large as desired subject to those two limits.[1158] Board outlines that do not tile efficiently may be constrained by panel width at the fabricator or the assembler.[840] A board too large to fit a regular panel is effectively a custom job.[1471]

## Circuitry on the panel

Because the panel is part of the PCB, traces, connectors and complete circuits can be placed on the frame outside the product outline, and signals routed on and off the individual boards.[552] This turns otherwise wasted frame area into production test infrastructure: traces run from each board out to the panel rails let a whole array be tested while still panelized, without breaking boards out, inserting batteries, and plugging leads into each unit in turn.[552]

The motivating case is instructive. A ten-up panel, arranged 5 by 2, reduced fabrication and assembly cost, but each unit still had to be snapped out, have a battery fitted, be tested and then have the battery removed again before shipping — a particular nuisance for a product containing a lithium cell.[552] Routing the supply and signal connections through the panel frame removes that per-unit handling.[552] Even a modest implementation of this pays for itself at test time, and where such traces are needed the individual board must be routed to bring signals out to its corners before the panel is assembled.[552] Nothing prevents adding active circuitry to the frame, in which case the panel warrants a schematic of its own.[552] The one constraint is separation method: V-grooving forecloses this option, since the scoring wheel destroys any trace crossing the groove.[552]

Designing testability into the product or the production panel from the outset is what makes this cheap; retrofitting test to a finished board is not.[552] Below the tens or hundreds of thousands of units that justify a dedicated automated test system, a panel-level test fixture is the practical middle ground.[552]

## CAD support

Panelization is a distinct CAD operation rather than a matter of copying and pasting board outlines. In Altium Designer the panel is created as a separate PCB inside the same project, and an embedded board array is placed into it with a nominated source PCB file, a column count and a row count.[239] The array does not render the underlying tracks, silkscreen and other artwork on screen, but it records the placement information so that the Gerber generation produces the full panel — a large panel takes correspondingly longer to output.[239] Panelization intent that the fabricator must act on, such as where V-grooving and routing are wanted, is added as lines on a mechanical layer, sometimes called a fab notes layer, alongside written notes the manufacturer reads and follows.[127][239][974] A single design can specify different treatments per edge, for example V-grooving on the top and bottom edges with the sides routed.[974]

Doing it manually by copying boards has traps: designators may be automatically incremented on paste, requiring the behaviour to be disabled.[239] Packages without a panelization feature leave no alternative to the manual approach.[239]

Panelization support is often positioned as a paid or professional-tier feature, on the reasoning that hobby users do not need high-volume manufacturing capability.[255] Automated panelization functions can be genuinely convenient — specifying a 3 by 3 array with top and bottom rail edges and having the tool calculate the resulting panel size, or checking that a 5 by 5 arrangement fits within the fabricator's maximum panel.[255] The common shortcoming is that the automation stops short of the details that make a panel manufacturable: an automatically generated panel may arrive with no tooling holes and no fiducials, which will cause trouble at the manufacturer, so the designer must know to add them.[255]

## Panelization in practice

Beyond assembly, the panel is the unit of work throughout bare-board fabrication. Photoresist-coated panels are aligned to film on both sides, sometimes with two different panel sizes exposed together for efficiency, and drilling is performed on stacks of several panels taped together and drilled at once.[939] Final routing cuts the specified panel outline out of the fabricator's larger working sheet.[939]

Finished boards are frequently shipped still attached to their panels, and assembled panels are a normal intermediate stock form.[266][HyXMhto615c] Some designers supply their assembler with a purpose-built aluminium frame carrier matched to the panel, which can also substitute for a solder paste stencil supplied by the assembler.[m9tza_c4sxc] Where a board must be built loose, an assembler can produce a custom carrier routed to the board's shape and mount the pieces in it, at an additional tooling cost that may be justified when a fully routed edge finish is required.[127]

Prototype quoting reflects the same structure: a service asks either for the dimensions of the single board or the dimensions of a customer-supplied panel, and if the fabricator is doing the panelization it computes the required area from the single-board size and quotes accordingly.[1259] A representative prototype quote for a 185 by 69 mm design returned a five-board minimum at around thirty-two dollars with a two to three day turn.[990]
