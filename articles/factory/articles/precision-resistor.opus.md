# precision resistor

A precision resistor is a resistor bought for the tightness of its tolerance and the stability of its value rather than for its nominal resistance alone, with grades running from 0.1% down through 0.01% to 0.005% and better.[72][735][374] It is the component that sets the accuracy of measurement instruments, current sources, voltage dividers and calibration standards, because in those circuits nothing downstream can be more accurate than the resistor the signal is developed across.[567][577][853] It is also, for its size, one of the most expensive passive parts on a board: a single fine-tolerance resistor can cost several dollars even in thousands, against fractions of a cent for an ordinary one.[264][1318][m9tza_c4sxc]

## Tolerance versus temperature coefficient

Absolute tolerance and temperature coefficient are separate specifications, and which one matters depends on whether the circuit can be trimmed or calibrated. Where a trimmer or a software calibration exists, initial tolerance is nearly irrelevant and tempco is everything: a shunt resistor 10% off nominal is perfectly usable provided its value does not move with temperature, mechanical stress or time, because the offset can be calibrated out once and then stays out.[1693] The same logic explains a 1% part sitting in a high-end multimeter front end — it is there as a low-tempco resistor that the instrument software calibrates around.[485] Conversely, ranges with no trimmer must carry the tolerance in the part itself, which is why 0.1% resistors appear on a microamp range while the trimmed milliamp shunt beside them is only 1%.[853]

The extreme case of this is a nominally loose part chosen entirely for its stability. Prema instrumentation used resistors specified at only 1%, but with practically zero tempco and zero drift, reportedly costing a couple of hundred dollars each, in a unit whose overall drift is 1 ppm.[613] Circa-trim IRC 10K and 5K parts marked ±5% appear in temperature-stabilised assemblies for the same reason.[1126] Where ordinary resistors are used with a trimpot in place of precision parts, tempco is the thing that has to be watched, because trimming corrects the value once and does nothing about how it moves afterwards.[400]

## Grades, values and prices

A 0.1% resistor is cheap enough to specify as a baseline: an entire design can be built to roughly 0.1% simply by buying 0.1% parts wherever the tolerance matters.[72] A 0.01% part can be had from a distributor for a few dollars.[735] Reels of 10 ohm 0.05%, 10 ppm parts exist but are not always in stock, and committing to a production run has meant ordering a thousand of them when no other source in the world had them.[562]

At the top of the practical range, a Vishay 10 k resistor sells for around $20 and is specified at ±0.005% (50 ppm) absolute at 25 °C, ±0.2 ppm/°C tempco, with a further 0.005% or 50 ppm of drift over its load life; the matching 1 k part is 0.01%.[374][461] Bench-grade reference resistors of this class are typically kept as a 10 k at 0.005%/50 ppm and a 1 k at 0.01%/100 ppm.[249] Genrad decade box elements have been found marked to 0.008%, which on a 10 k element amounts to 0.8 ohms.[461]

Not every position needs the best grade. In a $35,000 programmer, 0.5% parts are adequate where the requirement is only that a rail land within a tenth of a percent or so.[1060] In a resistance chain, the top decades carry 0.01% parts while the bottom decades are only 1%, since a low-order decade contributes too little to the total to justify the tolerance.[1476] The same grading appears in a DC voltage standard, where the top elements are 0.005% and the first decade is 0.02%, each one trimmed to its actual value.[210]

## Low-value shunts

The genuinely hard part to buy is a low-value current shunt at a tight tolerance. A 10 milliohm shunt at 0.1% costs upwards of $4 per resistor even in volumes of thousands, and getting anything better than typical 1% tolerance in a low-value shunt is difficult and expensive to source at all.[853][1318][72] One workaround is to lay out ten half-watt footprints in parallel and rely on the statistical distribution of the individual parts to give a combination tolerance better than that of any single resistor.[232]

Where the budget allows, specialist manufacturers will sell a 0.01% 10 milliohm resistor, and such a part is normally used four-terminal, with the two inner pins tapping the resistive element itself so that lead and solder-joint resistance falls outside the measurement.[1693] Four-terminal 0.1 ohm 0.1% shunts appear in older instrument front ends.[658] A four-terminal 1.25 ohm 0.02% part is good enough to give a clean Kelvin measurement even when the circuit around it is built on a breadboard.[577]

## Setting a current or a voltage

The canonical use is a precision voltage reference driven across a precision resistor, which by Ohm's law produces a precise current: 1.25 V across 1.25 ohms gives exactly 1 amp, and the accuracy of the source is the error budget of just those two parts.[567][577] The same pairing turns a bench voltage reference into a current standard cheaply enough to be a practical way of checking a multimeter.[735] A precision 10 K resistor used this way, with 10 V developed across it, has been measured to land within 0.008% of target.[579]

Such a circuit still has to be analysed for current that does not flow through the resistor. In one 1 amp source, a precise 1.25 V really was present across a precise 1.25 ohm resistor, and 1 amp really did flow through it, yet a separate 2 milliamp error current returning to ground meant the load did not see 1 amp.[577] Thermal management matters as well: the precision resistor in that source does not strictly require a heat sink, but fitting a small one as a matter of course is sound practice.[577]

References below about 1.2 V cannot be made directly, because band gap physics sets a floor, so a lower reference is produced by dividing a higher one down with 0.1% resistors and buffering the tap with an op-amp follower — or by dividing with ordinary resistors and trimming, at the cost of having to watch the tempco.[400] Where a divider is fed from panel-meter-grade circuitry, ordinary 1% resistors are enough to introduce a 16 mV offset that has to be trimmed out with an added series part before the display reads zero for zero input.[102] Avoiding ranging altogether sidesteps a good deal of this: a fixed 10 V range removes the need for precision ceramic divider networks.[1248] A strain gauge bridge, by contrast, cannot avoid it, needing a high-stability voltage reference and high-stability bridge resistors — junky ones will not do — feeding a high-precision differential amplifier.[722]

Precision parts occasionally appear outside instrumentation for identification rather than measurement, as in the centre-pin resistor of a laptop power adapter, where the value must be right for the machine to accept the supply.[1279]

## Inside instruments

A resistance calibrator such as the Fluke 5450A is, functionally, nothing more than a set of precision resistors in a box with automation around them; it measures nothing itself, and provides two- or four-terminal connection.[544] Its resistors carry Fluke part numbers and appear to have been made in-house, including a 40.5 k at 0.005%, and the technology behind manufacturing them is as much of the design as the analog section that switches them.[544] Recreating such an instrument today would change only the sourcing — the custom Fluke resistors would be ordered from a distributor at $20 each — while the terminal compensation, the low-leakage relays and the rest of the analog design would be essentially unchanged, with the low-range elements possibly still hand-wound to reach the required performance.[545]

Decade resistance boxes follow the same pattern. A commercial decade box rates each internal element at 0.5 W, which rises to a maximum of 2.5 W when several are dialled in series, and specifies accuracy as ±0.1% plus a fixed 0.036 ohm offset present even at a dialled setting of zero.[211] Genrad elements are individually wound and hand-tweaked at the end of manufacture.[461] In a Wheatstone bridge instrument, the dial readings are only meaningful because the arm resistors are precise and individually trimmable; once the bridge is calibrated the device under test is read straight off the dials at balance.[1476]

Kelvin-Varley and Kelvin dividers put a constraint on the lowest resistance in the chain: keeping the smallest element at 1 K rather than something much lower means switch contact resistance is nowhere near large enough to matter, so the divider can be manufactured without worrying about contact resistance at all.[210]

Analog multimeters are built almost entirely from precision resistors, in some cases hand-wired directly onto the range switch with no PCB at all, each resistor in its own moulded spacer with walls between.[634] Their values are not E12 or E96 series but obscure figures calculated specifically for the required range dividers, and grades around 0.5% with very low ageing are what gives such a meter its accuracy.[634][1013] Old reference-resistor assemblies of around half a percent appear on turret and panel-board construction of the same era.[1097]

Thick-film hybrid precision resistor networks are the mark of a high-accuracy digital meter's front end; their absence is expected in a budget instrument and notable in one claiming 0.025% class accuracy.[344][712] A discrete thick-film hybrid precision resistor of around 20 megaohms is an unusual thing to find where no hybrid network is otherwise present.[173] Thin-film precision networks work in essentially the same way as an ordinary 0805 thin-film surface mount resistor, with a serpentine trace pattern visible internally, and a discrete precision Vishay part is manufactured very similarly.[730] Manufacturing them by the older method meant coating a ceramic body with metal powder and cutting a helix of metallisation with a micro lathe, reliably reaching ±0.1%.[BUW6h88weXU] In 1968 Standard Electric Lorenz of Stuttgart required 5,000 precision resistors of 1/20 watt, 1 kilohm, 0.1% tolerance for a spacecraft, ordered through TRW and manufactured by the Ingstrom Corporation of Van Nuys, California.[BUW6h88weXU]

Improvements in off-the-shelf parts have removed one historical reason for analog ASICs: matching of resistors and switch resistance that once required a custom IC can now be bought as a 0.01% resistor and a modern switch.[1032]

## Bench standards and calibration

A small set of precision resistors, typically a 10 k and a 1 k, functions as a working resistance standard for checking meters, and a homemade box containing two such parts of 0.01% and 0.005% class serves the purpose adequately.[461][374] Keithley's recommended set for generating currents from an applied test voltage is 10K, 10 meg and 100 meg, mounted inside an earth-shielded box.[406] Held against such a standard, a good handheld meter reads spot on to nearly the least significant digit, and holds that reading with the meter heated to 38 °C and beyond in a thermal oven.[249] The same standard resistors are the routine check for repaired and reviewed instruments, and for testing whether a meter survives mains voltage applied to its ohms range.[876][556][1424][252] They are also a diagnostic tool: connecting a known precision 10 k to a suspect meter and getting readings that change when the range switch is fiddled — including impossible negative ohms — localises the fault to the switch contacts rather than the analog circuitry.[777]

Precision resistors used this way, together with a voltage standard, are what allows manufactured units to be cross-checked against each other and programmed pass-fail limits to be set for production test.[Ux7WdK6oym4][576] On an automated test fixture the expense is not a concern, since only one such board is ever built, so a real expensive voltage reference and real expensive precision resistors are the obvious choice.[552]

## Measuring and buying them

Verifying a low-value precision resistor demands more care than the resistor's own tolerance. For a 0.1% part of 10 ohms, the tolerance band is 0.01 ohms, whereas ordinary test leads contribute around 0.29 ohms of residual resistance — so lead resistance must be zeroed out before the reading means anything.[133] A precision part carries a marking with the extra digit of resolution its tolerance implies, such as 10R0 on a 10 ohm part.[133] Confirming that the right part is installed can also be done functionally, by driving a known current in and checking the output: 99.7 nA out for 99.9 nA in is not a 0.1% verification but is enough to show the correct value resistor is fitted.[133]

In procurement, precision resistors are the parts to watch. They are not sold at fractions of a cent, so partial reels have to be re-reeled by the distributor to avoid buying a full one — and re-reeled components cause their own feeder problems on a pick-and-place machine.[264] Assemblers normally ask for a few percent of extra parts to cover pick-and-place losses, which is trivial for jelly-bean passives but not for loose precision resistors at $4 or $5 a piece in quantities of a couple of thousand; it is worth asking the assembler to be extra careful with the expensive parts specifically.[m9tza_c4sxc] The same arithmetic applies to package choice, since a 5–10% wastage allowance on a dollar-apiece high-precision low-tempco resistor in a very small package adds materially to assembly cost.[1307] In hand assembly, the precision parts are placed first along with the other small components.[562]

Where the tolerance is not needed, it is not spent: instrument boards routinely mix precision resistors with ordinary carbon parts in the positions where tempco and precision are not critical.[649][1060]
