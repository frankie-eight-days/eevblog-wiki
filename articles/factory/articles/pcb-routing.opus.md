# pcb routing

PCB routing is the stage of board design in which the electrical connections implied by a schematic are realised as physical copper traces between pads.[974] It follows component placement and is inseparable from it: the rats nest lines that show which pin must reach which pin are the tool for judging a placement before a single track is drawn, and dragging parts around while watching those lines is how a designer decides whether a layout is routable at all.[974] The word carries a second, mechanical meaning in PCB manufacture — a spinning router bit cutting a board profile out of a panel — and the two senses run through the same body of practice.[939][127]

## Directional routing

The foundational technique for a double-sided board is to commit each layer to one direction: predominantly horizontal traces on one side, predominantly vertical on the other.[1535][974] The reason is capacity, not aesthetics. Traces running in arbitrary directions on both layers exhaust the available space quickly and force a large number of vias to escape the resulting deadlocks.[1535] Routing a large, densely populated digital board on two layers is only practical this way — one horizontal, one vertical.[1313] Attempting it without that discipline fails fast: "If you try going willy-nilly, you're gonna come a-gutser in your routing pretty quick."[1313]

The convention is visible in essentially every well-executed double-sided digital board of the through-hole era. Old computer and instrument boards show all top-side traces running vertically and all bottom-side traces horizontally, a signature of two-layer digital design of the period.[452][955][438][1060] Multi-layer boards of the same vintage extend the idea layer by layer, each successive layer running perpendicular to the one above.[523][536] The same alternating scheme appears even in solderless prototyping products, where pads run vertical in one region and horizontal in another so that connections can be made by bridging with solder blobs.[860]

Within that framework the working preference is to route as much as possible on one layer — typically the top — and drop to the bottom or to an inner layer only when forced.[974][1323] Vias jumping down to lower layers are what component placement should be chosen to avoid in the first place.[1323] Inner layers in particular should not be used for signals unless there is no alternative, because they are better dedicated to solid power and ground planes for signal integrity and EMC reasons.[1323] Running power traces across a layer where they are not needed kills routing room and leaves no flexibility, forcing exactly those unwanted excursions to internal layers.[1323]

## Routing space as a scarce resource

Routing space behaves like a budget that is spent by every other decision on the board. Slots or cutouts in a PCB destroy it outright, because power, data and everything else must then be routed around the obstruction.[934] A large electrically isolated heat-sink area devoted to a single device consumes area that a dense design cannot spare, whereas using the ground plane for the same thermal job changes the routing density entirely.[1347] Fine-pitch parts impose their own floor: a 0.5 mm pitch BGA can require a six-layer minimum PCB simply to escape its pins, and a fully populated FPGA can need eight layers to fan its balls out, leaving only a thin sliver of interior area for bypass capacitors.[514][384] A device with pins only around its perimeter leaves the middle free for bypass caps and a ground fill instead.[384] Where the layer count is too low to fan out cleanly, bypass capacitors get pushed out of the way of the routing paths and away from the pins they serve, which is not an optimal layout even though it works.[464]

When space runs out, the standard responses are structural. Rotating a package 45 degrees allows signals to escape a quad flat pack on a height-constrained board only 0.8 mm thick, where a square-mounted chip could not be escaped at all.[722] Blind vias free the area beneath them so traces can pass straight under rather than detouring, without chopping up ground planes.[1259] Pin swapping on a microcontroller or FPGA reassigns signals to pins that suit the geometry, easing a route that otherwise cannot be completed.[1449] Placement itself is a routing decision: moving a serial expansion connector from a board corner to the middle of the board next to its chip avoided dragging ten traces across the board and ripping up most of an existing corner layout.[245] Memory placed on the underside of a board directly opposite the chip it serves is chosen for routing reasons.[587][1662] An LCD controller can be placed physically under the LCD so its pins fan out to the display's pins, instead of dragging the whole set of LCD lines across the board to a microcontroller sited elsewhere for its ADCs.[1055]

## The iterative rip-up pass

Routing is not a single forward pass. A recurring stage of any layout is going back through finished work to tidy traces, eliminate redundant paths and free up routing space — dragging a via to open a clearance, slimming a footprint's pads so four traces fit between unused pads at a 15 thou clearance, discovering that one of three bottom-side traces can be eliminated entirely by snaking it around.[974] The work is exploratory: trying some routing to see whether an arrangement will work, and ripping it up if it does not, is normal practice.[974] The mental model is chess-like, requiring the designer to see many components ahead to where everything is going.[974]

Ordering matters, because whatever is routed first is implicitly prioritised. Two long bus traces spanning every chip could have been laid first, but doing so would have prioritised them over the dozen-plus traces per device that need to escape each package — so the long spans are deliberately left until later and the space freed up for them afterwards.[974] The exception is timing-critical work: high-speed memory buses and similar should be prioritised when routing out a PCB.[1247] A modest double-sided board of this kind represents on the order of four or five hours of work.[974]

## Trace geometry and craft

Tracks should snap to the centre of pads, and snap grids are changed constantly during a layout because no single grid suits every task — 0.05 mm is one useful setting.[1353] Traces should leave a pad from its centre, then break to 45 degrees, rather than emerging from the side or at an odd angle.[1365] The origin of the rule is the acid or etching trap, which is no longer a real manufacturing issue; it survives as a mark of professional layout.[1365] Right-angle corners in signal traces, especially traces that terminate on the middle of a pin's edge and then turn, are the recognisable signature of machine routing that no designer would produce by hand.[960][536][1060] Curved traces appear occasionally, sometimes mixed inconsistently with 45-degree segments on the same board.[491] Where traces run in tight bends on flexible circuits, they are deliberately offset rather than stacked one directly above the other, because stacking creates mechanical problems in a tight bend.[1262]

Copper planes must be pulled back from the board edge. If plane copper on inner layers runs right to the edge where the board will be routed, sawn or sliced out, the cutting operation raises burrs in the copper that can short adjacent layers together — a particular hazard on multi-layer stack-ups where inner planes are separated only by very thin prepreg.[Ep4r-wD7PPs] Ground planes should likewise never be split; a region left un-flooded under a trace forces the return current to take a longer path and wrecks the timing budget and signal integrity of the signal above it.[1247]

## Propagation delay and high-speed routing

A signal's delay along a trace is set by propagation delay, which does not change with frequency, and depends on how the signal is routed — a trace on the top layer over a ground plane is a microstrip, and one on an inner layer is a stripline, which costs additional layers.[1247][1530] A microstrip tuned to roughly 50 ohms gives about 50 picoseconds per centimetre.[1247] The consequence is quantifiable: a data line taking a 15 cm longer path than its siblings incurs about 1 ns of extra delay, which against a 100 MHz DDR memory clocking on both edges — 10 ns per cycle, so 5 ns per transfer — consumes a fifth of the available window.[1247] This is what drives serpentine traces and trace length matching, and matching every trace in a large controlled-impedance differential-pair interface is a very substantial routing effort.[1247][720]

PCB traces are transmission lines, and at higher frequencies the return current stops spreading across the ground plane and concentrates beneath the trace.[1439] Controlled-impedance differential pairs constrain placement as well as routing: feeding a programmable gain amplifier's differential output to a more distant ADC is possible but forces another channel's pair to cross over it.[1530]

## Autorouting

Autorouters are usable but not trusted with a whole board. Turning one loose on every net including power and ground produces ground traces everywhere and a mess; the productive use is selective — highlighting a memory section or one repeated functional block and letting the router integrate it, or completing individual nets that were left unrouted.[975][598] A repeated block can also be routed once by hand and the route duplicated, though how easily depends on the CAD tool.[598]

Compared against a manual route of the same board under the same conditions, autorouted output follows the broad directional rules but loses on obvious opportunities that a human spots at a glance, and takes real time to run on a fast machine.[975] AI-based autorouting shows the same characteristic failures, taking traces through the middle of pin fields where a human would carry them around the outside, and the manual result reads as visibly cleaner.[1535] Boards that were autorouted stay identifiable decades later by their right angles, their lack of any tidy-up pass, and traces that no beginner would draw that way.[536][960] Conversely, a board that looks clean, tight and consistently laid out in rows is recognisable as human work, and a good double-sided layout is difficult enough that a well-optimised one is worth remarking on.[598][645]

## Routing as a manufacturing operation

In fabrication, routing is the CNC cutting of board outlines with a router bit, and it is one of the two ways boards are separated from a panel; the other is V-grooving, in which blades score a V into the top and bottom faces leaving a thin web that can be snapped.[939][127] V-scoring is cheaper, easier and faster than routing.[939] Routing gives a beautifully clean, smooth edge with no burrs, whereas a snapped V-groove edge is sharp and not completely flat — the routed portion of a mixed board is "smooth as a baby's butt" where the V-grooved portion is rough.[127] Routing is also the only option for non-rectangular outlines, so the two are commonly combined on one panel: routed profiles for the odd-shaped portions, V-grooves along the straight edges so the board still snaps out easily.[127][1158][939]

A routing path is specified by the designer on a mechanical layer of the PCB file, drawn as an outline of the slot to be cut and annotated with a note telling the bare board manufacturer to route it out.[239][552][974] Fabricators are sufficiently used to this that no further explanation is normally needed.[552] The standard routing bit width is 2.4 mm, and a slot drawn at that width can be cut in a single pass; any width can be produced, but a single pass is preferable.[127][239][552] Routing lines are extended past the board edge so that the drill runs all the way out and yields a clean, sharp corner.[239] Tabs are left between board and panel so the assembled boards can be snipped out with side cutters — commonly four tabs around the perimeter.[239][241] Where a board is not panelised at all, it can be supplied loose or individually routed, and the fully routed edges can be rounded off for a clean finish, which is why a wrist-worn board with exposed edges was ordered that way rather than V-grooved and filed.[127][644] A fully routed board with no breakout tabs or V-grooving anywhere implies a custom jig to carry it through pick-and-place.[465] Panel snapping is itself a hazard to assembled parts, since flexing a scored panel puts stress on the board and can crack ceramic capacitors.[1037]

Routing paths also do work inside the board outline. A half-moon castellated edge connection is made by placing a plated through hole on the board edge and routing across it, so the router shaves off half the hole at the end of manufacture — an approach that can leave shorting burrs if done carelessly.[1681][668] A routed slot placed between connector pins is a way of physically separating contacts that must not leak to one another, in the extreme by leaving pins unconnected and cutting a large slot where they would have been.[1222] A board can also be routed so that a connector reaches the enclosure edge without paying for a larger rectangular outline.[291] Repairs use the same operation in reverse: badly burned laminate between two points cannot simply be gouged to half depth and must be routed out completely.[1036] In-house board routing machines exist for prototype work, LPKF being the benchmark manufacturer.[2vJ0c0ioAXY]

Panels must also carry tooling holes, around 4 mm being an industry-standard size, and fiducial marks, alongside whichever breakout method is chosen.[127] The full set of routing, V-scoring, plated through holes, solder mask, silkscreen and gold-flash finish is what distinguishes a professionally fabricated board from a home-etched one, and is available cheaply enough from prototype services that home fabrication is rarely worth it.[939]
