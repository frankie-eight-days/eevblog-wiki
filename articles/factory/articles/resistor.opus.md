# resistor

A resistor is a two-terminal passive component whose sole electrical function is to oppose the flow of current, and it is the most ubiquitous part in electronics — the component whose value, tolerance, power rating and placement decide the behaviour of almost every circuit around it.[27aG9xhfk6s][1180] Unlike a capacitor or an inductor it stores no energy: it dissipates power as heat while voltage is applied, and the instant the voltage is removed there is nothing left in it.[1406] That simplicity is deceptive, because nearly every practical design problem — setting a current, dividing a voltage, sensing a load, consolidating a bill of materials, keeping a part from cooking itself — comes back to choosing a resistor.

## Fundamental behaviour

The resistance value determines how many electrons can get through, and the voltage across the resistor determines how hard they are pushed; a higher voltage across the same resistor forces more current through it.[27aG9xhfk6s] The voltage across a resistor is a measure of the energy difference between the electrons entering and those leaving, so increasing either the current or the value raises the voltage drop.[27aG9xhfk6s] This is Ohm's law, and it is the relationship on which everything else rests.[27aG9xhfk6s] Resistors are not polarity sensitive and can be fitted either way round,[aQ2AVLs8_7k][1163] although in a simulator a resistor still has an assigned direction, which determines the sign of the current reported through it.[516]

An ideal resistor across an AC source produces no phase relationship between voltage and current — the resistive part of an impedance is its real component.[1730] Real resistors are not ideal: a resistor has a small amount of inductance in it, just as an inductor has capacitance between its windings, and there is no such thing as a purely resistive or purely reactive circuit.[1728] Resistors also generate thermal Johnson noise, and the higher the resistor value the more thermal noise it contributes — a term that sits outside the input noise of the op amp it surrounds.[528] Resistance is temperature dependent in ways that matter at extremes: a 1.5 kΩ resistor can rise to roughly 20 kΩ at 0.01 K above absolute zero, a property exploited to make simple chip thermometers for dilution refrigerators.[1594]

## Tolerance and value distribution

A data sheet states a tolerance — ±1% for a typical metal film part — but says nothing about how values are distributed inside that band.[215] Measured in bulk, the spread of a nominally 1% batch follows a Gaussian distribution centred on the nominal value: most parts sit within about ±0.1%, a fair number reach ±0.2%, and by ±0.5% there is almost nothing left, with the odds of finding a part out at 0.8% bordering on zero.[215][216] A batch of 1,000 resistors plotted against percentage deviation showed a total spread of only about ±0.6% against a ±1% specification.[216] This behaviour is characteristic of a reputable manufacturer's process and cannot be assumed of cheap unbranded parts.[215]

A common beginner error is to assume tolerances add arithmetically: putting four 100 Ω ±1% resistors in series does not give 400 Ω ±4%.[212] Because the individual deviations are randomly distributed about the nominal, series and parallel combinations tend to average out rather than accumulate.[212] Precision parts do exist for the cases that need them — 0.05% resistors are ordinary purchasable stock,[m9tza_c4sxc][1279] and 10 kΩ 0.005% 50 ppm and 1 kΩ 0.01% 100 ppm parts serve as bench references for checking a multimeter's absolute accuracy.[249]

## Power dissipation and derating

Power in a resistor is I²R, and the resulting temperature rise above ambient is set by the part's thermal resistance, which for a given package size is roughly the same regardless of resistance value.[212] A resistor cannot be made to dissipate its rated power without its temperature rising.[212]

Every resistor data sheet carries a derating curve.[191] A quarter-watt part is rated at its full nominal power only up to a maximum ambient of 70 °C; above that the permissible dissipation falls linearly to zero at the part's limit temperature, 155 °C in one typical case.[191] The 70 °C figure is ambient temperature, not the resistor's own temperature rise.[191] Some data sheets additionally give a thermal resistance graph relating dissipated power to temperature rise above ambient.[191][212]

Lead length matters. The PCB itself acts as a heat sink for a through-hole resistor, drawing heat out of the resistor core through the leads, so trimming the leads close to the board improves dissipation; with 25–30 mm of lead length the effect largely disappears and the heat stays in the core.[191] This heating can be used deliberately: passing current through a resistor with a cord tied around it weakens the cord until it breaks, which is enough to trigger a mechanical release.[191]

Getting the wattage wrong is a common and destructive mistake. Discharging a cell through a 10 Ω resistor demands close to 0.9 W, which a quarter-watt part cannot survive.[RFb3TwWzza0] Choosing a resistor an order of magnitude too low in value can put it far outside its rating, cook it until it cracks, and take it open circuit — after which the same failure repeats on the next power-up.[1423] Spreading the load helps: ten parallel resistors of half a watt each give 5 W of dissipation capability in a current shunt, so the shunt barely warms and its value barely drifts with temperature.[232] Splitting a shunt into several resistors also gives it a fault-survival margin, so a software error that dumps the full output current through it does not immediately blow it.[232] Where higher dissipation or voltage rating is wanted in surface mount, the larger 1206 package is used for exactly that reason.[1598]

## Choosing values

The E12 and E96 series constrain what is actually available. An LM317 feedback calculation that lands on 360 Ω is served by an E12 330 Ω plus a 33 Ω in series, giving 363 Ω — near enough for the purpose.[158] A required gain that produces an awkward resistor ratio is a good reason to restructure a design rather than hunt for exotic E96 values; a gain of ten is easy with E12 parts, an arbitrary ratio is not.[572] A resistive scope probe built around a 1 kΩ resistor gives an inconvenient 21:1 division ratio that no standard scope attenuation setting matches, whereas an E96 value just over 950 Ω yields a usable 20:1.[1718]

Many circuits do not need precision at all. An order-of-magnitude approach is sufficient for an LED dropper: with a 10 V supply and a target current somewhere between 1 mA and 10 mA, any value between 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ will light the LED, so a resistor can be pulled from the parts bin without a calculator.[286] Where the calculation does matter it is straightforward — 1.6 V across the dropper at 250 mA gives 6.4 Ω, rounded to the nearest E12 value of 6.8 Ω, with the wattage checked by I²R.[RT3godBXkOg]

## Bill-of-materials consolidation

Each distinct resistor value on a board is another reel, another slot in the pick-and-place machine and another BOM line, so it can be cheaper and better to use more resistors of a value already in use than to introduce a new one.[1307][127] Two 10 kΩ resistors in parallel substitute for a 5 kΩ; two in series substitute for a 20 kΩ.[1307][127] A 15 kΩ pull-up is rarely worth its own line item if 10 kΩ is already loaded.[127] The stated ideal of this practice — the holy grail of electronics design — is a board using one resistor value and one capacitor value throughout.[1307] The same logic drives jellybean choices: because the LM317 data sheet's nominal fixed leg is 240 Ω, keeping 240 Ω permanently loaded on the machine and reusing it elsewhere is preferable to adding a nearby 220 Ω.[1438] The habit extends to hand assembly, where an 820 Ω part can serve as an LED dropper specifically to avoid depleting stock of the more useful 1 kΩ.[1306] For bench stock, sorting by E12 value into drawers — all the decades of 10, then 12, 15, 18, 22 and so on together — keeps 10 Ω, 100 Ω, 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ in one place.[104]

## Roles in circuits

**Current limiting.** An LED is a current-driven device and needs a series resistor to limit its current, without which it will draw excessive power and be destroyed.[27aG9xhfk6s] The same applies to seven-segment displays, where omitting the resistor destroys segments outright.[s2KkgI-kyK0] A design that drives multiple segments from a shift register without a dropper per segment can exceed the driver chip's maximum current, and the fix costs only five resistors.[1491] Where LEDs are paralleled, matched droppers share current well: 82 Ω per LED at about 20.8 mA produces the expected 1.7 V across each resistor, confirming even sharing.[392]

**Voltage division.** In its most basic form a resistive divider is two resistors, with an output always some fraction of the input, and dividers of this kind are everywhere.[1399] Dividers set comparator thresholds and Schmitt trigger hysteresis, where 9 kΩ and 1 kΩ against ±10 V rails give convenient round threshold numbers.[941] A Wheatstone bridge is nothing more than four resistors forming two dividers, with a voltmeter or electrometer across the centre.[1476]

**Setting a parameter.** Regulator and reference output voltages are set by a resistor ratio,[158][1438] and some regulators set output voltage with a single resistor, lowering parts count relative to the traditional two-resistor approach.[1] Battery charger ICs commonly set charge current with one resistor via a formula given in the data sheet — 25 mA for a 50 mAh cell charged at half C, for example.[176] Such values can be genuinely critical: a 1.1 kΩ part calculated from a high-voltage supply's formula to give 160 V cannot be casually substituted with a 1 kΩ, which is the kind of choice that warrants an engineering note on the schematic.[952]

**Sensing.** A resistor in series with a supply converts current pulses into a measurable voltage; 10 Ω is chosen to be high enough that CPU current pulses produce tens or hundreds of millivolts rather than microvolts, but low enough not to drop the rail.[762] Multimeter current ranges work the same way, with range switch contacts shorting out shunt sections so that a 1 Ω resistor becomes the shunt on a 50 mA range, giving 50 mV full scale.[853][929] Shunt values are trimmed by paralleling — a 1 kΩ across a 1 Ω to bring it slightly under nominal[853] — or built up from parallel parts, as with two 6R2s giving 3.1 Ω to set roughly 400 mA of charge current.[812] Burden voltage is the unavoidable consequence: even a 1 Ω shunt drops 100 mV at 100 mA, and a 100 Ω shunt drops 10 V, which will collapse a 3.3 V rail entirely.[1604][1190]

**Mechanical and layout roles.** Through-hole resistors are 0.4 inches across and can be used to jump four, five or six traces at once, which is a significant routing aid on a double-sided board and something surface mount gives up.[244] Zero-ohm resistors are jumper links, plated straight through, and boards have been seen packed with them purely to avoid the cost of a double-sided PCB.[LAbWsqS51Tk] Optional fit-or-omit resistors combined with solder bridges give configurable microcontroller pin routing on a prototyping board.[1580]

## High-value and high-voltage resistors

High resistance values solve problems that no other component can. A high-voltage differential probe is not isolated; its safety comes from a series resistor chain — two 1 MΩ resistors end to end, then another 1 MΩ, then a further element across the isolation slot, giving four megohms in each lead.[932] A multimeter can detect a lead plugged into the amps jack by tapping off a chain of three 3 MΩ resistors.[1704] Substituting ten SMD resistors in series-parallel for a proper high-voltage input protection resistor is poor practice, since the SMD parts are not rated for the job.[1734] At the extreme, femtoammeter input ranges use resistors of 10 GΩ, 1 GΩ, 100 MΩ, 10 MΩ and 1 MΩ,[1755] and a condenser microphone capsule may be biased through a 5 GΩ resistor.[629] Very high-value resistors are also relatively expensive.[629] An antistatic wrist strap's 1 MΩ safety discharge resistor is normally built into the lead rather than the strap.[768]

High-value resistors also allow low-power design: a comparator with 25 nA input bias current draws effectively nothing from 100 kΩ dividers.[471] Where a time constant is needed, choosing a 10 MΩ resistor and a small capacitor is generally preferable to a low-value resistor and a large capacitor, because resistors are more versatile in a BOM and cheaper than high-value capacitors.[471]

## Measurement in circuit

Measuring resistance in circuit is unreliable and is a classic beginner trap: anything in parallel with the resistor corrupts the reading, and a 10 kΩ resistor can read 3.6 kΩ simply because the meter's test current finds another path.[1622][1636] Even an unpowered circuit is not safe, because the meter's terminal voltage may turn on semiconductor junctions; some meters offer a low-ohms range that keeps terminal voltage under about 0.6 V for this reason, a feature common on 1980s instruments.[1622] Two resistors that measure 131 kΩ in circuit against a nominal 539 kΩ each demonstrate the problem rather than a fault.[1726] The reading is trustworthy when the surrounding parts are genuinely out of circuit — with the IC removed and the relevant pins floating, a 100 kΩ integration resistor can be measured directly and read spot on.[777]

Repeatability of the measurement setup matters too. Probing a nominal 1 kΩ resistor should give the same reading under light pressure at any point along the leads; a variation of six counts between successive readings of the same part indicates a contact problem, not a component problem.[216] Contact resistance is itself measurable — a rotary range switch nominally around 0.9 Ω can climb to about 10 Ω over cycling.[u2-ot2vWLxI]

## Failure modes

Resistors are among the more reliable components, but they do fail. A one megohm carbon film part reading 170 kΩ is remarkable enough to warrant suspecting a missed PCB trace before condemning the resistor — and in that case a right-angle trace hidden under the body was the actual explanation.[1245] Real failures include corrosion: conductive residue on a board can eat through one lead until the resistor drops out of circuit entirely.[1072] Overheating takes a part open circuit.[1423] Fusible input resistors are fitted deliberately to fail — checking one that measures 10 Ω and is intact rules it out as the fault.[1511] Interaction failures are subtler: 150 µA of leakage in a tantalum capacitor combined with a 1 MΩ resistor is enough to hold a node at mid-rail and break a power-on reset.[1245] Older assemblies can fail by losing internal contact altogether, giving no linear response at all across several resistors that should read a few kilohms.[638]

## Classic problems

Two puzzles recur in electronics education. The infinite resistor problem asks for the resistance measured across one resistor in an infinite two-dimensional grid of identical 1 Ω resistors.[25] The resistor cube asks for the resistance between opposite corners of a cube built from twelve identical resistors.[1472] The cube yields to symmetry: certain nodes sit at identical potential, so the resistors between them carry no current — measured currents of 197 nA and 51 nA through 4.7 kΩ parts confirm this experimentally, and in an ideal circuit the current is exactly zero.[1472] Those resistors can therefore be either shorted or removed, whichever is more convenient, without changing the answer; physically snipping them out makes no difference to the circuit resistance.[1472] The arrangement is essentially a balanced Wheatstone bridge.[1472] Textbook networks of a few resistors and voltage sources are likewise the standard vehicle for teaching nodal and mesh analysis and Kirchhoff's laws.[820][819]
