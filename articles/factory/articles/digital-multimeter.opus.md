# digital multimeter

A digital multimeter (DMM) is a single instrument containing several instruments at once, designed to measure multiple electrical parameters — it has the functions of a voltmeter measuring both AC and DC, an ohmmeter measuring resistance, and an ammeter.[1636] It is the default measuring tool of electronics work, made in every form from pocket-sized units through the sort an electrician carries to the large benchtop instruments found in a higher-end lab, at prices spanning roughly $2 to $700 or $800 for a large advanced brand-name model.[1636] Regardless of type they are fundamentally similar in construction and operation.[1636] Beyond hand-held and bench instruments, the DMM function is routinely embedded in other equipment: oscilloscopes carry a DVM alongside cursors, counters and mask testing,[CMoBGGqojqs][474] portable scopes are sold with a meter built in,[358][D2PANd9Hu3U][ByUiOk00K0U] and integrated benches include a 5.5-digit multimeter as one of several instruments.[876]

## Display, counts and resolution

A typical meter has an LCD display, usually four digits on lower-end units and possibly five on higher-end ones.[1636] The figure quoted is the count, which is simply the maximum value the reading can go up to.[1636] A 6,000-count meter reads to 6,000, or 6.000 on a lower range; a 3,200-count meter measuring the same 3.1 V or 3.2 V shows the same number of digits, but at 3.3 V it ranges up and a digit of resolution is lost.[1636] More counts is therefore better.[1636] Most meters carry a rotary selection dial to choose between functions — the origin of the name multimeter — although some substitute front-panel push buttons; a real range switch has the practical advantage that its off position is unambiguous.[1636]

Resolution scales upward through the bench classes. Five-and-a-half digits is about all that is wanted in a mid-range integrated instrument;[876] on a dedicated bench DMM, five and a half digits is regarded as modest and six and a half the expected step up.[1686] At the top, a 7.5-digit instrument such as the Keithley DMM7510 combines an 18-bit converter, a million samples per second update rate and 14 ppm accuracy for around $4,000.[731] Resolution of that order is what makes fourth-decimal-place work possible: total changes of barely 100 microvolts in a cell's terminal voltage are visible on a 6.5-digit meter, and more than 6.5 digits are available in its trend chart mode.[515] Designing a 6.5- or even an 8.5-digit DMM requires some of the best engineering in the world.[1032]

## Input impedance and circuit loading

A digital multimeter does not have infinite input impedance.[286] The standard figure is a nominal 10 megohms on most voltage ranges, AC or DC,[1067][1747] and this is what nearly every meter presents.[286][72][515] Connecting the meter across a circuit node therefore places that resistance in parallel with whatever is under test, exactly as an analog movement does, only with far less effect.[1067]

The consequence is quantifiable. Ten megohms across a 1 megohm resistor causes a measurement error whose size depends on the rest of the network; with a 100 k source resistance the error is roughly 1%.[286] Measuring the midpoint of a matched 200 k divider fed with precisely 10 V gives a reading below the expected 5 V, though for that source impedance the error remains small.[1067] The error grows with source resistance, so high-impedance circuits are where loading genuinely ruins a measurement and produces false readings.[1747][286] The same arithmetic governs circuits designed to drive a meter: 100 ohms in series with a 10 megohm input gives negligible error, and the meter impedance has to fall to about 100 k before that 100 ohms contributes even about 0.1%.[72]

Better meters, both bench units and some hand-helds, offer a second input impedance: a high-Z mode alongside the standard 10 megohms, which visibly reduces the load — the voltage on a 9 V alkaline cell rises when the meter switches out of 10 megohm mode.[515] In one uncommon direction the low impedance of an analog meter is the useful property and the DMM's high impedance is the trap.[1747]

## Accuracy and its limits

Basic DC volts accuracy is the headline specification. An ordinary 3.5-digit meter should be at least 0.5% accurate, which is sufficient for tasks such as setting a lithium-ion charge voltage of 4.2 V to within the ±1% the chemistry demands.[919] Mid-range hand-helds do better: a compact 6,000-count meter specified at 0.2% basic DC volts plus three counts sits in that class,[432] a vintage Gossen Metrawatt claims 0.25%,[745] and a 1980s bench instrument such as the HP 3478A offers 5.5 digits with high stability, to the point that used examples can generally be expected to still meet their specifications.[2] Because the millivolt range is usually the most accurate range on a digital multimeter as well as the easiest to use, instrumentation designed to be read out on a meter is often scaled to land on it — for example targeting the 200 mV range of a 2,000-count meter.[72]

Specified accuracy is not the whole error budget. Temperature affects the meter more than it affects a good resistance standard: across a 4 °C change, a hand-selected aged standard shifted by about 0.0002 while the meter reading shifted by roughly 0.0008, four times greater, with the excess attributable to the meter or the interconnects rather than the resistor.[834] Meters also drift over decades; an old manual-ranging unit can be found 2.6% out on one voltage range and 1.4% out on current while its resistance function remains respectable, sometimes correctable with an internal trim pot.[775]

Current measurement introduces a thermal error of its own. A DMM measures current by using its voltmeter to read the drop across an internal shunt; after carrying high current for a period the shunt warms, its resistance increases, the voltage across it rises, and the displayed current creeps upward even though the actual current has not changed.[gqzZHbEfWDU]

There are measurements a DMM simply cannot make. Its resistance and diode functions apply only two or three volts to the circuit, so an insulation punch-through that only occurs at high voltage — such as a fault putting mains onto the heat sink of an LED tube — cannot be found with a multimeter and requires a megohmmeter instead.[362]

## Displacement of the analog meter

Digital meters beat analog ones in almost every aspect, and the honest way to structure the comparison is to enumerate where the digital is better first.[1067] The digital instrument states the voltage, resistance or current directly, whereas an analog meter requires interpreting the needle position and first zeroing the movement mechanically.[1067]

Resolution is the starkest gap: even a 3.5-digit 2,000-count display beats any analog meter, and even a $2 digital meter beats the resolution of a movement.[1067] The needle itself has a width, and a full sweep contains perhaps a couple of hundred needle widths at best, so the last figure is a guess.[1067] Analog scales are also not provided for every switch position, so a 25 V range must be read off the 250 V scale with the last digit dropped.[1067]

Accuracy follows. A classic analog meter specifies about 2% for DC volts, worse than practically the worst cheap digital meter on the market, and a better example such as the Triplett 630-NA manages only 1.5% on DC, degrading to around 3% on AC.[1067] Analog input impedance is not a fixed 10 megohms but varies with range as an ohms-per-volt figure — 20,000 ohms per volt DC means 20 k on the 1 V range and 200 k on the 10 V range — which is a trap and one of the major reasons digital meters took over.[1399][1067] Only at the 500 V and 1,000 V ranges does such a meter reach the 10 to 20 megohms a digital meter offers everywhere.[1067]

The traditional defence of the analog meter is needle response to fast-changing signals, and modern fast-updating bar graphs answer it: Fluke were among the first with a fast-responding bar graph designed precisely to replicate and beat the analog movement.[1067] A digital meter's bar graph tracks a 10 Hz and even a 20 Hz signal visibly, and at 5 Hz with a 1 V RMS signal the excursions are plainly readable — behaviour not obtainable on the analog meter.[1067] The other analog party trick, gauging capacitance from the decay of the needle, is superseded by a dedicated capacitance mode that is easier and more accurate and requires no calculation from a decay factor.[1067] Combined with ohms measurement, which analog meters are poor at, the digital meter wins on accuracy, price, resolution and usability.[1067]

The high input impedance that distinguishes the DMM was historically achieved by other means. A VTVM used its tubes to obtain a high input impedance comparable to today's 10 megohm digital meters; in the transition away from tubes in the 1970s and 1980s the FET VOM substituted field-effect transistors for the valves to get the same active high-impedance front end, at the cost of needing a battery or mains supply.[1013] FET analog meters accordingly do reach 10 megohms.[1399]

## Historical development

The first commercial DMM was made in 1953 by Non-Linear Systems, using grain-of-wheat bulbs and edge-lit lucite digits.[986] Early instruments were coarse by later standards: one of the first digital multimeters, advertised at the end of 1970, offered 17 ranges at 0.1% with no LED display at its price point,[416] and a 1978 advertisement touted 1% accuracy, 12 separate ranges and a three-digit 0-to-999 display, not even three and a half digits.[142] A period construction-project digital voltmeter used state-of-the-art LED displays and the Fairchild 3814 LSI, equivalent to 3,000 transistors, for a 4.5-digit readout with only three DC voltage ranges.[416]

Analog meters remained predominant in retail advertising in 1985, when the cheapest digital alternative was $59.50 against $10 for a technician's 10 k ohms-per-volt analog cheapy.[416] By 1990 digital multimeters had come to dominate, and analog meters were relegated to legacy sales.[416] True RMS measurement was a notable capability well before it was commonplace, and its absence was what construction projects for true RMS AC voltmeters existed to remedy.[142] Fully manual-ranging meters were common into the 1980s;[775] most digital multimeters are auto-ranging today, while manual-ranging digital meters remain available.[1067]

## Functions and interfaces

Beyond the core voltage, resistance and current functions, mid-range hand-helds add temperature, a 60 mV DC range, microamps, a crest or min-max capture mode, and in some cases a built-in electric field detector for locating live wires and power points.[432] Min-max capture has become common enough that other instruments describe their own V min and V max readings by analogy with a multimeter's min-max mode.[1402] Continuity with a fast, latching beeper is a basic expectation,[D2PANd9Hu3U] and dedicated jigs exist to exercise it, feeding pulses of adjustable mark-space ratio into a meter to see whether it beeps.[1681] Almost all meters include an automatic power-off timer of a few minutes.[1636]

True RMS versus average-responding is a standing product distinction. The Fluke 70 series were average-responding meters, and the line has effectively been discontinued in favour of the 170 series — the 175, 177 and 179 — leaving the 70 series to legacy customers.[1448] The difference can be small in hardware terms: an average-responding Fluke 77-IV can be converted to a true RMS 177 for around ten cents.[1448]

Digital interfaces exist but are not always convenient. Brymen's 6,000-count meters publish an RS232 protocol that, rather than emitting the reading as a numeric string, outputs the LCD digit segments and maps them, requiring the host to decode the display.[5v8qX9PrjnA] The meter form factor has also been extended into other measurement types, including LCR tweezers built as an all-in-one instrument with a USB port.[1375]
