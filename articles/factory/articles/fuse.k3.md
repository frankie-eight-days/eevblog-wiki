# fuse

A fuse is a sacrificial overcurrent protection device: a calibrated metallic element placed in series with a circuit that heats by I²R dissipation (V²/R across the element) until its metallurgy gives way and the element melts, interrupting the circuit.[1677] It is the basic last line of defence against fire and equipment damage — an unfused fault on a mains-fed transformer can pump kilowatts into a shorted winding indefinitely, where a correctly placed fuse removes the power before anything catches fire.[1152] Fuses are designed primarily to clear gross overloads and short circuits rather than subtle fault conditions; a mains-rated fuse will not necessarily blow on a high-impedance fault such as water ingress, which is typically not low-impedance enough to clear it.[1602][1164]

## Operating principle

A fuse is not a precision switch that opens at its printed rating. The element has thermal mass, so it takes time to heat up and time to blow — "Fuses don't blow instantly. They take seconds to blow."[373][376] There is a thermal delay mechanism plus manufacturing tolerance, so a fuse must be over its rated current for a significant amount of time before opening; "It just doesn't magically blow it instantly at 2.5 amps. That's not how fuses work."[1164] High-speed footage of fuse elements blowing shows the wire melting, momentarily re-solidifying, and solder running along the wire as it cools, with smoke escaping from solder and flux residue at the end caps.[857]

The relationship between overload magnitude and clearing time is published by manufacturers as a time–current characteristic curve, and replacement fuses should be checked against these curves for suitability in a given application.[376]

## Unpredictability at low overloads

Fuses are predictable under substantial overload or short circuit but erratic under mild overload: "Fuses behave in predictable ways when subjected to substantial overloads or short circuits, but low overloads exist in a less predictable realm."[1377] Manufacturer application data quantifies the spread: "110% of rating of a mini automotive fuse will open somewhere between 100 hours and never. At 135% of rating, the fuse opening time is between 0.75 seconds and 10 minutes."[1377] Currents between 110% and 135% of rating are a recognised design problem because they subject the element to high heat for extended periods while its behaviour remains difficult to predict, and the fuse may not blow before damage occurs downstream.[1377] Depending on type and tolerance, a fuse run slightly above its rating can blow in seconds, tens of seconds, minutes, or tens of minutes — or never.[1377][1676]

Standards define only coarse boundaries. IEC 60127 and UL 248 govern miniature fuses; under IEC 60127-4, "Fuses must not open in less than 1 hour at 125% of the rated current and must open within 2 minutes at 200% of the rated current," and opening times at 135% range across types from 0.75 seconds to 30 minutes.[1377] Endurance requirements also exist — a fuse must survive, for example, 100 cycles at 1.05 times rated current and one hour at 1.15 times rated current.[1377] Manufacturers commonly decline to characterise the low-overload region at all: some datasheet curves start at twice nominal current, and at least one major manufacturer states "We don't provide any data below four times the nominal current."[1377] Bench measurement confirms the gap: a 400 mA fuse run at twice its rating survived more than five minutes while creeping toward 100 °C, where the datasheet curve implied roughly one second.[1377]

Characteristic curves are not stable over time. Fuse manufacturers continually tweak their proprietary metallurgy and wire-processing, so datasheets for the same model only four years apart (2020 versus 2024) can show crossing, differently sloped curves.[1677] Ambient temperature derating further shifts both blowing time and carrying current.[1377]

## Construction and types

The two broad construction classes encountered in electronics work are glass-cartridge fuses and ceramic high rupture capacity (HRC) fuses.[598] HRC fuses are the required type for serious measurement and mains work; quality gear uses "HRC ones too, none of this glass fuse rubbish."[725] High-quality multimeters specify 1000 V-rated HRC fuses with high breaking capacity — for example 10 kA on a 450 mA range fuse and 20 kA on the high-current fuse of one CAT IV 600 V meter, and an 11 A, 1000 V, 20 kA breaking-capacity fuse in another.[832][1692][1447] Voltage rating and breaking capacity matter because a fuse clearing a high-energy fault must extinguish the resulting arc.

Form factors vary widely: M205 and 3AG glass cartridges, axial-leaded fuses, surface-mount fuses, ceramic-bodied mini-HRC types for connector pins (10 A, 125 V units paralleled across DIN 41612 pins), PCB-mount fuses soldered directly into the board, and even a fuse in a TO-92 transistor package (the ICPN10).[502][725][598][1496][1104] At the cheapest end, equipment may substitute carefully crafted fusible links in the input jacks for actual fuses, and a length of fine mod wire can act as an improvised fuse during bench repair.[1254][V0RWwSw96Sw] Resettable PTC poly switches serve a similar protective role but are a distinct, self-resetting device; where reversed batteries can deliver large currents, a poly switch avoids the service burden of a blown one-shot fuse.[330][1152] Electronic fuses also exist as complete products, such as a mains power cord containing a cut-off module.[1164]

## Resistance and burden voltage

A fuse is not a zero-impedance link. HRC fuses — particularly the 1000 V safety-rated types — have significant resistance, which can equal or exceed the current shunt resistor in a multimeter; a fuse may be 1 ohm or even 2 ohms or more.[929] Five sample fuses of one type measured a tightly grouped 0.49–0.50 ohm cold DC resistance by four-wire measurement, though the element's resistance rises as it heats.[1677] One multimeter fuse dropped 0.6 V at 400 mA including test leads.[1676]

This resistance is a direct contributor to multimeter burden voltage: the resistance between the current jacks is the sum of the internal fuse and the current shunt, and it can be significant enough to disturb the circuit under test — a 10 mA loop falling to 9.8 mA when the meter is inserted.[1604] In one multimeter design exercise, a 600 mA fuse of about 1 ohm imposed an irreducible 500 mV burden voltage on the 500 mA range, forcing the design toward a switched topology that routed lower ranges through the 11 A fuse instead.[929][931]

## Multimeter input protection

In a properly designed multimeter, the fuse sits in series with the current jacks, backed by a diode clamp network across the current shunt.[376][1449] In the Fluke 27 implementation, a diode bridge plus four diodes clamps the shunt voltage to a nominal 3.6 V; across a 5 ohm shunt that is only 2.5 W against a 4 W resistor rating, while the clamp forces many amps through the fuse so the fuse blows first.[376] The diodes need not be fast or exotic — ordinary slow 1N4007 types are adequate because the fuse takes time to blow anyway — but their surge rating matters: single half-sine surge ratings of 30 to 50 A guarantee the fuse clears before the diodes fail.[373][376] A multimeter without this kind of supplementary amps protection is considered poorly designed, and spare fuses should be kept in stock because blown meter fuses are routine.[373]

The current-range fuses protect only the current ranges: "These fuses have nothing at all to do with protecting the voltage jack. Common misconception out there."[373] Input-jack detection schemes in some meters exploit the split jack connected down to the fuse to sense probe placement.[1663]

Fuse quality is a fast proxy for meter quality: small glass fuses and an unfused 10 A jack are reasons to reject a meter outright, while HRC fuses indicate a well-designed, safe instrument — cheap meters routinely ship with "10 amp max, unfused" jacks or fusible links instead of fuses.[75][872][1254] Serviceability also varies: some meters allow fuse replacement from a dedicated compartment, while others require removing self-tapping screws and splitting the case.[60][1007][1447][894]

## Rating and selection pitfalls

A fuse rating far above the load's normal draw protects only against gross faults. A 2.5 A fuse on 240 V mains passes 600 W — adequate for gross shorts but incapable of catching a smouldering solder-joint failure, which was judged a badly specified fuse.[1164] Similarly, a 6 A primary fuse (1440 W) protecting a 750 W hot-air gun was double the element rating and failed to blow during a meltdown.[1171]

Conversely, ratings too close to the load cause nuisance blows: motors and similar loads produce current spikes well above the running current, and ordinary current measurement — even fast microcurrent gear — can miss transients short enough to take out a fuse unexpectedly.[HBLKxC4JzTA] Distorted mains current waveforms with poor power factor also matter: the I²R spike currents flow through the fuse and must be accounted for in the fusing design of a product and its distribution system.[1413] Some meter manufacturers deliberately fit a fuse rated below the displayed measurement range (a 400 mA fuse in a 600 mA meter), which works but means the fuse will eventually blow somewhere near full-scale use.[1377]

## Thermal shock and the "Jones effect"

Thermally shocking fuses by freezing them can permanently raise their current-carrying capability. Frozen 630 mA fuses held 1.45 A and 1.65 A indefinitely — currents that would blow an untreated fuse in under two seconds — and one survived 2 A for 53 seconds, while an untreated control fuse at 1.6 A blew almost instantly.[1677] The treated fuses showed permanently reduced voltage drop (around 0.68 V versus roughly 1 V at the test current) even after returning to room temperature, indicating a lasting change in the element alloy.[1677] The effect was dubbed the "Jones effect for fuses" in the absence of an existing name, with the explicit caveat that it is not a recommended technique, merely a possible last resort to squeeze a higher current rating from stock fuses.[1677]

## Repair practice

Fuse continuity is a standard first check in any dead equipment: a good fuse measures near zero ohms (0.1 ohm typical), while an open fuse reads overload or megohms.[905][1493][502] Blown fuses are a frequent single-point repair across consumer and laboratory equipment.[330][385][861] However, a blown fuse usually blew for a reason — a downstream short or failed component — so the protected rail should be checked for shorts to ground before replacement, since simply shorting or replacing the fuse into a fault can destroy the circuitry the fuse was protecting.[330][725] That said, fuses do occasionally fail on their own: manufacturing defects, inrush surges from the main filter capacitor, or age can take one out with no underlying fault.[8blgmbXfDEc][861] Where an exact replacement is unavailable, an axial fuse of the same rating can be bodged in temporarily, and a higher-value fuse can substitute briefly for testing purposes only.[631][502]

A multimeter set to a current range doubles as a fuse-protected shorting tool when deliberately pulling a node to ground, giving a direct readout of any overload current.[777] Fuse placement in product design extends beyond the obvious mains input: individual oscilloscope channels, feedback sense lines, parallel transistor banks, battery packs (three parallel fuses on a 65 V, 50 A pack), and three-phase inputs each warrant their own fusing.[1185][1412][1381][1499][814] Components bearing individual certification marks, fuses included, simplify overall product safety certification.[1082]
