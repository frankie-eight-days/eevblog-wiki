# voltage drop

Voltage drop is the potential difference that appears across any element when current flows through it, given by Ohm's law as the product of current and resistance.[1216][820] Resistors, semiconductor junctions and deliberate current shunts drop voltage by design, but so does every conductor that was supposed to be a perfect wire — test leads, binding posts, connector contacts, PCB traces, vias and even large copper planes are all non-zero resistance, and none of them can be assumed to be zero.[1216][1526] Because the drop scales with current, a resistance that is irrelevant at milliamps becomes a measurement error, a regulation failure or a reset at amps, which is why voltage drop is one of the most common causes of results that look like a fault in the device under test but are actually a fault in the setup.[774][1281]

## In circuit analysis

Resistors do not generate voltage; they drop it when current is passed through them, and the polarity of that drop follows the assumed current direction.[820] Kirchhoff's voltage law states the relationship directly: the sum of the applied voltages around a loop equals the sum of the voltage drops.[819] The sign convention is where the technique catches people out — a voltage source traversed from positive to negative behaves as a drop rather than a source, so whether a battery term enters an equation as positive or negative depends entirely on the arbitrary current direction chosen for that loop.[819][820] In mesh analysis the same element can appear as a drop in one loop equation and as a generated voltage in another, depending on which mesh current is being followed through it.[820]

## Test leads, connectors and remote sensing

The single most frequent practical manifestation is the drop between a power supply's output terminals and the actual load. A precision supply holding 5.000 V at its terminals delivers something noticeably lower after a couple of feet of cable, which is the entire reason bench supplies provide remote sense terminals.[649] With sense leads tapped at the load, the supply compensates for the lead drop and delivers the programmed voltage where it matters; without them, the front-panel readback and the load voltage diverge.[1691][1732]

The magnitudes are larger than intuition suggests, even with heavy cable. A supply set to 10 V shows about 50 mV of lead drop at 1 A, rising to roughly 430 mV at 10 A.[1691] On one laboratory supply, half its maximum current — 3 A — produced about 0.93 V at the load against 0.85 V measured after the wiring, with about 150 mV of lead drop at 6 A even with substantial leads, enough that a gauge increase would have been the correct choice for that current.[667] Drops of this order routinely explain readback mismatches: a supply displaying 1 V while the load sees 1.1 V is not miscalibrated, it is simply not remote sensing.[1293] The same appears on cheap and expensive hardware alike — output voltage falling short at maximum current is attributable to lead loss rather than poor regulation, confirmed by measuring directly at the terminals.[1265][1606][1298][1036][1030]

Small currents through short wires are not exempt. Moving a voltmeter tap from a battery terminal to a nearby input jack changes the reading measurably at 250 mA, purely from a short jumper and the contact resistance of a spring terminal, and the discrepancy vanishes when the current is turned down.[141] At the other extreme, a battery discharge test drawing about an amp through thick cable and massive binding posts still developed enough drop to produce a 40% error in the apparent result, while the instrument itself was working perfectly.[774] Lead drop is one of the standard traps of bench work: at 8 A through wimpy output leads, power is dissipated in the leads and the displayed output power is simply wrong.[895] Thin, unrated cable produces the same effect in the field — a dodgy unbranded USB cable dropped enough voltage that the processor it powered kept resetting and glitching, symptoms that would otherwise send a repair down entirely the wrong path.[1281]

Where the drop cannot be eliminated, it must be measured around. Four-terminal, or Kelvin, measurement senses voltage right at the point of interest with a high-impedance path carrying essentially no current, so no drop develops along the sense wires and the reading reflects only the element under test.[317][1732] Whether four-wire technique is necessary is a judgment call about magnitude: with a 10 MΩ multimeter load and milliohm interconnects, the calculated drop is negligible and shorting bars suffice; with a large load, sensing must move to the load itself.[ZYC763Vx9O8] Sense lines should be taken to the exact point being regulated — even the drop across a large flood-filled copper pour between the sense tap and the true output is a real error term.[667] Probing follows the same rule: input and output voltages of a regulator must be measured directly on its terminals, with the ground reference on the ground pin, or wiring and trace drops contaminate the result.[158][224] Efficiency measurement is a case where the drop must be actively fought — holding a fixed 5 V at a converter's input required trimming the supply to 5.31 V at 100 mA output and re-trimming whenever the load changed.[957]

## PCB traces, planes and distribution

On a board, voltage drop drives copper geometry. Trace resistance follows from width, copper weight and length, and calculators derive the resulting drop directly; the acceptable drop is an electrical design constraint that sits alongside the thermal current-rating constraint, and the two are separate issues.[1559] Vias have their own resistance — around 0.33 mΩ for a typical via — and the same tooling gives drop alongside cross-sectional area and thermal resistance.[543] Running thin traces to multiple high-current connectors produces too much drop; wide buses or a star arrangement are the alternatives, subject to available routing space.[934]

Modern low-core-voltage devices make the budget brutal. A large FPGA drawing 8 A through a 20 mm wide power trace develops about 80 mV of drop, and the ground return develops the same again, giving 160 mV against a data sheet limit of 125 mV — already a violation before the plane under the device is considered.[1216] Halving the effective length halves the drop, so a shorter run yields around 40 mV, with a further few millivolts of variation across the plane itself depending on how many vias break up the copper.[1216] Even a large ground plane is not zero resistance, and predicting the drop across one properly requires finite element analysis rather than a rule of thumb, since simple calculators for the problem are not accurate.[1216] Fitting a heavy jumper wire across a finished board is the visible signature of exactly this problem — someone lowering resistance to reduce the drop at a given current.[1216]

Distribution topology matters as much as copper area. Splitting power planes keeps the drop caused by a high-power section from disturbing a lower-power one.[1216] Star grounding prevents a chip's reference from riding on the drop produced by large switching currents flowing along a shared return, which would otherwise modulate that reference dynamically as the load switches.[1216] Where a couple of hundred milliamps of switching current shares copper with sensitive digital parts, the resulting ground and power bounce may only be tens of millivolts, but that is enough to push a marginal design over a threshold.[1365] Conversely, a ground region carrying no current develops no drop, so all the ADCs and DACs referenced to it sit at the same potential.[244] The same reasoning motivates multiple bulk capacitors in parallel fed by separate traces: splitting the current three ways cuts the drop in each trace proportionally, and a pulse load tied to shared copper will droop the rail feeding a neighbouring load that may not tolerate it.[742]

Because distribution drop is unavoidable, high-current rails have traditionally been trimmed high. A nominal 5 V rail set to 5.25 V puts circuitry near the connector at the top of tolerance and circuitry at the far end of a densely populated board near 4.75 V, with both ends still in spec.[1216] Trimming precisely to 5.000 V on a high-current system is therefore a mistake, since cable, connector and trace drops eat into the margin on the way to the boards.[905] The practice extends to large modular products, where a main switch-mode rail may be set to 5.3 V specifically to allow for the drop across the internal cabling.[725]

Fault finding on a powered plane exploits the drop rather than fighting it. Probing between adjacent points on a live board gave 13 mV from one ASIC to the next, 40 mV further along and 81 mV further still — real drops caused by devices legitimately drawing large current, which makes isolating an actual low-resistance fault on such a plane very difficult.[401]

## Semiconductor junctions

Diode forward drop is a design cost that has to be budgeted. A 1N4001 or 1N4148 drops around half a volt, while a Schottky diode — a metal–semiconductor junction rather than a PN junction — measures around 0.142 V, and that lower drop is why Schottky parts are used in switch-mode supplies where efficiency matters.[1747] Diodes and LEDs are non-linear in voltage drop, though LED light output per milliamp is close to linear.[1427] The measured drop also depends on how it is measured: forward biasing into a lower-value load draws more current and shows a larger drop.[1747]

A series diode is the cheap and simple way to protect a battery-powered product against reverse insertion, but it costs about 0.6 V, which is crippling on a 3 V battery — it is only usable if the drop can be afforded.[1449] Other elements have the same character: current can be fed into a microcontroller through the protection diode on an ordinary I/O pin, incurring a drop but still powering the chip.[831] A boost converter's output diode shows its drop against the input rail whenever the rest of the circuit draws current.[366] Multimeter input protection uses the effect deliberately: a bridge of four diodes clamps the voltage across the current shunt at a nominal 3.6 V, limiting what the shunt can develop before the fuse acts.[376] Within a bipolar transistor, most of the collector-to-base voltage is dropped across the thin, lightly doped, effectively high-resistance region rather than the low-resistance material.[748]

Junction drops also set operating points. In a tracking pre-regulator, the pre-regulator voltage is the constant current times the setting resistor plus the base-emitter drop, which makes the result temperature dependent through that junction.[329][260] An LED dropper resistor of 1 kΩ with 4.5 V across it passes 4.5 mA, more than enough for an indicator.[182] In a battery leakage tester, an excessive drop across the internal pass transistor prevented the discharge voltage from ever falling far enough to re-toggle the internal flip-flop.[1653] Nixie tubes present a related trap: unswitched pins do carry a drop through the tube, which tempts a designer into using lower-voltage driver transistors, but the open-circuit voltage measured at those pins shows that the drop cannot be relied on and drivers must withstand the full voltage.[950][RXk_wr0g8UM]

## Current measurement and burden voltage

Any current measurement that works by sensing voltage across a shunt is a deliberate voltage drop inserted into the circuit, and shunt value is a direct trade between resolution and intrusion. A 1 kΩ shunt is catastrophic — 100 mA through it demands 100 V that a 3.3 V supply cannot provide, collapsing the product's rail to zero; 100 Ω still calls for 10 V; 1 Ω gives a manageable 100 mV but sacrifices resolution at low currents.[1190] The reverse problem appears at the other end of the scale: a shunt sized for 100 A in an electricity meter produces roughly 200 µV at 1 A, which becomes difficult to measure at all.[409] A shunt suited to several hundred millivolts full scale runs into the converter's noise floor and the op-amp's offset — 25 µV in one case — at the bottom of its range.[1693] The practical target is a drop large enough to be visible above the noise but small enough not to disturb the circuit: a 10 Ω series resistor was chosen to turn CPU current pulses into tens or hundreds of millivolts on a scope rather than microvolts, while staying low enough that the supply rail did not collapse.[762]

In a bench supply's own current sensing, a 0.2 Ω shunt at 2 A dissipates only 0.8 W but produces a drop that may or may not be acceptable against the available headroom, whereas 0.1 Ω gives at most about 0.4 V.[232] Ballast resistors sharing current between parallel pass devices face the same limit — they cannot be made too large, since 10 mΩ each, 5 mΩ in parallel, adds 10 mV of output drop at 2 A.[222] Low-side sensing places the shunt in the ground return, so the circuit's ground reference is lifted by the drop — around 0.1 V at 1 A — which is why high-side sensing is generally preferred despite needing more circuitry.[87][222]

A multimeter's current ranges have the same problem under the name burden voltage. A good meter specified at 1.8 mV per milliamp develops 360 mV at 200 mA, which on a 5 V circuit is already enough to push it out of tolerance, and far worse on 3.3 V or 1.2 V rails.[2][i069j6S80OU] The effect is not theoretical: a development board measured through a meter's low current range failed to start up, its LEDs barely lighting, and ran correctly the moment the meter was moved to the amps jack with its far lower burden.[642] Burden voltage stacks with wiring drop whenever the meter and the probe point are not co-located, so measuring load voltage through a breadboard and a meter's current path compounds two errors at once.[224][324]

## Cables, connectors and mains-scale losses

At mains scale the accumulation of contact resistance becomes the dominant loss. A chain of 283 double adapters in series measured about 9.5 mΩ per section — wires and contacts together, with most of the resistance in the loosely sprung plug contacts rather than the copper — and delivering 7.31 A produced 228.1 V in against 187.65 V out, a drop dissipating 295.7 W, or roughly 1.045 W per adapter.[1526] The supply sagged to 202 V at a kilowatt and 182 V at two kilowatts, with a further drop across twenty-odd metres of extension cord ahead of the chain.[1526] The same mechanism appears in low-voltage portable power: a 12 V output measuring 10.2 V at a secondary connector points to substantial drop in the connector and leads rather than the battery, and wiggling a marginal connection visibly moves the reading.[lYKjScnkeq0][IXVTMUQGN5U] Whether such losses invalidate a test depends on what is being measured — watt-hour totals from a load that integrates at its own terminals remain valid even without four-wire sensing.[IXVTMUQGN5U]

Series elements in an AC distribution path drop voltage too: a fluorescent tube driver in the current path may account for 50 to 100 V out of 240 V available.[362] In phantom powering, the drop is the design mechanism — 10 mA drawn through a 3.3 kΩ feed resistor drops 33 V from the 48 V supply, leaving around 15 V at the microphone, which is ample for the analog circuitry.[616]

## Loads, sources and internal resistance

A source's own internal resistance drops voltage under load, which is why open-circuit battery measurements are misleading. Loaded and unloaded discharge curves separate by exactly the drop across the cell's internal resistance, so measuring open-circuit voltage when discussing discharge curves or cut-off voltages is fundamentally wrong.[779] A power supply's current limit can create the same effect artificially: setting a conservatively low limit lets an inrush surge push the supply into current limiting, which drops the voltage and upsets the converter being tested.[895] A three-terminal measurement suffers where any current flows through the wire shared between the sensing point and the return, so the shared conductor should be short and heavy.[141]

Series resistance intended for filtering is constrained the same way. A 100 Ω resistor in an RC ripple filter drops a full volt at only 10 mA of output current, which is unacceptable when a rail must stay near its nominal value.[1116] A capacitance-multiplier ripple filter drops around a volt across its pass transistor, and that drop varies with load, base resistor, transistor type and gain — it removes ripple but does not regulate.[1116] A linear regulator dropping 12 V input to 5 V output dissipates the 7 V difference times the full output current.[512] Elsewhere the drop is generated deliberately: an expanded-scale voltmeter uses a resistor sized to develop a specific drop — 3 V at the known bias current — to shift the input range.[204][221] A logic output driving current into a load develops a drop at its output resistance, which in a cascaded shift register can corrupt the data passed to the next device.[1491] Even a plain series resistor in a valve filament supply is worth checking: 1.2 V across an 8 Ω resistor explains both the reduced voltage downstream and the half-watt part rating chosen for it.[1394]
