# heat sink

A heat sink is a piece of metal attached to a dissipating component to disperse waste heat into the surrounding air, so that the component's junction stays at a reasonable operating temperature.[105] Virtually every electronic component turns some of the power passing through it into heat, and the heat sink is the element that decides whether that heat leaves the die fast enough to keep the part alive.[105] Its effect is quantified by a single number — thermal resistance in degrees Celsius per watt — which makes heat sink selection an arithmetic exercise rather than a matter of taste.[102][744]

## Thermal resistance and the design calculation

Semiconductor data sheets give thermal resistance from junction to case and from junction to ambient. A typical power transistor might specify 3.13 °C/W junction to case and 62.5 °C/W junction to ambient; the latter figure means that if the device is mounted free-standing on the board with no heat sink at all, dissipating one watt raises its junction 62.5 °C above ambient.[105] Heat sinks are specified the same way: a commercial extrusion rated at 4.5 °C/W will rise 4.5 °C above ambient for every watt put into it.[102] The elements stack in series exactly as resistances do in an electrical circuit, and the chain can be walked through with back-of-the-envelope arithmetic to arrive at a junction temperature, with airflow and geometry then adjusted until that number is acceptable.[105]

The method is accurate enough to be trusted. A dummy load dissipating 11 W into a 4.5 °C/W heat sink was predicted to sit about 50 °C above a 20 °C ambient, giving roughly 70 °C; the measured heat sink temperature was a couple of degrees over 70.[102] Data sheet figures are not always representative, however — one small regulator's heat sink measured about 4 °C/W where the nearest catalogue part was quoted at 15 °C/W, the difference attributable to larger fins and therefore greater surface area.[895] Because so many terms in the chain are estimates, sound practice is to add margin deliberately: take some real measurements, apply a fudge factor, and choose a heat sink twice as big as the calculation says is needed.[105]

Two traps recur. Data sheet dissipation ratings are usually quoted at 25 °C ambient, but the temperature that matters is the local one — if the heat sink has climbed to 60 °C, the device is derating against 60 °C, not against the 25 °C of the room.[222] And the thermal resistance figure for a bare heat sink assumes it is radiating into free air; forced air changes it completely, so the specification must match the actual installed condition.[744]

## Airflow

Heat sinks are not very efficient without air flow.[1309] The same 10 W into the same heat sink produced 76.3 °C in still air and 35.7 °C once air was blown across it, with the device case dropping from about 87 °C to about 47 °C.[105] A small regulator that survived comfortably with airflow across it cut out entirely when run as a free-air heat sink at the same dissipation.[895]

Fin orientation follows directly: fins must run parallel to the airflow, and heat sinks are routinely found with their fins deliberately aligned to the intake direction even where the fins are short and the available surface area is therefore limited.[1289][1396][1639] Fins turned across the flow simply block it.[1396] The same logic governs the whole enclosure. Intake vents placed low draw air across the board and up through the heat sinks before exhausting, which is more efficient than venting at the top.[864] Air can be channelled through an extrusion so that the whole flow is forced through the fin gaps rather than around them,[607] and formed aluminium ducting can be used to take air in from the sides, through the fans, over the main processor heat sink and out the back.[308][1280] Obstructions matter — an L bracket placed in the stream breaks up the flow,[790] boards stacked across a duct block it,[946] and blowing the exhaust of one CPU heat sink into a second one is poor practice.[726]

Fan-cooled equipment often runs its fan under temperature control, so a heat sink can counter-intuitively run cooler under load than at idle, because loading the outputs spins the fan up.[512][PcxEO3fA_Ls]

## The chassis as a heat sink

Using existing metalwork saves a component. A power device can be bolted or clamped to the side of a case, sometimes through a custom folded aluminium bracket with individual clamps and sil pad isolation,[261] and the practice of using the rear panel or an internal backing plate as the sink is long established.[1034][695] Variations include a bracket angled to push a switching transistor against the back panel,[788] a 7805 tied down to the chassis for the 5 V rail,[840] a series pass transistor mounted directly to the chassis,[1153] a soldering station's bottom case,[111] a die-cast speaker enclosure,[1156] and a brass centre block connecting to both the upper and lower cases.[236] A folded sheet heat sink that mates with and screws into the aluminium back panel shares its load with the chassis.[452] Aluminium plates and thermal pads can be moulded to contact every major heat-generating package on a board at once.[470] The approach has a hard limit: a plastic-backed case cannot be used as a heat sink at all, and an alloy-backed case would have allowed a larger passive solution in its place.[PcxEO3fA_Ls]

The PCB itself is a heat sink. Copper flood fill thermally relieved to a device's ground pin serves as one,[326] and placing a resistor's leads close to the board lets the copper pull heat out of the resistor body — though lead thermal resistance limits how much, and the relationship tapers off with lead length.[191] Relying on the bare board alone can fail, requiring a bodged-on heat sink at the production stage.[872]

For surface-mount parts, heat can be taken through thermal vias and a transfer bar to the case, which then acts as the heat sink; this is a professional solution for getting rid of a couple of watts, not for a hundred-watt amplifier.[744] Since every element in the chain adds thermal resistance, going straight from the case to the heat sink and eliminating the vias, sil pads and transfer bar gives a lower junction temperature.[744] The competing pressure is production cost: bolts, washers, heat bars and screwed-on heat sinks are all extra assembly operations, which is why surface-mount heat sinks exist, kept small by the limited suction force of pick-and-place heads.[1347] Where no catalogue part fits the volumetric constraints, a custom heat sink is neither difficult nor expensive at production runs of a couple of thousand.[1196]

## Mounting and interface

The joint between device and heat sink is part of the thermal path. Thermal pads and sil pads provide electrical isolation while maintaining coupling, and once devices are isolated at the board no further pad is needed between the transfer bar and the case.[744][470] A heat sink carrying isolated devices may be left at no potential at all, floating.[607] Clip-on heat sinks that slot into the board without any compound, stick-on heat sinks fixed with thermal adhesive, and thermal adhesive bonding LEDs or Peltier plates to aluminium are all in common use.[682][689][664][1343]

Thermal compound rewards attention. The single central blob of paste relies on the mating surface being flat; on a base with machining ridges, paste is caught in the grooves and can travel along them instead of spreading across the die.[MiqxUaWkGYY] A mounting that does not sit flush needs a great deal of paste to compensate and performs poorly for it.[648] Retention hardware is typically spring loaded and cannot easily be over-tightened, with the corners done up diagonally,[462] but paste can dry out and mountings can loosen with age, producing over-temperature shutdowns years later.[yKflC-fmSX8] Chips of differing height under one common heat sink are accommodated by machining raised lands into the sink to meet the shorter packages.[1289]

A heat sink can be given secondary duties: carrying the main current in a MOSFET bridge,[504] pressing shield cans down onto the board to eliminate a hand-solder step,[1717] or acting simultaneously as the display mounting bracket.[1477] Temperature sensing is usually a single TO-92 device clamped to one side of the extrusion, since a large aluminium heat sink has negligible internal thermal resistance and the other side will read the same.[862]

## Typical loads

Linear regulation is the classic case: a linear supply needs a big heat sink on the back and is wasteful for a given output power, the price paid for its noise and ripple performance.[166] Where two regulators share one heat sink, the dissipation shifts between them depending on whether the supply is in constant-current or constant-voltage mode.[221] Multiple channels sharing an extrusion interact — the shared channel gets hotter, and worst-case thermal testing means loading every channel on that sink at once.[828][440] Electronic loads put essentially all of their rated power into the sinks: 300 W into two large finned extrusions with fans drawing air through them,[281] or 250 W into a single heat sink carrying ten power transistors.[862] In an instrument combining several functions, the purely linear electronic load section carries the largest heat sinking.[1550] Switching topologies remove most of this: once the design goes switching, massive heat sinks are no longer required.[1513]

LEDs are mounted on heat sinks because they are not efficient and dissipate the majority of their input power as heat in the die.[1401] A COB module's data sheet may cap the aluminium PCB temperature at 85 °C, which becomes the design target for the heat sink behind it, and the extrusion is anodised for improved dissipation.[773] Home-built arrays follow the same arithmetic: eight LEDs putting about 12 W into an aluminium strip produced a 13 °C rise, implying roughly 1 °C/W for the strip.[50] Die temperature can be inferred from a measured heat sink temperature by adding the data sheet junction-to-solder-point resistance and the measured resistance from that point to the fins.[361] Where a panel has no aluminium backing and no thermally conductive adhesive, the only path out is the board copper.[916]

Digital parts are increasingly in the same territory. A single-board computer drawing about 3.7 W reached 90 °C on the processor, close to thermal shutdown, making a stick-on heat sink mandatory rather than optional.[883] Cluster builds glue a small heat sink to each board with thermal adhesive and rely on passive convection.[934] Elsewhere a heat sink built into the lid presses down onto the processor beneath.[858][2]

Thermoelectric generators invert the usual purpose: the heat sink is there to hold one plate anchored at ambient so that a temperature differential exists at all. Without an adequate sink on the cold side, both plates equalise and the generator produces nothing.[664] The thermal mass is bonded with thermal adhesive for good contact.[664] The same mass effect appears at the bench, where a large copper plane or a power transistor tab acts as a heat sink that pulls heat out of the soldering iron tip,[1064] clip-on heat sinks are placed on component legs to protect sensitive parts during soldering,[180] and a heat sink bonded with thermal adhesive to a chip presents such a thermal mass that removing the chip requires hot air with a proper nozzle.[405]

## Failure modes

Undersizing shows up immediately in temperature. A 5 W dissipation in one undersized regulator heat sink ran off the end of the manufacturer's own curve, producing roughly a 100 °C rise above ambient — over 90 °C measured through the case vents and well over 110 °C with the case off and no airflow.[512][549] The fix was a larger heat sink matched to the same board footprint, so no layout change was needed beyond moving the capacitors clear.[549] Deleting a heat sink entirely is worse than the margin might suggest: a 7805 found with its sil pad still fitted but its heat sink missing overheated within seconds of power-up.[621] A tiny surface-mount power brick with no heat sink at all reached die temperatures over 100 °C and tripped its own protection.[957]

The neighbours suffer too. Electrolytic capacitors sandwiched between two heat sinks are heated by them, and heat is what shortens electrolytic life,[52][1714] which makes the capacitor nearest a heat sink the first suspect in a failure.[1714] Orientation matters for the same reason — a board mounted so that heat rises past a capacitor is a bad design where the inverted arrangement would have been acceptable.[682] And when reading thermal images of an assembly, the hottest pixel is not necessarily on the heat sink at all; a cut-out in the extrusion can expose something else behind it.[pwS6HnM9PDo]
