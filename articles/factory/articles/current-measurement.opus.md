# current measurement

Current measurement is the third of the three basic multimeter functions, alongside voltage and resistance, and the only one of the three that cannot be performed by touching two probes to a live circuit.[75][1636] Because a meter measures the current flowing *through* itself, the circuit has to be opened and the instrument inserted in series — a link cut, a trace broken, a jumper pulled.[1636] That single structural fact drives almost everything else about the discipline: the burden voltage the meter inserts, the shunt resistors that set its accuracy, the separate input jacks and fuses, and the entire family of clamp, transformer and field-sensing techniques that exist to avoid breaking the circuit at all.

## The series constraint

A meter placed across a circuit reads voltage; there is no probing sequence that yields current in-circuit.[1636] To measure the current in a link, the link is cut and the probes placed either side of the gap; to measure it in a board trace, the trace is cut.[1636] The positive lead must also be moved to the dedicated current jack, which is why meters that measure current are usually identifiable by a third or fourth jack marked A, mA or µA.[1636] Some two-jack meters do measure current, but many pocket meters and basic instruments deliberately omit the function — the Fluke 101 has no current ranges at all, while the otherwise similar 107 adds a 10 A range.[1636][1574] Omitting current is a defensible choice for a general-purpose or household meter, since breaking a circuit is not something a casual user typically needs to do, and some pocket meters are better for leaving it out.[1636][1083][1362] One meter was advertised with a current capability that was subsequently withdrawn, leaving a 100 mA glass fuse guarding an input that cannot measure current at all.[1238]

The current position is also the dangerous one. A rotary switch turned to a current range and then applied to the mains shorts the supply through the meter's shunt.[94] Meters that omit current ranges entirely still have to survive mains voltage on every remaining range.[94] Instruments increasingly detect a lead plugged into a current jack while a voltage function is selected and warn about it, and some warn every time a current range is entered.[FgkD8K0Ssdc][249][1540]

## Burden voltage

Every multimeter on the market inserts a voltage drop when measuring current, developed across its shunt resistor and its fuse.[1604][931] The effect is easy to demonstrate: 1 V across a 100 Ω resistor gives 10 mA by Ohm's law, and the meter reads 10 mA — until the circuit is broken and the meter inserted in series, at which point the meter's own drop reduces the current actually flowing.[1604] The consequence is not merely an error in the reading; it can prevent the circuit from working. An attempt to measure the charge current of a fast NiMH charger in the 10 A range failed because the combined drop of the leads and the meter's burden voltage stopped the charger from charging at all.[812]

Burden voltage is therefore a specification worth reading. One handheld claims less than 40 mV on its 300 µA range and measures 30 mV in practice.[249] Redesigning the current front end to lower the drop across both the shunt and the fuse is a recognised design goal for a better multimeter.[931]

## Shunts, ranges and accuracy

Current is measured by developing a voltage across a shunt resistor, and accurate shunts are hard to make — particularly at high current.[75][1017] Meters consequently specify worse DC current accuracy on the 10 A range than on the milliamp and microamp ranges, with the microamp ranges usually the most accurate.[75] A current specification of one percent or one and a half percent is poor; the target is to get as close as possible to the meter's basic DC volts figure.[75] A Fluke 28 Series II reaches 0.05% plus one count on DC volts and 0.2% on both ohms and current.[64] A Fluke 45 specifies 0.05% on current, which is unusually good, though it lacks a microamp range.[791] Most meters do not do current particularly well.[791]

Shunts are physically trimmed as well as designed. In one meter, heavy copper bands are soldered at an exactly chosen point to set the value of the highest current shunt, with progressively thinner wire gauges forming the lower ranges.[1097] Older instruments use a ten-turn trimmer alongside the 10 A jack shunt.[866]

Which ranges an instrument carries matters more than its headline accuracy. A 60,000-count insulation-resistance multimeter with 0.09% accuracy and a 6 µA range has no 10 A jack and tops out around 600 mA, which is a showstopper for anyone doing high-current work.[894] A bench THD multimeter offers only 1 A, 100 mA and 10 mA ranges to a 3 A maximum, with no microamps.[478] A clamp-oriented handheld whose lowest current range is 60 mA is adequate for electrical work and inadequate for electronics.[973] A 6½-digit bench meter with milliamps as its lowest range gives only 0.1 µA resolution.[1189] The most damaging gaps are in the middle: a cheap handheld missing both the 100 mA and 10 mA ranges leaves 9 mA — an everyday electronics value — with essentially no resolution.[1095] Absent low ranges are felt too; a 600 µA range that reads about half a percent out is tolerable at a low price point, but the missing 60 µA range is not.[1731]

Range and jack handling is a common failure. Two related oscilloscope-multimeters read 115 mA and 110 mA respectively when fed 1 mA into the correctly chosen milliamp jack with the amps function selected, and one raised its 10 A over-range beeper while on the milliamp jack.[1597][1540] A bench supply reads a phantom 30 mA with nothing connected whenever its current limit is set below 10 mA.[512] Cheap process calibrators sourcing and measuring current are unreliable enough to be a lottery, one reading over 24 mA at maximum and another producing nonsense — including a negative reading — when sourcing 30 mA.[1257]

## Designing measurement into a product

Because current requires a broken circuit, well-designed boards provide the break in advance. Development boards commonly fit a jumper in the supply rail specifically for current measurement, so the jumper can be removed and a meter or a microcurrent adapter inserted to read the processor's consumption directly.[290][642] Vintage instruments do the same at the rail level: link wires along a power rail exist so they can be cut and the current on that rail measured during servicing, and jumper links with removable inserts allow currents to be measured and circuits broken into for debug.[208][1237] Where no provision exists it must be improvised — desoldering a series resistor and fitting a two-pin header, or tacking a pair of pins onto a pad so an ammeter can be clipped in.[413][330] The point of insertion has to be the real return path: measuring only 20 to 30 mA on a supply wire turned out to be an artefact of a ribbon cable carrying the return current, and breaking into the correct conductor revealed roughly 700 mA after boot.[512]

## Low currents and wide dynamic range

Low-current work is a distinct specialty. The microcurrent adapter uses a MAX4239 chopper amplifier to keep offset low, and outputs a scaled voltage — 1 mV per mA on one range — so that an ordinary voltmeter reads current.[24][708] Bench instruments now reach comparably low: one bench supply design measures down to microamp output current with a circuit capped at 10.24 mA, switching to a coarser path above that, giving continuous coverage from a few microamps to a couple of amps.[232] A programmable supply offering 1 µA resolution on its 200 mA range devotes many display digits to current.[1402]

At the extreme, electrometer-class instruments convert current to voltage across very large fixed resistors — a 250 GΩ resistor serves a 2 pA range — with the input node kept off the PCB and routed directly to standoffs to control leakage.[1017] Such gear reaches down toward attoamps and is expensive enough that its owners describe metrology as a path they choose not to go down.[1017][s2KkgI-kyK0] A femtoammeter of this class ships with its current input disabled by default and must be enabled in the connection settings before it measures anything.[1755]

Dynamic range, not resolution on any single range, is the design driver. In a combined voltage-and-current instrument the voltage channel can be served by an 8- or 10-bit converter because nobody cares about the last digit there; the current channel is what needs the high-resolution converter spread across eight ranges.[1190] Residual offset is the other limit: a power meter shows about 1.8 µA of residual offset on its 5 mA range, the consequence of a deliberate choice to prioritise a low noise floor over low offset in the input amplifier.[1693]

## Product consumption and battery life

Estimating battery life rests on measuring current in each of a product's operating modes — sleep, wake and active — rather than on long-term endurance testing, which is impractical for products rated for hundreds of hours.[1331] Datasheet figures should be checked: a device specified at 8 milliamps measured 5 milliamps in practice.[1533] Purpose-built profilers both supply the product and measure it, sourcing up to 500 mA through screw terminals so no separate supply is needed, with a trigger input for correlating measurement with product events.[1331] A battery simulator displays voltage and current together, captures pulse current, and integrates current over a settable interval.[1005] Cell capacity measurement ideally uses a four-terminal arrangement with the cell voltage taken differentially into one amplifier and the current into another, both logged; a three-terminal meter with the current section inserted in the cell's negative lead is the compromise, driving a constant-current load of, for example, 250 mA.[141]

Routine measurements of this kind are quick: a display module drawing 4.25 mA, most of it the LED; a meter drawing 0.3 mA on DC volts; an upgraded instrument display pulling roughly 150 mA.[413][1393][1669] A fault investigation distinguished 1.25 mA at power-on from about half a milliamp in sleep.[f-LTv1GqCMw]

## Measuring without breaking the circuit

Where the circuit cannot be opened, current is sensed through its magnetic field. A non-contact probe of this type covers milliamps up to the amps range with a 5 MHz bandwidth and can display current in a PCB track, with a toroid adapter supplied for conventional clamp-style measurements.[207][296] Its limits are worth stating plainly: ±2.5 mT maximum field, equivalent to 2000 A/m, and ±10 mA to ±10 A at ±5% accuracy through the toroid, with a nominal 1 A per volt output.[296] Accuracy in track mode depends on the user's own calibration.[296] Such a probe is the practical way to view isolated current waveforms on live equipment.[400]

Field-sensing outputs are voltages, so the scope must be scaled deliberately: the probe is set to ×1 because the output is direct, the manual consulted for the scale factor, and the reading converted — 240 mV at 1 A per volt is 240 mA, 230 mV at 1 V per amp is 230 mA.[1252][915] Most digital scopes can be told to display the channel in amps directly rather than leaving the conversion to the operator.[1252]

Current transformers serve the same purpose permanently installed. A UPS passes a single mains wire through a small transformer to get an isolated tap on the input current, with a second transformer on the output, so that input and output power can be computed alongside the tapped voltages.[504] A three-phase smart meter passes each phase through its own sense element and measures each phase voltage relative to neutral.[409] An EVSE uses a current transformer or Hall-effect sensor for its current channel, giving it enough information for real and apparent power even though its display shows only voltage and current.[1507] Household energy monitors use clamps to read solar production, house consumption and grid export separately — and these clamps carry a direction arrow that must be respected on installation, though a negative consumption reading is not by itself proof that a clamp is reversed.[1390] Clamp instruments have their own error modes: one exhibited an offset that tracked correctly with 3 A applied, identifying the fault as offset rather than sensor or amplifier failure.[Yk_T-uCbg10]

## Inferring current from a voltage

Much practical current measurement is a voltage measurement across a known resistance. Triac conduction is observed by scoping the voltage across the load resistor, which is proportional to the current through the device.[1172] Mains-referenced coil current is obtained by reading 218 V RMS across a 43 kΩ resistor and dividing, giving about 5 mA.[425] The leakage of MOV-protected meter inputs is characterised by putting a current meter in series with a 10 MΩ input impedance at 100 V, where 10 µA confirms the expected 10 MΩ, and watching the current rise faster than linearly as the applied voltage climbs toward 1000 V.[rT0g1QmKE5E] Heatsink work uses the same arithmetic in reverse: 10 V across a MOSFET at 1 A is 10 W dissipated.[105]

The same principle underpins LCR measurement. A resistor is placed in series with the device under test, the voltage and current and their relative phase are measured, and from those three quantities inductance, capacitance, reactance, series resistance, dissipation factor and quality factor all follow.[757][922]

Current also shapes what a voltage reading means. A diode does not sit at a constant 0.6 V drop; the diode curve gives low drops at low currents, so 0.2 V across a protection diode indicates a small processor current rather than a fault.[831]

## Transients

Averaged current is a poor guide to peak current. Motor drives produce spikes significantly higher than expected, so a circuit measured at about 1 A and protected with a 3 A fuse will trip unexpectedly.[HBLKxC4JzTA] Characterising current transients requires appropriate test gear rather than a handheld ammeter.[HBLKxC4JzTA] Charging circuits show the same behaviour: a 15-minute NiMH charger delivers roughly 7.6 A pulses rather than a steady current.[35] A cyclically loaded circuit reads 64, 88, 91 and 65 mA in succession as its phases interleave.[1653]

## Current in fault-finding

Measuring supply current is a cheap way to avoid chasing the wrong fault. Where all rails in an instrument sagged together, checking the current first ruled out the overload the service manual's troubleshooting guide would have pointed to, and identified a bridge rectifier instead.[804] Current draw serves as a power indicator on boards with no status LED.[1493] A microamp range in series establishes whether a suspect module draws anything at all, and whether what it draws is a residual capacitor charge or genuine operation.[1702] Watching for a current change while freezing a suspect component with inverted air duster is a standard test — a current that does not move disconfirms the theory.[401] Current mode also finds unexpected uses, such as forcing a pin low through the meter while observing the result.[1672]

Free-energy claims are tested the same way: measuring 12 mA through the load LEDs and about 24 mA at 1.5 V into the circuit gives roughly 36 mW input against the claimed output.[708]

## Verification and calibration

Current can be derived rather than sourced. Given a trustworthy voltage standard and a trustworthy resistance standard, current follows from Ohm's law with no separate current standard required.[374] In practice a constant-current source is used: 350 µA fed to several meters at once, or 50 mA, exposes the spread between them — one comparison found only three least significant digits of spread on current, tighter than expected, and another found about 5 counts across fourteen units.[99][852][1051] A source good to 30 mA is more than sufficient to calibrate a 4000-count meter.[1578] Verification catches both gross faults and marginal specifications: a bench meter that would not measure current at all turned out to have its input terminals physically detached, while a 1 mA reading on a 100 mA range that looked fine calculated out to 0.26% against a specification of 0.05% plus five counts.[1189][791]
