# lm317

The LM317 is an adjustable three-terminal linear voltage regulator, and one of the small handful of parts that qualify as true jellybean components — stocked as a matter of course, used everywhere, and worth knowing in detail.[1438][168] A single part number covers any output from 1.25 V to 37 V, set by two external resistors, which is why it earns a place in a kit where the fixed 78XX series would require a different part for every rail.[1438] It carries the same protection features as the fixed regulators — short-circuit current limiting, thermal overload protection, safe area compensation — at a nominal 1.5 A.[1438]

Its weaknesses are as well known as its strengths: it will not regulate below 1.25 V, it needs a minimum load to regulate at all, and it is emphatically not a low-dropout part.[221][1438][158]

## How it works

Internally there is no mystery and no real difference from a 7805: an error amplifier driving a series pass transistor, usually a Darlington pair, closed around a voltage reference through a feedback divider.[221][90] The fixed 78XX types build the divider into the chip; the LM317 brings the feedback out to an adjust pin so the divider sits outside, and the two work on exactly the same principle.[90]

The internal reference is 1.25 V, developed between the output and the adjust pin.[221][158] The classic configuration puts a fixed resistor R1 from output to adjust and an adjustable R2 from adjust to ground, giving the standard output formula; rearranged for design work, R2 equals V out divided by 1.25, minus one, multiplied by R1.[1438][158]

That reference voltage also explains the floor. The output cannot be commanded below 1.25 V, because the part can only hold the reference across R1 — and that limitation is what disqualifies it from a lab supply that must reach 0 V.[221]

### R1 and the adjust pin current

240 ohms is the nominal value for R1 in the datasheets, and it is a value worth standardising on: a design that uses several LM317s can share one 240 ohm part across the whole bill of materials, and that part can often be reused elsewhere in the circuit for parts consolidation.[1438] A 220 ohm is an equally serviceable substitute in practice.[158] The value should not be pushed far upward — beyond roughly 500 ohms the error terms become significant and the design will no longer meet the datasheet specs.[158]

The reason the value is low in the first place is the adjust pin's own error current, which flows through R2 and adds a term to the output equation.[158] Typical adjust pin current is 50 µA, but design calculations should use the worst case unless the exact part is known.[158] With 100 µA through 363 ohms the added error is about 36 mV, turning a nominal 3.3 V into 3.336 V — not serious for casual work, but something to be aware of when designing LM317 circuits.[158] The low divider values also serve a second purpose, discussed below: they supply the device's minimum load.[158][221]

## Minimum load current

The minimum load current needed to maintain regulation is the classic "big trap for young players" with this part.[1438] The device needs 3.5 mA minimum, which puts it on par with the 78XX series — the 78XX has no minimum load requirement but carries a higher quiescent current instead, so neither family suits micropower design.[1438]

In ordinary use the requirement is invisible, because the feedback divider is specified with values low enough to present the minimum load itself.[221] It becomes visible the moment the divider is removed. Driving the adjust pin directly from an external source leaves the output with no minimum load at all, and an explicit load resistor must be added to make the circuit stable — a 1K resistor serves on the bench.[221] Similarly, an application that genuinely draws only 1 mA needs a resistor on the output purely to establish the minimum current.[1438]

The output capacitor, by contrast, is not required for stability, though it is normally fitted as a matter of course.[1438]

## Dropout voltage

The LM317 is a standard linear regulator with a high dropout voltage, not an LDO.[158] The datasheet input-to-output differential is typically 3 V minimum — more than the 7805 requires.[1438] As a working rule of thumb the dropout is 1.5 to 2 V at full current, against perhaps 0.2 V for an LDO.[158]

Measurement bears the graphs out closely. Defining dropout as the input differential at which the output falls by 100 mV, a 3.3 V output at 20 mA and 25 °C junction temperature drops out at just over 1.5 V of differential; at 200 mA the same part measures 4.958 V in against 3.264 V out, or 1.69 V.[158] The transition is not clean — the part does not drop out as smoothly as one would like.[158]

The practical consequence appears whenever the LM317 is asked to make 3.3 V from a 5 V USB rail. Characterising such a design worst case means setting the input 5% below nominal, to 4.75 V, then raising the load current until the output falls 5% below 3.3 V.[158] A regulator run at room temperature on this margin gives roughly 150 to 160 mA of usable output before it leaves regulation.[158] An LDO in the same socket would deliver 500 mA without difficulty, needs no adjustment resistors if a fixed version is used, and requires only input and output caps.[158] Choosing the LM317 for a 5 V to 3.3 V conversion is, on those numbers, a gamble that trades most of the available current for the convenience of a part already in the junk box.[158]

## Constant current configuration

Shorting the adjust pin to the output and inserting a single resistor turns the regulator into a constant current source: the device holds its 1.25 V reference across that resistor, so the current is 1.25 V divided by the resistor value — 1.25 A for 1 ohm.[1438][221][992][1688] Both the LM317 and the 78XX family can be used this way, and the whole circuit amounts to the regulator, a resistor and a capacitor.[1438][992]

Grounding the output of such a current source and connecting the input to the supply under test converts it into a constant current dummy load for power supply and battery testing.[102] The configuration has real limits: the sense resistor must be a very low value and can only occupy a narrow range if the circuit is to stay stable.[102] At more modest currents the arrangement is unremarkable and works well — an LM317 current source dissipating about a watt is a routine solution.[992]

Because a linear regulator passes essentially all of its output current through from the input, the input current in such a load equals the output current, which makes the drain on a battery under test genuinely constant.[141]

## Use in adjustable and programmable supplies

The LM317 with a voltage knob and a current knob is the basis of hundreds of published bench supply designs, and a supply built from one plus a couple of knobs is among the classic self-build projects.[221][168] The device is robust, low noise, relatively stable, and works in both constant voltage and constant current configurations, which can be cascaded.[221]

It falls down when software control is wanted. Driving the adjust pin from an external source works — whatever voltage is fed into the adjust pin appears at the output plus 1.25 V, so 1.25 V in gives 2.51 V out and 5 V in gives about 6.25 V — but that fixed 1.25 V offset must be carried through every calculation, and it can be compensated in firmware without elegance.[221][500] The other objections are that the output cannot reach 0 V, and that the adjustment pot behaves poorly at the very bottom of its range, so setting an output current as low as 1 mA is not something the arrangement does well.[221] One route around the floor is to generate a negative rail so the 1.25 V offset can be pulled down to zero; the offset itself is at least fairly consistent.[500]

A design that needs to go lower must leave the part behind. A regulator with a 0.8 to 0.9 V minimum operating voltage and a single programming resistor is a better starting point than the LM317's 1.25 V and two-resistor divider, at the cost of relying on the load to supply the minimum current below that point.[222] The alternative used in a full custom design is to keep the topology — error amplifier plus series pass transistor — and replace the internal 1.25 V feedback path with circuitry that injects an external 0 to 1 V control signal.[221]

## Thermal behaviour and failure

Being a linear pass element, the LM317 dissipates the full input-to-output differential times the load current, and thermal design is where it is most often abused. Its operational temperature range extends to roughly 120 to 125 °C, and it has built-in thermal overload protection, so an overheated part shuts down rather than fails.[512]

The Rigol DP832 supplied its isolated 5 V logic rail from an LM317 that ran extremely hot — around 90 to 95 °C on the tab early on, with heat sink readings of 110 to 120 °C after ten minutes of operation, approaching a 100 °C rise above ambient.[512] The junction is hotter still, and must be computed from the datasheet's junction-to-case thermal resistance rather than guessed.[512] Running a regulator at that temperature to supply a nominal 5 V digital rail leaves no design margin whatsoever, and the resulting thermal shutdown was the identified cause of the unit resetting.[512] The part fitted also had a conspicuously thin TO-220 tab compared with a properly specified device.[512]

The revised board changed nothing but the LM317 supply and its heat sink, which was enlarged to a part that fitted the existing footprint; the fix brought the tab down to just over 70 °C in one measurement and just over 81 °C in another with no airflow, against well over 110 °C before.[549] The transformer tap was not changed, so a high mains voltage still delivers more input voltage into the regulator than is necessary — a lower tap would have been preferable.[549]

Similar arithmetic applies elsewhere. Tab temperatures around 70 °C on a TO-220 in a Stanford Research SR650, and 72.4 °C on the analog LM317, indicate no gross overload but poor thermal design in a chassis that should not have needed a fan at all.[621] Note also that a dropout figure quoted at 25 °C is a junction temperature, not an ambient one; serious characterisation requires a probe on the case, and even that understates the junction.[158]

## Relatives and equivalents

Several variants share the type number. The plain LM317 is the 1.5 A part; the LM317M is a 500 mA version; the LM317L is a low power version limited to 100 mA.[1438][660] The 117 designation denotes the military-qualified equivalent of the 317, and in a hermetically sealed welded ceramic package it can command hundreds of dollars against a jellybean's cents.[1435]

The 1117 is a different part despite the similar number — the jellybean low dropout regulator, available in fixed and adjustable versions.[1438] Its adjustable version has an identical pinout to the LM317 and an identical divider formula, so it can be treated as a low dropout equivalent of the LM317 or 7805.[1438][371] For negative rails the complementary part is the LM337, and split analog supplies are commonly built from a matched 317 and 337 pair.[621][391][538]

Second sourcing is not automatic even within the family. A new die released under a modified part number — for example a 317M variant qualified for automotive use — changes the specifications, and a regulator that has been requalified may drop out differently or oscillate with different loads and bypass capacitors than the legacy chip did.[1752]

## As a voltage reference

An LM317 trimmed to a chosen output can serve as a crude voltage reference, with initial accuracy in the region of 1 to 1.5%.[1438] That is sometimes good enough, but precision work — ADC references, comparator threshold detection — generally needs better than the couple of percent a voltage regulator offers, along with the temperature coefficient specification a regulator does not provide.[1438] The part can also be combined with a series pass transistor and a precision reference to build a more accurate fixed regulator.[1438]

## In practice

The LM317 turns up constantly in teardowns, typically as the low-current housekeeping regulator in an instrument whose main rails are handled elsewhere.[1438] Examples span a Rigol DSA815 spectrum analyser, whose TO-220 devices are simply LM317s and 337s; a Siglent oscilloscope where an LM317 is the only secondary-side regulation; an HP 35670A analyser using adjustable 317s and 337s alongside fixed 7800-series parts; a universal programmer; a low-cost process meter; a Rigol DP832; and a monitor board where it supplies the low-end rail.[391][1355][538][371][1248][511][1162] It also appears in retro equipment and in field bodges — one homemade modification mounted an LM317 with heatsink compound directly against a case used as a heatsink.[1724][839]

For anyone specifying a fixed 3.3 V rail, the sensible default is a parametric search on current, package, dissipation, price and availability, with the LM317 plus two resistors as the ready alternative when a suitable fixed part is not to hand.[400] Where the requirement is more demanding, better regulators exist, and a low dropout part is the right choice whenever the input-to-output margin is tight.[158]
