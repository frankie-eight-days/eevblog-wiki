# function generator

A function generator is a bench test instrument that produces standard electrical waveforms — sine, square, triangle, ramp, pulse, noise, and DC — at user-selected frequency, amplitude, and DC offset.[143][1724] It is one of the fundamental pieces of laboratory equipment, providing the stimulus half of stimulus-and-measure work: any bench that has an oscilloscope also needs a way of generating basic signals.[168][954] Entry-level capability starts with kit instruments costing as little as $15 that produce basic sine, square, and triangle waveforms by DDS, while high-performance arbitrary waveform generators can cost tens of thousands of dollars.[954][308][1679]

## Analog versus DDS architectures

Function generators come in two architectural types: analog designs that generate the waveform with analog circuitry, and direct digital synthesis (DDS) designs that generate the signal digitally and convert it with a digital-to-analog converter.[168] The DDS type is the recommended choice for a general lab instrument, and nearly all modern function generators are DDS-based.[168][823] This distinguishes them from RF signal generators, which are proper analog oscillators — for example spanning 9 kHz to 1.5 GHz — rather than DAC-based waveform synthesizers.[823]

DDS is inexpensive but has characteristic weaknesses: it exhibits jitter, and square waves in particular suffer badly from jitter in DDS implementations. As one Keysight engineer put it, "Square waves are horrendous with jitter in DDS technology."[1032] Keysight's Trueform architecture was developed to address these problems and to simplify the internal structure of the generator.[1032] At the high end, even the instrument's own power supply becomes a signal-integrity problem: in one high-end Keysight generator, switching power supplies feeding the high-power ASICs created spurs in the output at 120 MHz, requiring a power-supply redesign to reduce the noise.[1032]

In practice, the waveform synthesis hardware takes many forms depending on instrument class. Oscilloscope-integrated generators are commonly implemented in an FPGA with op-amps providing the analog output drive.[842] Low-cost instruments may use a microcontroller's internal DAC — a 4 megasample-per-second update-rate DAC in a GigaDevice MCU suffices for a basic 10 MHz square/triangle/sine source[1723] — or even a discrete R2R ladder network driven by a PIC32.[1056] Keysight integrates the waveform generation into its MegaZoom oscilloscope ASICs rather than using a separate DAC.[1639][976]

## Output stage, termination, and amplitude behavior

Function generators have a 50-ohm output impedance, and proper use demands attention to termination. Instruments offer selectable output load settings — high impedance or 50 ohms — and the setting must match the actual load.[143][6qjqhnQiQXQ] For accurate amplitude measurement, the high-impedance output setting should be used, because with 50-ohm termination the accuracy of the load, the divider formed with the source impedance, and the cabling all enter the error budget.[6qjqhnQiQXQ]

Feeding a generator through unterminated coaxial cable produces transmission-line reflections that make the measured amplitude vary strongly with frequency; at particular frequencies the reflected wave cancels the incident wave right at the generator output, and the signal at the oscilloscope can nearly vanish.[652] An apparent amplitude-versus-frequency problem in such a setup is therefore usually a measurement-technique error, not a faulty generator.[652] Conversely, the 50-ohm output impedance is too high to drive low-impedance devices directly — a curve-tracer application, for instance, requires an op-amp buffer stage after the generator.[1137] A function generator output also cannot drive heavy loads such as a 24-volt solenoid directly; a power transistor stage is needed.[87E3wqfxHqg]

Output amplitude is not perfectly flat over frequency. A typical specification is ±0.5 dB amplitude flatness over the full range for a 20 MHz class generator[143]; a better unit may hold within about 0.4 dB[652], but the working rule is to never assume constant output amplitude over frequency and to verify it during swept measurements.[572] Accuracy also degrades at very small amplitudes: a generator set to 10 mV peak-to-peak may actually deliver 4.7 mV at the low end of its range[196], and generators with a fixed full-scale output (e.g., 2.5 V) produce noisy, erroneous waveforms when asked for tiny signals.[692] Care is also required when injecting signals into circuitry carrying DC: connecting a generator directly to a node sitting at several volts DC can feed that voltage back into the generator's output stage, so AC coupling through a series capacitor is the safe approach.[966]

## Modes and auxiliary features

Beyond continuous waveforms, function generators provide burst mode, frequency sweep, and modulation. Burst mode generates a train of cycles followed by a dead period, useful for exciting resonant devices such as glass delay lines with pulses near their 4.43 MHz resonance.[386] Sweep capability drives applications like vibration testing, where a 30-second logarithmic sweep from 10 Hz to 200 Hz can be programmed for a shaker table.[1443] Amplitude, frequency, and pulse-width modulation are standard on mid-range and better instruments[1701][1724], as are symmetry and DC-offset controls.[1724]

Most generators provide a sync or trigger output: a pulse generated at the start of each burst or cycle, with selectable polarity, which gives an oscilloscope a clean trigger reference and is the preferred fix for trigger-jitter problems when measuring burst signals.[387] Some oscilloscopes can similarly reconfigure their built-in generator's auxiliary output as a trigger output for waveform-update-rate measurement.[1478] Some generators also include a hardware frequency counter input; the Rigol DG4000 series implements reciprocal counting, giving high resolution even at low frequencies, though the function is considered an odd fit for the instrument class.[325][456]

## Bench applications

Component and curve testing. A function generator driving an oscilloscope in XY mode forms a simple component tester: a circle or ellipse indicates a capacitor or inductor, an L-shaped curve a diode, a sloped straight line a resistor (slope indicating value), a vertical line a short circuit, and a horizontal line an open circuit.[1137] Unlike a fixed-frequency component tester, this arrangement lets the component be characterized at any desired test frequency.[1137] The same generator-plus-series-resistor topology is the internal operating principle of LCR meters: the generator drives the device under test through a known series resistor, and the resulting voltage is amplified and digitized.[81]

Frequency response measurement. A generator swept in frequency, with output amplitude read on a multimeter or oscilloscope, yields a frequency-response plot without any specialized instrument.[396][620] Combining a function generator with an oscilloscope in a network-analyzer arrangement — especially with 14-bit resolution on both sides — produces magnitude and phase Bode plots directly.[692] Oscilloscopes with built-in generators increasingly offer this as a one-button Bode plot feature.[1309][N63dJHbCdbk]

EMC and troubleshooting. A generator is a standard tool for recreating interference problems: a 5–10 MHz square wave at 10 V peak-to-peak applied near a multimeter's current input reproduced a conducted common-mode susceptibility fault in the Keysight U1272A.[933][987]

Electromechanical drive. Low-frequency sine output drives voice-coil-type actuators such as hard-disk head positioners (bandwidth around a kilohertz or two), camera lens motors at 1–2 Hz, and shaker tables via a power amplifier.[395][849][1442]

Oscilloscope characterization. A 1 kHz square wave with a fast edge from a generator substitutes for a high-frequency source when checking oscilloscope bandwidth via rise time[196]; a 1 kHz triangle wave into a high-impedance input reveals ADC clipping for dynamic-range measurement.[6qjqhnQiQXQ]

## Integration into other instruments

Oscilloscope-integrated waveform generators began with the Agilent 2000 X-Series, whose ASIC included a 20 MHz generator producing sine, square, ramp, pulse, DC, and noise, with 0.1 Hz to 20 MHz range, ±0.5 dB flatness, −40 dB (1%) harmonic distortion, 500 ps square-wave jitter, and 18 ns rise/fall time.[143] The original implementation conspicuously lacked arbitrary-waveform capability; it was added later as a free firmware upgrade.[209][1032] The feature then propagated across the industry: the Keysight 1000 X-Series carries a 20 MHz generator in a $450–700 scope[976][N63dJHbCdbk], the Siglent SDS2000X Plus a 50 MHz generator enabling Bode plots[1309], the Tektronix 2 Series a 50 MHz generator plus a four-channel digital pattern generator[qE-SwtYQM6E], and the Rohde & Schwarz MXO4 a 100 MHz built-in generator.[1529]

Integrated generators carry real limitations, however. The GW Instek GDS-2000A has two generator BNCs on the front panel, but the manual states only one generator module can be used at a time.[475] On the Moku:Go, opening the oscilloscope instrument switches off a running waveform generator, and the generator lacks a selectable 50-ohm/high-impedance load setting entirely.[1701] Generators built into ultra-cheap handheld scope/multimeters are frequently non-functional in practice: the function generator in the Zoyi ZT-703S is described as useless, and the signal source in the FNIRSI DPOX180H has a non-usable pin output and is best treated as absent.[1597][1582]

Function generators also appear inside instruments of other classes. The Keithley 2015 THD multimeter contains a low-distortion audio-frequency generator on rear-panel BNCs to support automated total-harmonic-distortion measurement.[478] Some multimeters provide simple generator or square-wave outputs[184][1007], and USB all-in-one instruments build them in alongside scopes and supplies: the Digilent Analog Discovery pairs a 14-bit, 100 MS/s generator with a 14-bit scope front end[692][464], the OpenScope MZ offers a basic generator limited to 3 V peak-to-peak with ±1.5 V offset[1056], and the NI VirtualBench provides sine, square, triangle, DC, and arbitrary waveforms.[876] Retro combination instruments such as the BWD 603B MiniLab integrated a 2 MHz analog generator with symmetry, offset, TTL output, and AM/FM modulation alongside an oscilloscope and supplies.[1724][1716]

## Performance range

The price-to-performance span is extreme. Kit generators cost about $15 and produce basic low-frequency waveforms[954]; a new analog 3 MHz instrument such as the Instek GFG8219A represents the traditional entry-level bench unit[5], and usable generators were available around $150.[168] Mid-range DDS arbitrary generators such as the Siglent SDG5082 provide 80 MHz bandwidth at 500 MS/s for a four-figure price.[497] At the top of the general-purpose market, the Uni-T UTG9504T offers four channels at 500 MHz bandwidth and 2.5 GS/s for nearly $6,000 — though only channels 1 and 2 achieve the full 500 MHz (at 14-bit resolution), while channels 3 and 4 are limited to 200 MHz at 625 MS/s, albeit at 16 bits; its closest competitor is the two-channel Siglent SDG6000 series.[fM4bbKVw2og][cziiWo6Uh5M][1679] Combined instruments such as the Agilent 81160A — a 330/500 MHz pulse, function, arbitrary, and noise generator in one chassis — reach roughly $20,000.[308][309] Such prices reflect the cost of the high-speed DACs and the substantial software and engineering development behind a modern generator, and specialized instruments can exceed these figures by an order of magnitude.[1679]
