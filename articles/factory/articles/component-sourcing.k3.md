# component sourcing

Component sourcing is the process of finding, evaluating, selecting, and purchasing the electronic parts that go into a design. It consumes the majority of the effort in product development: a designer will typically spend more time searching for suitable parts that meet electrical, mechanical, visual, and cost criteria than on the circuit design and PCB layout themselves, with each of the hundreds of individual part decisions in a product potentially involving hours of searching.[72][88][87] For kits and small production runs, the bill of materials and component availability — not the circuit — is usually the hard part, sometimes requiring volume commitments of several thousand pieces on a single part to guarantee supply.[555]

## The parametric search workflow

The standard workflow begins with parametric search on catalog distributor sites — Digi-Key, Mouser, Farnell, Newark, RS Components — which allow filtering across many manufacturers at once by parameters such as accuracy, on-resistance, package, price, and stock.[72][1180] Aggregator services such as Octopart and FindChips search across distributors simultaneously to compare price and availability, and are used routinely to verify that a candidate part is actually obtainable before it is designed in.[2][87][754] Distributor search engines are not exhaustive, however; parts frequently appear only on a manufacturer's own website, so a thorough search cross-checks manufacturer parametric tools as well, since a missed part can mean a missed optimum.[139][1180] Search results must then be confirmed against stock: filtering to in-stock items only can collapse hundreds of candidates down to a manageable few dozen.[87] Obscure or unbranded parts — for instance an unidentified Sino Power MOSFET — may require searching the manufacturer's own site directly when the part number yields nothing on the major catalogs.[1461]

## Economics of search time

Time spent searching is justified by production arithmetic. Saving 10¢ on a single part across a 10,000-unit run returns $1,000, so at a nominal engineering rate of $100 per hour, five to ten hours spent finding the right part still comes out ahead; genuinely critical parts can merit days of searching.[1180] Selection of apparently mundane components can be protracted in professional contexts — selecting and qualifying a suitable switch for a navy simulator took three months.[1686] For cost-sensitive designs, BOM costing is conventionally done at 1,000-off distributor pricing as a realistic benchmark, since that is the quantity break distributors publish.[1679] Very large buyers obtain pricing far below catalog figures through direct deals, which is why BOM estimates for products from companies with the buying power of Amazon or Apple tend to overstate their true component cost.[220]

## Availability-driven design

Availability is a first-order design constraint, not a purchasing afterthought. A part with a 9-week lead time or thin stock is a design-in risk, and where stock differs between variants of the same part, the well-stocked variant wins.[248][87] The consistent practical rule is to order the parts before designing the PCB: "Buy the parts before you design the PCB."[1539] Parts that go out of stock between schematic capture and layout can leave a board with an unpopulated footprint, as happened with an FT230XS USB-UART that became unavailable everywhere after design-in.[357] Distributor stock shown online may be physically offshore — a part listed by Farnell or RS in Australia may actually sit in a UK warehouse with the attendant delay.[1387][GbADsyp9wM8] Lead times of 18 weeks or more effectively disqualify a part regardless of price.[87] During shortages the problem compounds: high-end FPGAs have shown zero stock and 52-week lead times at catalog distributors, and products designed around a single constrained microcontroller have forced small businesses to close entirely when multi-year lead times made the part unobtainable.[1396][1539] Shortage conditions also attract brokers — described as "component vultures" — charging around $15 for a chip normally worth $2–$3, which alone can blow out a product's cost target.[121]

## Jellybean parts and second sourcing

A jellybean part is defined by four properties: it has been manufactured for a long time, it is available from dozens of manufacturers both Western and Asian, it is extremely cheap, and it is stocked in depth — millions of pieces across distributors even during component shortages.[1436][1599][1736][1611] The engineering value is substitutability: if the specified manufacturer cannot supply, any equivalent with the same part number will do the job, and jellybean MOSFETs can be found at around 1.3 cents each with hundreds of thousands in stock across multiple vendors.[1436][1736] The "holy grail" of design is multiple qualified suppliers for every part in the bill of materials, though most real products contain at least one single-source part.[1697] When relying on an obscure ultra-cheap source, equivalent name-brand alternates should be listed on the BOM as insurance against that source drying up.[1461] Within a product line, reusing parts already in the company's catalog and already loaded on its pick-and-place machines is standard practice, because "the last thing you want to do is use yet another component that you've got to stock."[1405] Consolidating the number of distinct line items matters for the same reason: a board using 160 different component types costs more to assemble, and any single missing part out of those 160 can halt manufacture entirely.[1307]

## Bill of materials discipline

The BOM is the boundary between engineering and purchasing. A competent design engineer specifies exact part numbers down to package and grade suffixes, plus a list of qualified alternates, and competent purchasing staff buy only what is listed from the specified suppliers.[1752][1697] Ordering suffix letters carry real meaning — package, supply voltage range, temperature grade — and getting one character wrong yields the wrong part.[193][1074] Specifying a generic part ("buy an LM317") is cited as the worst practice, because characteristics then vary by manufacturer and batch across the production run, with no consistency between units.[512][1752] Even legitimately purchased parts can change under a fixed part number: a manufacturer die revision that alters a specification the design happens to rely on will flow automatically into production and fail in the field at scale, which is why end-of-life and change notifications matter at high volume.[1752] Conversely, if the BOM permits any qualified supplier, the assembler will load whichever reel is on hand, and batch-to-batch or brand-to-brand variation — for example in the piezoelectric behavior of a generic 10 µF ceramic bypass capacitor — becomes an uncontrolled variable in the product.[1697][855] Connectors nominally rated for reflow have arrived in batches that melt, apparently due to changed plastic formulation or substituted clone parts, one batch working and the next failing.[782]

## Distributors, the gray market, and counterfeits

Authorized distributors — Digi-Key, Mouser, Farnell, and similar catalog suppliers — are the standard defense against counterfeit parts; fake components from these channels are described as virtually unheard of, whereas gray-market purchases regularly deliver fakes or wrong-tolerance parts.[1057][197][134] Re-reeled cut tape from a distributor carries less traceability than an original manufacturer reel, since the original label is lost when the distributor applies its own sticker.[1057] Supply chains are porous enough that even large, reputable companies get stung by counterfeit parts, which is why driver-level retaliation against end products (as in the FTDI bricking episode) punishes buyers rather than counterfeiters, who have already been paid.[676] Historical teardown evidence shows the consequences of price-only purchasing: the Osborne 1's memory was bought as the cheapest chips available from whatever source was at hand, mixing brands and factories on one board.[955] Cheap consumer products similarly use "whatever grade component they can find," including potentially fake capacitors, which precludes any reliability expectation.[6] Partial reels and cut tape also create practical assembly problems, feeding poorly in pick-and-place machines.[264]

## Chinese supply channels

A large parallel ecosystem of parts exists that is invisible to Western catalog channels: Chinese-market microcontrollers at 3 cents, linear regulators at 1 cent, and 10-cent RISC-V parts are stocked in tens of thousands by suppliers such as LCSC but do not appear on Digi-Key, Mouser, FindChips, or Octopart.[1132][1147][1524] Accessing this channel means going direct to Chinese suppliers or manufacturers, via LCSC, Alibaba, or AliExpress; AliExpress is preferable to Alibaba for small buys because Alibaba listings frequently carry placeholder prices and require quote negotiation.[400] Direct dealing with larger Chinese vendors is workable if exact part numbers are specified precisely, and Mandarin language ability is a significant asset.[111] Hobbyist-scale purchasing exploits the same channel: resistor kits and assorted parts ship from China for one or two dollars delivered, making a $300 lab setup feasible.[954] The trade-offs are quality opacity — no-name film and ceramic capacitors carry elevated failure risk in stress applications such as capacitive droppers — and distribution gaps, since even legitimate Chinese manufacturers' parts may be unobtainable from any catalog stockist.[1486][1037][1275]

## Production purchasing

Production quantities change the purchasing rules. Assemblers require parts on reels rather than loose cut tape; supplying 100 loose resistors for 100 boards forces manual re-reeling and extra charges.[127] Minimum order quantities bite hard: a part available only as a 3,000-piece reel at 40 cents forces a $1,209 outlay to obtain 100 needed parts, and parts that come only in 2,500-piece reels must simply be designed out for small runs.[127][239] Distributor re-reeling services (around $7) supply exact quantities, though buying the full reel is often simpler and provides stock for future runs.[m9tza_c4sxc] Orders must include attrition overage — roughly 5% extra on passives, or 10% / 50 extra pieces as alternative rules of thumb — because pick-and-place machines drop parts and reel leaders waste the first components fed through the machine; parts worth fractions of a cent are never recovered, so a 1,000-board run cannot be kitted with exactly 1,000 of each part.[562][239][m9tza_c4sxc] A turnkey assembly service removes all of this — the assembler sources everything from the Gerbers and BOM alone — at a substantial price premium and loss of control.[127] Contract assemblers typically supply 80–90% of components themselves, asking the customer to buy in only product-specific parts, a division that reflects who bears the sourcing risk.[684] For one-time-programmable parts, factory programming happens before delivery, with parts shipped pre-programmed on tubes or reels straight to the line.[1141] Historically none of this existed: components were expensive, lead times ran to 40 weeks, and salvaging parts from discarded equipment was standard practice, particularly outside the US.[54][riBwRC_CaAA]

## Lab stockpiling

Maintaining a comprehensive personal component stock is a losing game — "you're never going to have the part you want in your lab" — because modern designs need specific devices for specific reasons. Next-day or same-day delivery from catalog distributors has largely eliminated the case for deep personal stock beyond jellybean staples.[107]
