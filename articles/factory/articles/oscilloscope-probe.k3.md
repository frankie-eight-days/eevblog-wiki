# oscilloscope probe

An oscilloscope probe is the interconnect between an oscilloscope input and the circuit under test, and it is a designed measurement component rather than a piece of wire: its resistors, capacitors, and deliberately lossy cable are matched to the oscilloscope's 1 MΩ, roughly 15–18 pF front end so that the probe-plus-scope system achieves a specified bandwidth and a flat frequency response.[1266][1367][1445] Because a probe always loads the circuit it touches, its impedance characteristics are as much a part of a measurement as the oscilloscope itself.[1367][1445]

## Construction

A standard ×10 passive probe places a 9 MΩ series resistor at the tip in parallel with a small compensation capacitor; together with the oscilloscope's 1 MΩ input resistance this forms a 10:1 resistive voltage divider, giving the probe a 10 MΩ DC input impedance.[453][778][1399] The "×10" marking is a misnomer — the probe attenuates by ten rather than multiplying.[778][1367]

The cable is not ordinary coax. The centre conductor is a single fine strand of a resistive, nichrome-like material, often given deliberate kinks or wiggles, forming a lossy transmission line with a DC resistance on the order of 230–330 Ω where plain coax would measure near zero.[453][1266][594] This lossy construction is what allows a ×10 probe to achieve wide and flat bandwidth without the 50 Ω termination that a direct coax connection requires at both ends; an unterminated plain coax run instead exhibits transmission-line reflections that corrupt amplitude measurements as frequency rises.[453][1266][652] The trade-off is that passive probes of this type top out around 500 MHz, with 700 MHz regarded as pushing the technology and roughly 1 GHz the practical ceiling, while properly terminated coax can go considerably higher.[1266][1529][792]

## The ×1/×10 switch and bandwidth

Many general-purpose probes carry a switch selecting ×1 or ×10. In the ×1 position the switch simply shorts out the 9 MΩ resistor and compensation capacitor, connecting the tip straight through to the oscilloscope input, so the probe presents the scope's own 1 MΩ DC input impedance with the full cable capacitance across it.[453][1367]

The consequence is a drastic bandwidth reduction in ×1 mode — typically an order of magnitude or more below the ×10 figure. Representative numbers are 6.5 MHz for a typical switchable probe, 8 MHz ×1 versus 350 MHz ×10 for a Rigol probe, and 5 MHz ×1 versus 100 MHz ×10 on a tablet oscilloscope's supplied probe; a few designs reach 20 MHz in ×1.[1445][441][1317][EWR4RQPzB6U] In ×10 mode the divider network reduces the effective capacitance seen by the circuit, which is why ×10 probes deliver the high bandwidth and why many quality probes are supplied as fixed ×10 only.[441][778] A lone ×1 probe simulated in LTspice with 329 Ω series resistance and about 100 pF of cable capacitance over a 1.2 m cable yields about 7.8 MHz bandwidth by itself, dropping lower once the oscilloscope's input capacitance is included.[1445]

The ×1 position retains two legitimate uses: avoiding the ten-fold attenuation when measuring very low-level signals, and exploiting the low bandwidth as incidental filtering when measuring noise in a restricted band, since a power-supply noise specification given over 0–20 MHz cannot be honestly measured with a 350 MHz ×10 path picking up everything above it.[1367][771][441]

A persistent trap is that no oscilloscope can detect the position of a switchable probe's ×1/×10 switch; professional fixed-attenuation probes omit the switch partly for this reason. The switch is easily bumped while handling probes on the bench, and unless the oscilloscope's software probe attenuation setting matches the physical switch, every vertical reading is out by a factor of ten — an error that catches experienced engineers as well as beginners.[778]

## Input capacitance and probe loading

The figure that matters at high frequency is not the 10 MΩ DC resistance but the probe's input capacitance, sometimes specified as total system capacitance — the capacitance the circuit sees with the probe plugged into the oscilloscope. Typical values run from about 17 pF for a 150 MHz general-purpose probe and 14–18 pF for 200 MHz class probes down to 11–12 pF for 500 MHz probes and 3.9 pF for a 1 GHz passive probe.[1367][704][1362][810][792] At frequency, that capacitance presents a capacitive reactance that loads the circuit far more than the DC resistance suggests.[1367]

Loading effects are readily observed in sensitive circuits: a 10 MΩ ×10 probe is enough to hold an LED trigger circuit permanently on, to pull a floating CMOS clock pin low and stop a counter, to disturb a piezoelectric oscillator's tone, and to load a peak-detector capacitor measurably at low frequencies.[471][801][855][490] Probing the oscillator pin of a crystal circuit with a ×1 probe is a standard demonstration of how severely the added capacitance can upset an oscillator.[1614] Every probing connection loads the circuit in a complex way, which is why probe loading can and should be included in circuit simulation.[1445]

## Compensation

A ×10 probe must be compensated to match the individual oscilloscope channel's input capacitance, using the oscilloscope's probe-adjust square-wave output (typically 1 kHz): an under- or over-compensated probe shows rounded or overshooting corners and misrepresents amplitude.[1362][842] The compensation adjustment is a trimmer capacitor, typically with a range on the order of 0–50 pF; lower-bandwidth probes carry the adjustment at the probe head, while high-bandwidth designs place the network at the cable's base near the BNC.[453][1362] Some oscilloscopes provide a guided on-screen probe-adjust procedure that displays the live waveform and indicates whether alignment is needed.[842]

## Grounding technique

The long alligator-clip ground lead supplied with probes is an inductor, and any length of wire is an inductor; used at speed it produces overshoot, undershoot, and ringing that belong to the measurement setup rather than the circuit.[1365][1081][324] Proper high-frequency technique replaces the ground lead and tip with the short low-inductance spring ground contact supplied in the probe accessory kit, keeping both the signal and ground paths as short as possible.[324][1081] The ground lead also acts as an antenna, and the probe assembly as a whole will pick up ESD and high-frequency disturbances capacitively even when disconnected from any circuit.[1107][20]

## Safety

The probe's ground clip is hard-wired through the cable shield and the oscilloscope chassis to mains earth, and on a multi-channel oscilloscope all channel grounds are shorted together.[279] Clipping the ground lead onto any point that is not at earth potential creates a short circuit capable of carrying substantial current; the slim ground lead is the weak link and can vaporise in the hand if the shorted node has enough energy behind it.[279] Equipment assumed to be isolated frequently is not — a USB-powered board, for example, becomes mains-earth-referenced through the host computer — so ground status must be verified before probing.[279][279] A common consequence of learning this the hard way is one destroyed probe per career.[131] Active FET probes carry the same restriction: they are not floating, and even expensive units carry warnings of a few volts maximum common-mode rating.[1525] Measurements on floating or mains-referenced circuits require a differential probe, an isolated oscilloscope with fully shrouded connectors and insulated probe accessories, or an optical-fibre probe.[1137][810][1557]

## Probe types

Passive probes contain no active electronics — at most compensation trimmers and resistors — and span three broad designs in ascending performance: the switchable ×1/×10 probe, the fixed ×10 probe, and plain coax-based probing, which is technically the highest-bandwidth and lowest-cost option but demands correct termination.[1367] Beyond 500 MHz, active FET probes become the appropriate tool because their input capacitance is far lower than any passive design; commercial examples reach multi-gigahertz bandwidths, and a simple active probe can be built around a single BF998 FET in common-drain configuration feeding a 50 Ω-terminated input.[1715][1525] Specialised active probes for power-rail measurement add large DC offset ranges, on the order of ±20 V, so that millivolt-level ripple on a high DC rail can be examined directly.[1735]

High-voltage passive probes extend the divider to 100:1 or 1000:1, enabling measurements of hundreds of volts — for example inductor back-EMF spikes approaching 700 V — that would otherwise exceed both the oscilloscope's input rating and the ×10 probe's.[1409][778][1753] Voltage rating also differs between switch positions: on one tablet oscilloscope the input is rated 40 V in ×1 mode and 400 V only with the ×10 divider engaged.[1317]

A capable low-cost alternative for high-bandwidth work is the DIY resistive transmission-line probe: a series resistor of about 1 kΩ at the tip over properly terminated coax yields an input capacitance of only 1–2 pF and, with careful construction, signal fidelity comparable to commercial gigahertz-class probes for a few dollars in parts.[1718]

Probe linearity itself is a limit in precision work: a good probe may be linear only to about 1%, one part in a hundred, which is inadequate when a measurement demands one part in several thousand.[1119]

## Measurement artifacts

Two artifacts intrinsic to probes can masquerade as circuit faults. First, passive probes are microphonic: the multilayer ceramic capacitors inside the probe head exhibit the piezoelectric effect, and tapping the probe on the bench produces a damped oscillatory shock response that can exceed 2 V peak-to-peak. The response persists with the tip shorted (at reduced amplitude and altered frequency), depends on the probe's physical orientation, is essentially independent of the ×1/×10 switch position, and is common to all oscilloscopes.[162][983][983] The same effect lets a ×10 probe with its input shorted serve as a crude vibration or acoustic sensor, able to pick up the vibrational modes of a PCB at a specific frequency, such as 11 kHz from an excited board.[1743]

Second, the probe cable's inductance resonates with the oscilloscope's input capacitance as an LC tank, practically always near 100–120 MHz. Broadband static energy — for example from an operator standing up from a chair — is picked up directly by the cable and rings this tank, producing a clean sinusoidal burst on screen even with the probe tip shorted, with the ground lead disconnected, or with a plain coax cable (terminated or not) substituted for the probe. The pickup is a property of the cable itself, not of the ground-lead loop, and occurs on essentially any scope and probe combination; it can be injected during a real measurement and mistaken for a circuit glitch.[14][20][21]

## Oscilloscope-side handling

Many oscilloscopes implement probe detection through a contact pin or grounded ring around the BNC, automatically switching the channel's scaling when a matching ×10 probe is attached; third-party probes without the pin require the attenuation to be set manually in the channel menu.[864][778] Because probe attenuation is purely a software scaling factor and does not change the hardware input ranges, the menu setting must match the physical probe or the vertical readout is wrong by the probe ratio.[778] For multi-channel work, oscilloscopes also provide per-channel deskew to time-align signals arriving through different probes.[704] Hands-free probe holders — weighted or magnetic stands that hold a sharp sprung tip on a fine-pitch SMD pin under their own pressure — address the practical problem of holding several probes on adjacent pins while keeping a hand free, though they inherit the same grounding and signal-integrity limitations as hand-held probing.[GS0WqUKZ-3c][1362][1324]

In Australian usage the probe is colloquially a "crow probe", from cathode-ray oscilloscope.[21][594]
