# analog to digital converter

An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) turns a continuous voltage into a number, and it is the component that sets the ultimate limit on what a digital measuring instrument can see. Everything downstream — an oscilloscope's screen, a multimeter's display, a spectrum analyser's noise floor, a digital mixing desk's processing — operates only on the data the converter produced, so resolution, full-scale range and integration time at the ADC propagate through the whole instrument.[1226][594][1379] Once a signal has been converted, it can be processed, filtered and transformed essentially without limit, which is why the boundary between analog and digital in modern equipment keeps moving closer to the input connector.[598][1109]

## Resolution, reference and the size of one bit

Resolution is expressed in bits, and the number of distinct output codes is two raised to that power: a 12-bit converter has 4,096 steps.[232] The voltage represented by one step follows directly from the reference voltage divided by that count, so a 2.5 V reference on a 12-bit converter gives roughly 610 microvolts per bit — an awkward number that forces scaling arithmetic on every reading.[232] Choosing a 2.048 V reference instead makes the values come out exactly, at 500 microvolts per bit, which is the reason such odd-looking reference values are common in instrument design.[232] The same divide-down logic runs in reverse when specifying a design: to resolve 0.1 W out of a 2,400 W mains load requires around 24,000 counts, which means at least a 15-bit and realistically a 16-bit converter per channel.[409]

The corollary is that the input signal should be scaled to fill the converter's range. A divider chosen so the maximum output voltage lands exactly at the 2.048 V reference uses every available code; the design intent is blunt — "You don't want to piss away any bits."[232] The same principle governs the choice of a current shunt: the shunt must not be made so small that its voltage drop falls below one least significant bit, since the aim is to measure accurately down to the last bit.[232] With a 500-microamp-per-bit design and a 0.2-ohm shunt, that sets a floor of 100 microvolts across the resistor, which in turn constrains the input offset voltage of the amplifier driving the converter.[232]

Converters carry inherent uncertainty of about plus or minus one digit, which is a property of the converter itself rather than a fault; a reading differing in the last count is unremarkable, while differences of two or three counts start to indicate a genuine discrepancy between instruments.[852] Errors also become a larger percentage of the reading when the measurement sits low in the range, because fewer ADC counts are involved.[852]

## Built-in versus external converters

Microcontroller-integrated converters are adequate for a great many tasks and are ubiquitous: 4-bit converters monitoring a battery during charging in a toothbrush,[284] an 8-channel 8-bit ADC inside a 68HC11 doing gas-sensor data logging,[603] an 8-bit multi-channel converter in a banknote acceptor reading sensors,[525] a 12-bit converter in an 8051-derived current-probe micro,[1413] and 12-bit, 1 megasample-per-second converters with eight multiplexed input channels in a multicore applications processor.[624] More exotic integrated parts exist, including devices carrying a dozen or so 20-bit delta-sigma converters alongside programmable logic.[510]

The practical ceiling on integrated converters is around 10 bits, which is why most microcontrollers stop there; pushing to 12 bits inside a microcontroller generally performs worse than moving to an external part.[232] Integrated converters are also usually not as good as dedicated ones even at the same nominal resolution.[1413] A design needing better than 10-bit measurement therefore commonly uses an external ADC and DAC pair.[232] Where no converter exists at all, the analog signal must be squared up into a digital one before it reaches the processor — a comparator ahead of a microcontroller with no ADC turns a microphone preamp output into something the part can accept.[713]

Peripheral selection is rarely free of conflict. On LCD-driving microcontrollers, analog input pins are frequently mapped onto the same package pins as LCD segment drivers, so adding decimal points to the display consumes analog channels; a part that cannot yield three ADC channels alongside the required display gets ruled out.[248] Resolution can eliminate a candidate outright, as an 8-bit integrated converter does where 10 bits are needed.[87] The wider discipline is the same: designing a product rather than a one-off forces a survey of perhaps a dozen different converters against price, performance, availability, package and power consumption at once.[302]

## Driving and laying out a converter

A successive-approximation converter cannot be fed from an arbitrarily high impedance, so voltage buffers ahead of the ADC inputs are part of the circuit rather than an optional refinement.[232] Datasheet limits make this explicit: an integrated 12-bit converter specifying 500 ohms maximum analog source resistance and 2 microamps of input leakage needs a low-impedance driver to keep errors down.[642] Where many slow signals share one converter, an analog multiplexer feeding the ADC through a low-pass filter is the standard arrangement — cell voltages in a balance charger reach the converter via a 4051 multiplexer with dividers and filtering,[397] and an LCR meter simply multiplexes its voltage and current measurements into a single ADC and calculates the rest.[757]

Converter datasheets are also the place to look for layout guidance; a good part will at minimum describe the layout requirements in text, and choosing a part with a proper datasheet is itself a layout strategy.[131] Physical placement matters in high-speed designs too: the differential pairs and serpentine length-matched traces running between the front-end drivers and the converter, and from the converter to the acquisition FPGA, are visible signatures of a sampling front end.[1260][790][1717]

Converters are a point of vulnerability to interference. A multimeter whose readings collapse when a hand is brought near the leads is suffering conducted and capacitively coupled energy flooding the ADC beyond what it can handle.[987]

## Converters in oscilloscopes

Most digital oscilloscopes have historically used 8-bit converters, giving 256 levels.[876][878] That resolution defines the usable dynamic range, and much of the practical technique of digital scope use is about not wasting it. A waveform occupying only a fraction of the screen is digitised by only a fraction of the available codes, producing worse resolution and worse automated measurements; using the vernier or fine-adjustment control to expand the waveform to just within full scale uses the maximum number of bits and gives a smaller standard deviation on measurements.[1228][1226] On scopes where the vernier drives a real variable-gain amplifier the gain is genuine hardware gain, calibrated internally; on others the front end has no such amplifier and only digital gain control inside the converter chip itself, which is not equivalent.[1228] Going the other way is worse: once the signal exceeds the converter's range it clips, the data simply is not there, and any math function computed from it produces garbage.[793][594] A power-rail probe's DC offset control exists precisely to slide a small ripple signal into the converter's range so the full dynamic range is available for it.[1733][1038]

The converter's full-scale range is not always documented. It can be bounded experimentally by increasing a known input until clipping appears — a measurement that put one scope's full scale somewhere between 8.75 V and 8.8 V peak to peak — but the result may reflect where the input amplifier clips rather than the converter itself, and without a schematic or a manufacturer figure the two cannot be separated.[6qjqhnQiQXQ]

Sample rate is bought by interleaving converters. Five dual-channel AD9288 parts give ten converters, each running at 100 megasamples per second to produce an aggregate 1 gigasample per second; input relays switch the signal between five and ten converters depending on channel count, which is why dual-channel mode halves the rate.[19][37] The AD9288-40 devices used in that design are 40 megasample-per-second parts being run at 100 MHz — overclocking that works because speed grades are often the same die.[19] The same architecture recurs across generations: one converter shared across four channels drops a scope from 1 gigasample per second to 250 megasamples when all channels are on,[674] two converters each shared between two channels preserve full rate on two channels but halve it on four,[1042][1503][1309] and the difference between a manufacturer's higher and lower model can amount to the absence of one converter.[1563] Dedicated fast parts are purpose-built for this: the ADC08D1520 is an 8-bit, 1.5 gigasample-per-second dual converter capable of being interleaved for double rate on one channel,[875] and the related ADC08D1000 delivers 7.4 effective bits at 500 MHz analog bandwidth.[790] Where two converter chips exist but the signal is not routed between them, both cannot be used on one channel and the per-channel rate stays at the single-converter figure.[1317]

Resolution above eight bits has moved steadily down-market. A 9-bit converter was an oddity distinguishing one vintage Tektronix scope.[565][564] A 10-bit converter with a true 1 mV noise floor makes FFT-based EMC probing usefully sensitive,[1188] and a custom in-house 10-bit converter gave one manufacturer a true 500 microvolt per division range over full bandwidth that no competitor in the price class matched.[983][1218][1226] Twelve-bit converters across all four channels then appeared in entry-level instruments,[1566][1612] and a custom in-house 14-bit converter followed, with software-magnified ranges below the true front-end sensitivity being acceptable precisely because the converter has the bits to spare.[1638] Some parts are natively multi-mode: the HAD1520 is a true combined 8-, 12- and 14-bit converter with a 16-bit data output and a precision mode at 14 bits, with sample rate falling as resolution rises.[1004][ByUiOk00K0U] Others offer adjustable resolution from 8 to 16 bits with sample rate traded against it, reaching 1,000 megasamples per second in single-channel 8-bit mode at half a watt.[521] At the low end, a 10-bit 50 megasample-per-second converter costs 86 US cents in thousands.[1540][1723]

High-resolution acquisition mode raises effective resolution above the converter's native bits — nine, ten, twelve, or as much as fourteen bits depending on sampling — and is more forgiving of artefacts than mathematical averaging.[878] Averaging in the acquisition path can likewise turn a 12-bit converter into a 15-bit result.[1744]

Two consequences of the sampling architecture are easily mistaken for faults. The trigger circuitry in many scopes is a separate analog path from the acquisition converter, so a scope can genuinely trigger on an event that never appears on screen, and an external trigger input is physically a different channel entirely.[1320] Modern scopes may instead derive the trigger digitally from the converter output and implement functions such as AC trigger coupling in software.[685] Separately, the front-end bandwidth filter means the converter never sees a true square wave at the bandwidth limit — the higher harmonics are filtered out and the converter samples something closer to a sine wave, which is what makes sine x on x interpolation a mathematically valid reconstruction.[1213]

## Converters in multimeters and precision instruments

Precision DC measurement uses a different class of converter entirely. Integrating architectures — dual-slope, multi-slope, single-slope and charge-balance designs — dominate, and in high-end meters the converter is frequently not a purchasable chip. A hybrid multi-slope part may contain only the converter logic, with the integration capacitor and switching outside it.[427] Manufacturers roll their own: a constant-frequency charge-balance single-slope converter in a picoammeter,[1017] a custom pre-amp and ADC visible on the analog board of a bench meter,[613] a converter recognisable by its sampling capacitors rather than any part number,[613] and in a 6.5-digit meter no individual ADC chip exists on the board at all.[485] Older designs used matched pairs such as the ICL71C03 and ICL8052 forming a 4.5-digit converter and display driver,[406] a single-slope 8/10-bit converter in a vintage phone,[492] or a 555 timer running the conversion against a voltage reference.[482] A dedicated 17-bit converter is what enables a soldering station to hold temperature accurately without calibration.[1106]

Integration time is the key specification. Setting the aperture to one mains period — 20 ms at 50 Hz — averages out line-frequency interference during the conversion itself, not mathematically afterwards, so a noisy reading becomes flat. More power line cycles buys accuracy and 50/60 Hz rejection at the cost of measurement speed.[1379] Some converters build both mains frequencies' rejection in, removing the need to select it in software.[171]

Off-the-shelf precision parts do appear: a 24-bit four-channel ADS1242 with an integrated programmable gain amplifier, 21 effective bits and 600 microwatts consumption suits a battery-powered handheld,[171] a 24-bit part performs the measure half of a sub-ten-dollar process meter,[1248] and an 18-bit converter running at a million samples per second turns a 7.5-digit meter into a digitiser.[731] Integration ranges from a single multimeter chipset doing range switching, conversion and LCD drive together, through front-end-only chipsets, to a sigma-delta converter built into the main microcontroller.[853][0Oo2J66FLZA] Meter architecture also allows reuse: a scopemeter with no dedicated multimeter chipset simply switches the multimeter input onto a spare channel of the scope's converter, and the multimeter specifications reflect that.[430]

Calibration is bound up with the converter. The quantities that determine a meter's calibration are the voltage reference, the analog-to-digital converter, and the resistor divider network; cleaning or reassembly does not disturb them, but replacing the microcontroller that contains the converter does, because the calibration absorbed that converter's errors.[qmcSEJehqZM][0Oo2J66FLZA] Where converters are used as laboratory instruments in their own right — measuring the voltage across superconducting magnets carrying 12 kiloamps — they themselves must be periodically verified against a precision source.[a4Xpsenpd6E]

## Converters in RF and spectrum analysis

Spectrum analysers increasingly dispense with the analog envelope detector in favour of an all-digital IF: the intermediate frequency stage is sampled directly by a high-resolution, high-sample-rate converter and everything after that is software.[1109][892] The converter chosen for this need not be exotic — a 12-bit AD9235, a part marketed for ultrasound and low-cost oscilloscopes, is sufficient.[1109] At higher input frequencies the signal is mixed down first so that a relatively slow converter working in the tens of megahertz can handle a 20 GHz instrument's IF.[470]

Direct sampling has an upper limit set by converter technology. Sampling straight to digital is practical into the single-digit gigahertz, and beyond that the parts cannot be bought — instrument makers develop proprietary custom ASIC converters instead.[1032] The same architecture appears at scale in deep-space communications, where the LNA output is down-converted to a 300 MHz IF that travels as analog to the operations centre and is sampled directly there.[1547]

Processing gain makes low-resolution converters do work that resolution alone would forbid. An 8-bit converter gives roughly a 50 dB noise floor, but digital down-conversion and discrete Fourier transforms over a narrow resolution bandwidth improve signal-to-noise by around 57 dB, yielding a 107 dB noise floor from that same 8-bit part.[587] Expressed the other way, a traditional analyser sampling at 20 megasamples per second would need a 12.5-bit converter to match what an 8-bit converter achieves at 10 gigasamples per second.[587] This is what allows a mixed-domain scope to add a 3 GHz spectrum analyser for a few tens of dollars of RF parts: the RF is fed into the converters already present for the analog channels, tied together to reach 10 gigasamples per second, exploiting the fact that the converter hybrid itself has an input bandwidth in excess of 5 GHz.[587]

## Other applications

A converter reading a peak detector allows the stored peak to be reset immediately after each reading via a MOSFET across the capacitor, rather than waiting for a resistor to discharge it — and brute-force sampling of the input to find a maximum is the alternative that the two-component detector avoids.[490] A single ADC pin can serve an entire keypad by giving each key a different printed resistor in a divider, the measured voltage mapping to a key in software; instrument and consumer designs do this routinely, and a dicky contact then shows up as an erratic voltage and spurious key detections.[1282][1602][1360] Signal-chain design aims to present the converter with a correctly scaled and level-shifted signal so that its dynamic range is fully used, whether the source is an ECG front end[660] or an audio input feeding I2S data to a DSP.[1322] Charger designs continuously scan cell sense lines through the converter to detect insertion and control the switching accordingly.[812]

Multi-channel data acquisition is the converter's other natural home, from 64-channel ADC cards built for particle physics experiments[994] to commercial units offering 14 analog inputs at 16 to 24 bits, expandable to 84, with a 24-bit option specified at 1 microvolt noise-free.[1190] At the extreme, a seismic streamer system carried 24-bit converters every 30 metres along 10 kilometres of cable — up to 10,000 channels of real-time synchronous sampling, collected over an E1 protocol on an ATM fibre-optic network with a 500 V DC distributed power system.[500][40] The pairing of fast converters with FPGAs, made cheap and available by the cell phone industry, is what made a modern digital scope buildable at all, and even so the high-speed 14-bit converters and DACs in a compact USB instrument are expensive enough that they, not the software, dominate the hardware cost.[207][1596][1552]
