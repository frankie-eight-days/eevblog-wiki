# analog to digital converter

An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) turns a continuous voltage into a numeric code that a processor can act on, and it is the boundary at which every measuring instrument stops being an analog circuit and becomes a computer.[853][391] Its resolution and its input range jointly set the smallest change an instrument can resolve, so the ADC — together with the voltage reference feeding it — is usually the component that defines what a meter, scope or data logger is actually capable of.[232][qmcSEJehqZM] Once a signal has been converted, essentially anything can be done to it in software, which is why so much circuitry that used to be analog has migrated to the digital side of the converter.[598][391]

## Resolution, reference and volts per bit

Resolution is set by the bit count and the reference voltage together. A 12-bit converter has 2 to the power of 12, or 4,096, steps; against a 2.5 V reference that works out to roughly 610 microvolts per bit, an awkward number that forces scaling arithmetic on every reading.[232] Choosing a 2.048 V reference instead makes the values come out spot on with no fudging, and the same trick can be pushed further upstream: setting a current-sense amplifier gain of 500 rather than 200 yields exactly one microamp per bit on a 12-bit converter, which is a nicer number to work with than 2.5 microamps.[232][238] A 1 V per amp current-sense output against a 2.048 V reference gives a usable range of 0 to 2.048 amps at 500 microamps per bit.[232]

The bit count needed follows from the required resolution at full scale. Resolving 0.1 watt in a domestic energy meter that must cover well beyond 2,400 watts implies about 24,000 counts, so at least a 15-bit and realistically a 16-bit converter per channel is required; a 12-bit converter is only adequate if sub-watt resolution is abandoned.[409] The reverse case is equally common: matching a 10-bit DAC needs only a 10-bit ADC, and paying for 12-bit measurement of a quantity a 10-bit output cannot control is waste.[259]

Resolution is also what the last digits of a reading are made of. When a measured value sits low in the range, the error becomes a greater percentage purely from the ADC count.[852] At the other end, a converter can be better than the displayed digits: a bench multimeter whose main 6½-digit display is static may still be producing more resolution internally, with the processor calculating a floating-point result from the converter output that can be extracted over LAN, GPIB or a CSV dump.[489]

## Converters inside microcontrollers

Integrated ADCs are cheap, multi-channel and adequate for a great many jobs. Microcontrollers routinely carry 8- to 12-bit converters with a dozen or more multiplexed input channels, used for battery voltage during charging, sensor reading, and general housekeeping.[444][595][603][525] Built-in converters are, however, generally not as good as dedicated parts, and pushing a microcontroller's ADC to 12 bits is the point at which an external converter usually wins — which is why most of them stop at 10 bits, since that is about all they are good for.[232][1413] Even where 12 bits are claimed, the effective number of bits is the question that matters.[642]

Selecting one is a real constraint problem: price, performance, availability, package type and power consumption all have to be met at once, and dozens of candidates may need to be evaluated.[302] Peripheral conflicts bite as well — on some parts almost all the ADC pins are multiplexed onto the LCD segment drivers, which can rule a device out entirely when three analog channels are needed.[248] And a converter sharing a die with switching loads is exposed to them: PWM-driven display current in the same chip has to be managed so it does not interfere with conversions.[400]

Before integration, an ADC was a separate and expensive chip. The first reprogrammable microcontrollers had no converter at all, and a design without one had to square the analog signal up with a comparator and feed the microcontroller a digital edge instead.[1054][713] Where a converter exists, it can replace hardware elsewhere: an entire keypad can be read on a single ADC pin by giving each key a different printed resistor divider and mapping the resulting voltage in software.[1282] The same technique reads front-panel key matrices and rotary range switches, and its characteristic failure mode is a dirty contact producing a voltage that wanders and decodes as the wrong key.[1602][1592][1360]

Getting samples out of the converter efficiently is a firmware problem in its own right. On STM32 parts, setting the WAIT bit in the CFGR1 register stops the converter advancing through its channel sequence until the data register has been read, so a slow CPU or late interrupt no longer loses samples — an easy fix to miss in the user guide.[eUA2TRX1NTc] Better still is to ignore the ADC interrupts entirely and let DMA move each completed sample into memory without occupying the CPU, using circular mode so the transfer restarts itself; this both minimises latency between channels and removes the per-sample overhead, and the transfer size can be set to 16 bits so buffers need not be padded out to 32.[eUA2TRX1NTc]

## Driving the converter

An ADC input is not an arbitrary load. A successive-approximation converter cannot be fed from an arbitrarily high input impedance, so voltage buffers ahead of it are not optional decoration.[232] Datasheet limits make this explicit: an analog source resistance of 500 ohms with 2 microamps maximum input leakage means the source must be low impedance to keep errors down.[642] A peak detector is a case in point — the storage capacitor must be followed by an op-amp voltage follower presenting very high impedance to the cap and a low-impedance source to the converter, or the act of measuring droops the value being measured.[490] Signal conditioning should also fill the range: an ECG front end scales and level-shifts the signal so that the 10-bit dynamic range is used to the full, and a microphone preamp gain is chosen with the following converter's dynamic range in mind.[660][611] Layout deserves the same care, and a good converter datasheet gives layout guidance directly or in an application note.[131]

## Oscilloscope acquisition

In a digital oscilloscope the ADC sits immediately behind the front end, fed as a differential pair from the variable-gain amplifier, with length-matched serpentine tracks carrying the sample clock.[1717][976][1260] Eight bits has long been the norm, 256 levels, which becomes visible as individual quantisation steps as soon as a small signal is expanded.[876][594] Ten-bit and then 12-bit converters displaced it, and 14-bit true converters followed; a 14-bit part is not the same thing as a 12-bit converter running in a high-resolution mode that reports up to 18 bits.[1226][983][1638] Building a converter is not the same discipline as building digital logic — the process technology is different, and the parts that do it best are sometimes designed in-house rather than bought.[1124][1638][983]

Channel count is bought with converters. A scope with two dual-channel ADCs runs 1 GSa/s with two channels on and halves to 500 MSa/s with four; deleting one converter in a cheaper model halves the sample rate again, and models that share a single ADC between two channels lose rate when both are on but keep it if channels one and three are used instead.[1042][1355][1503][1510] The reverse trick is interleaving: feeding the same analog signal into both halves of a dual converter with a deliberate skew doubles the effective clock rate in single-channel mode.[790][864] Taken to an extreme, five dual 40 MSa/s converter chips — ten ADCs — staggered at 100 MHz each produce 1 GSa/s, with the parts running well above their marked speed grade.[19][89] Converters at this rate dissipate accordingly; they can be the hottest device on the board, sitting around 74 to 75 °C while the applications processor stays near 55 °C, and 5 GSa/s parts require serious heatsinking.[1146][587] Sample memory must also be sized for the word width — a 12-bit ADC needs two bytes per sample, so a given memory depth costs twice the bytes.[1545]

## Using the converter's range

Because vertical resolution is finite, an oscilloscope measurement is only as good as the fraction of the converter's range the waveform occupies. A signal squeezed into a few divisions wastes converter range and degrades both resolution and the accuracy of automated measurements; using the vernier to bring the waveform just inside full scale gives the measurement more bits to work with and produces a smaller standard deviation.[1228][1226] On scopes with a genuine variable-gain amplifier this is real hardware gain rather than a software trick, and some designs instead apply digital gain inside the ADC chip itself.[1228] DC offset serves the same end from the other direction: shifting a DC-coupled rail down into the converter's window is what lets a power rail probe resolve small ripple on a large standing voltage, and a 12-bit converter then allows a genuine zoom into that detail.[1733][1735][1038] Overrange is unrecoverable — once the front-end amplifier or ADC buffer clips, expanding the vertical scale afterwards cannot recover data that was never captured.[793] The full-scale converter range can be found empirically by increasing amplitude until clipping appears, though the result may reflect where the input amplifier clips rather than the converter itself.[6qjqhnQiQXQ]

Two further effects are easy to misread. The trigger path is a separate analog system from the acquisition path, so a scope can genuinely trigger on an event that never appears on screen because the converter and the trigger circuitry are not seeing the same thing.[1320] Modern designs remove the discrepancy by triggering in the digital domain on the converter output, which also allows a one-button 50 percent trigger level anywhere within the capture window.[685] Separately, what reaches the converter is the bandwidth-limited signal, not the original: a 200 MHz square wave into a 200 MHz front end arrives at the ADC with its harmonics rolled off, closer to a sine wave, which is precisely the condition under which sin x over x interpolation is a mathematically valid reconstruction.[1213]

## Buying resolution with processing

Bits can be manufactured after the fact. High-resolution acquisition mode trades sample rate for vertical resolution, taking an 8-bit converter to nine, ten, twelve or even fourteen effective bits, and it is far more forgiving of artefacts than mathematical averaging.[878] In a spectrum analyser the same principle appears as processing gain: digital down-conversion and discrete Fourier transforms over a narrow resolution bandwidth lower the noise floor by roughly 57 dB, so an 8-bit converter that would ordinarily manage about 50 dB of dynamic range delivers a noise floor near 107 dB.[587] This is what makes it economic to bolt a spectrum analyser onto an oscilloscope: the RF front end feeds the converters already present for the analog channels, and the added hardware is a VCO, some attenuation, switching and amplification for a few tens of dollars.[587]

The all-digital IF follows the same logic. Rather than an analog log amplifier, envelope detector and video filter, the signal after the IF stage is sampled directly with a high-resolution, high-sample-rate converter and everything downstream is done in software inside an FPGA or DSP.[391][1109][892] That converter need not be fast — the mixer and resolution-bandwidth filter deliver something in the order of tens of megahertz — but it must be precise.[391][470] Direct sampling of RF is spreading upward as converters improve, currently reaching single-digit gigahertz, beyond which parts cannot simply be bought and proprietary custom ASIC converters are developed instead.[1032] A 300 MHz IF sampled directly into FPGAs is how a deep space communications complex handles its receive chain.[1547]

## Precision instrumentation

Precision measurement uses a different class of converter entirely. In a 6½-digit bench multimeter the ADC is not an off-the-shelf chip at all — fast 8- and 10-bit parts cannot be cascaded to reach that performance — and the converter is instead built from a multi-slope architecture whose integration capacitor and switching sit outside the logic chip.[485][427][489] Older instruments show the ancestry plainly: a matched pair implementing a 4½-digit dual-slope converter and display driver, or a timer IC operating the conversion against a voltage reference.[406][482] Distinctive external sampling capacitors on an analog board are enough to identify an integrating converter without tracing it.[613] A 17-bit converter is what allows a soldering station to hold temperature accurately without calibration.[1106]

Integration time is a measurement parameter in these instruments, not a post-processing one. Setting the aperture to one mains cycle — 20 ms at 50 Hz — averages mains-frequency noise out inside the converter's own sampling window rather than afterwards, buying accuracy and 50/60 Hz rejection at the cost of measurement speed.[1379] Calibration belongs to the same group of components: what determines a meter's calibration is the voltage reference, the analog-to-digital converter, and the hybrid resistor divider network, so replacing a microcontroller containing an integrated sigma-delta converter can shift the readings while a firmware update alone cannot.[qmcSEJehqZM][0Oo2J66FLZA]

Because it sits at the end of the input chain, the converter is also what the input protection network exists to defend, and protection topologies vary considerably between manufacturers according to how the ADC and its dividers work.[373] Susceptibility shows up the same way: RF conducted into the input jacks can flood the ADC to the point where the reading is meaningless.[987]

## High channel counts and data acquisition

Where many channels must be digitised at once, the converter defines the system architecture. A towed seismic array ran up to 10,000 simultaneously sampled real-time channels of 24-bit conversion, with converters roughly every 30 m across 10 km of cable, the data carried over an E1-protocol ATM fibre optic network and power distributed at 500 V DC.[40][131][500] Multi-channel converter cards for particle physics experiments follow the same pattern, 64 channels to a card with the analog section at the front and the digital interface at the back.[994] At laboratory scale, general-purpose data acquisition hardware combines converters and DACs on one board, with 16-bit conversion at 100 kSa/s or 24-bit conversion for load cells, RTDs and thermocouples.[1190]

Cheaper multi-channel front ends multiplex instead of duplicating. Cell balance voltages in a battery charger are divided down, passed through an analog multiplexer whose channel the microcontroller selects, and fed to a single converter via a low-pass filter; an LCR meter multiplexes its voltage and current measurements into one ADC and computes the rest.[397][757] A charger can also poll sense lines continuously through the converter to detect when a cell has been plugged in.[812]

USB and modular instruments have made 14-bit converters commonplace: 14-bit conversion at 100 to 125 MSa/s appears across several such products, with resolution stated bluntly enough to matter — 340 microvolts per bit means that a 100 microvolt per division setting shows nothing at all.[692][1552][858] Higher-end units offer selectable resolution from 8 to 16 bits, trading sample rate for bits, up to 1 GSa/s in 8-bit single-channel mode at around half a watt.[521] Some combine a 16-bit low-speed converter with an 18-bit high-speed converter and blend the two so a swept measurement crosses the handover without a discontinuity.[1624] These parts are not cheap, and high-speed converters and DACs alongside an FPGA are a large part of what such hardware costs to build.[1596] Their availability is historically contingent: the volumes generated by the cell phone industry are what made fast converters and FPGAs cheap enough that building a digital scope became a realistic proposition.[207]

At the limits, converter noise rather than converter bits sets the outcome. A 14-bit front end with two LSBs of channel noise gives 200 mV of noise at an 800 V full scale, and holding that performance across the entire frequency range is the hard part of the design.[1119]
