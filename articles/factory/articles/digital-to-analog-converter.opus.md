# digital to analog converter

A digital-to-analog converter (DAC) takes a digital number and produces a proportional analog output — usually a voltage, sometimes a current — without any further work by the circuit that fed it.[225] It is the counterpart to the analog-to-digital converter, and the two are almost always found together: a modern instrument digitises what it measures and synthesises what it outputs, so a DAC sits behind almost every generated waveform, control voltage, offset and threshold in the bench.[281][511][1023] Its two headline parameters are resolution, which fixes the size of the smallest output step, and update rate, which fixes how fast those steps can be walked through.

## Resolution and step size

Resolution is stated in bits, and the arithmetic that follows is the whole of it: a 12-bit converter has 4,096 steps, giving just over 1 mV per step across a 0 to 5 V span.[225] A 10-bit device driving a 0 to 20.48 V output through a gain of ten gives 20 mV per bit, which is adequate for a general-purpose bench supply.[259] At the other extreme, an 18-bit part such as the DAC9881 works out at 38.146 µV per bit,[802] and a 20-bit unbuffered voltage-output part like the AD5791 reaches 1 ppm resolution and 1 ppm integral non-linearity while operating from bipolar supplies of up to 33 V.[500]

Because step size depends on both bit count and output span, the two must always be quoted together.[1560] Eight bits is coarse enough to be visible: a generated waveform driven from an 8-bit DAC shows obvious staircase structure on a scope.[1008] The quantisation steps of a source DAC can be resolved directly by a sufficiently low-noise 12-bit oscilloscope,[1501] and even an analog scope will show the step response of the DAC inside a digital function generator.[196]

## Against PWM

For the common job of letting a microcontroller set a voltage, the DAC competes with pulse-width modulation. A multi-turn potentiometer costs five to ten dollars, a dedicated DAC around two dollars and up, while a PWM module is effectively free because most microcontrollers already contain one and only a resistor and capacitor are needed to filter the output.[225] Historically the deciding factor was that most microcontrollers had no DAC on board at all.[225][221] For a given number of bits the two approaches give identical voltage resolution — a PWM-based DAC is a digital-to-analog converter that happens to use the PWM technique.[225] A rotary encoder or a microcontroller port can drive either equally well.[225]

The case for an external converter is resolution and simplicity: where an on-chip PWM peripheral offers only 10 bits, an external 12-bit DAC paired with an external 12-bit ADC buys more resolution than the microcontroller can manage on its own.[232] The situation has since changed, with 12-bit dual-channel DACs appearing in mainstream microcontrollers,[900] including parts whose internal DAC is judged good enough for a non-precision programmable supply,[1561] a 4 Msample/s update-rate DAC adequate for a handheld function generator,[1723] and 10-bit DACs alongside ADCs and PWM in ARM microcontrollers of modest cost.[571]

## Interfacing

Discrete DACs are fed over a serial bus, typically SPI or I²C.[225] The choice has board-level consequences: moving from an SPI DAC to an I²C DAC in one power-supply design freed three microcontroller pins and cost slightly less.[259] Where an SPI DAC and SPI ADC coexist, the clock and data lines can be shared and only the chip-select lines duplicated.[232]

Converter families are often deliberately software-compatible across resolutions. A 16-bit-wide command word is sent regardless of whether the part is 8, 10 or 12 bits, with the device simply ignoring the surplus least significant bits — four on the 8-bit part, two on the 10-bit part — so the same firmware drives any member of the family.[240] The output is likewise always referenced to full scale, 4,096, whatever the device's actual resolution.[240] The corollary is that the word length is not optional: a converter fed only 14 of the required 16 bits does not recognise the transfer as a valid command and produces no output at all.[240] Other failure modes at this interface are mundane and worth checking before the converter is blamed — a dead clock line,[240] or a chip-select driven through an I²C I/O expander that has itself stopped working.[240]

For waveform output, feeding the DAC from software in a busy loop makes a good sample rate hard to achieve. The standard technique is to hold the waveform in a lookup table and let a DMA controller traverse it, optionally triggered by a timer for a defined sample interval, which frees the CPU entirely and runs the converter at its maximum data rate.[eUA2TRX1NTc] Where DMA is shared with less critical traffic such as an LCD refresh, the DAC transfer is given the higher priority, since a momentary pause in the screen update is harmless while a gap in the waveform is not.[eUA2TRX1NTc]

## Resolution is not accuracy

The distinction between resolution and accuracy applies to DACs exactly as it does to multimeters.[225] A 12-bit converter genuinely steps its output in 1.22 mV increments,[225] but the absolute value of those steps is set by the reference, the gain error and the offset error. In one measured case a nominal 0.5003 V output came out at 0.495 V, with a second channel of the same device reading 0.4973 V from identical data; the data sheet's typical gain error of −0.1 % of full scale plus an offset error of up to 20 mV at the low end accounts for it.[240] The blunt summary of that result: "So much for our precision DAC."[240] Probing directly on the converter pins and on the reference input pins rules out voltage drop elsewhere on the board as the cause.[240]

The reference is therefore part of the converter. A single 2.048 V reference commonly feeds both the DAC and the ADC in a design,[259] and its own tolerance bounds what the converter can do.[240] Some DACs carry a programmable internal reference with no provision for an external one, in which case the internal zener sets the ceiling on performance.[440][1248] Layout matters too: keeping the ADCs and DACs on a signal-ground region carrying no load current puts them all at the same potential.[244] Where a DAC sets a precision offset, a low-drift reference is required to support it.[1744]

The analog circuitry after the converter must also suit it. A DAC swinging from 0 V to 2.048 V into an op-amp demands an input stage that works down to the negative rail, which not every dual-supply op-amp provides.[238] Current-output DACs need an I-to-V stage — a resistor network followed by a current-to-voltage amplifier in an arbitrary waveform generator,[805] or a pair of op-amps behind a dual current-output part.[391]

## Waveform generation

Almost all modern function generators are direct digital synthesis instruments: the waveform is a digital construct converted by a DAC, as distinct from a true analog RF signal generator.[823] Sixteen bits is the usual resolution,[1560] and the converters used are among the most expensive parts in the instrument. A dual-channel 16-bit part running at 1.2 Gsample/s costs around $60 in 2,000-piece quantity and dissipates about 1.5 W on its own at full rate;[805] a four-channel arbitrary generator uses 2.5 Gsample/s 14-to-16-bit converters, with the higher-bandwidth channels each given a dedicated DAC and the lower-specification channels sharing one.[1679][1679] Even so, the output drivers can cost more than the converters: twelve 900 MHz current-feedback amplifiers at $7 each exceed the DAC spend in one such instrument, because a DAC cannot drive a 50 Ω output directly.[1679]

Supporting circuitry follows from the data rate. Traces from the FPGA to the DAC are length-matched,[1431][497] the clock source is a dedicated low-noise VCO with its own low-dropout regulator because analog performance depends on it,[497] and anti-aliasing filters follow the converter output.[805]

The same converters appear in oscilloscopes with built-in generators — a 14-bit DAC904E is a typical choice[864][1042] — though in some designs the DAC is absorbed into the instrument's main ASIC and has no visible presence on the board.[1639] USB and modular instruments follow the pattern: 14-bit ADC and DAC pairs at 100 to 125 Msample/s,[692][875][858] with an output amplifier giving roughly ±5 V,[1552] and the end-to-end bandwidth of about 10 MHz set by the whole chain including both converters.[692]

An incidental consequence of DDS architecture is that any generator with a DC or DC-offset mode is a high-resolution programmable voltage source. With 16 bits over a ±10 V span, adjustment in 0.1 mV steps is possible, which is finer than the 1 mV granularity often assumed.[1560][1560] Absolute accuracy is the limitation, since DC output is not the instrument's purpose and the internal voltage standards are not exceptional; the technique suits fine adjustment rather than absolute setting.[1560]

## Housekeeping and control roles

Much of the DAC population in test equipment does unglamorous work. Offset control is the most common: quad 12-bit voltage-output parts for the offsets of a handheld scope,[810] a dense octal part with a 5 ppm/°C on-chip reference doing offset duty in another,[1038] small DACs scattered around a high-speed scope's front end,[1545] and an offset-nulling DAC inside a high-voltage differential probe.[1744][1631] Logic analysers of mid to high grade use a DAC to make the input threshold adjustable, typically over a range of about −5 V to +5 V, in place of fixed logic-family presets.[44][1018]

Programmable sources are the other large category. A bench supply's control loop needs only a reference for its error amplifier, and that reference can equally come from a DAC or a potentiometer.[861] A multi-output supply may give each channel its own processor, reference, ADC and DAC.[511] Electronic loads pair a DAC to set the demanded value with an ADC to read it back, alongside the MOSFETs and sense resistors;[281] a fully digital load implements constant-current, constant-resistance and constant-power modes in software and therefore needs a fast DAC and ADC,[862] often 16-bit parts matched to each other.[862] The same approach makes a home-built load practical from little more than a DAC, a FET and an op-amp,[393] and feeding the measured voltage back to a microcontroller that recomputes the DAC setting on the fly turns a constant-current load into a constant-resistance or constant-power one.[140] Source-measure units and 6½-digit-class instruments use 16-bit multiplying DACs in the same role.[718]

Where the output voltages need not be precise, 8 bits is enough: a universal device programmer uses an 8-bit quad DAC to set the various programming voltages up to 12 V or 25 V,[371][371] and a high-end programmer with per-pin clamp and current-source control uses 12-bit parts.[1060] Other appearances include the calibration DAC in an oscillator-oven upgrade, whose absence leaves only a trimpot adjustment,[647][647] a DAC driving the inverting input of a femtoammeter's front end for burden-voltage calibration offset,[1755] a 12-bit DAC in a 4-20 mA process-control transmitter,[DX_f0Cg6pHo] and an R-2R ladder of only three bits inside an uncommitted logic array, used to switch the gain of an op-amp in a current- and temperature-sense loop.[501]

## Video and audio

Video output is a classic DAC application: a three-channel part such as the ADV7125 driving a VGA display is what an FPGA-based display controller uses,[145] and a simple FPGA VGA demonstration can be built either with a DAC or bodged from resistors.[711]

Audio DACs are specified by dynamic range rather than by bit count alone — a stereo audio DAC with 102 dB signal-to-noise ratio and sample rates to 96 kHz is representative of portable recorder hardware.[271] Integrated codecs bundle DACs and ADCs with a digital mixer, an I²S interface and on-chip DSP,[ag-MjKAfATw][1405] and digital mixing consoles replace analog pots and op-amps with converter pairs feeding a DSP engine.[738] An unusual historical variant is a floating-point audio DAC with a 10-digit mantissa and a 3-digit exponent, giving 16-bit dynamic range.[256]

One implication of a digital audio chain is often ignored in marketing: between a memory card and the DAC the signal is nothing but ones and zeros, so a product whose output is affected by the card's radiated emissions is a product that has been designed incorrectly.[719]

## Selection and cost

Parametric search on distributor catalogues is the practical way to choose a converter, filtering on resolution, channel count, package, interface and internal reference.[232][1180] In one design the cheapest 12-bit four-channel part in an eight-pin package came out at $2.40 in 100-off, with the nearest alternative from another vendor unable to meet the channel-count and package constraints; the matching DAC from the same vendor was likewise the cheapest option.[232][232] Microchip's analog line is a recurring choice for this kind of part on grounds of low power, low price and availability.[5]

At the top of the market the picture inverts. The high-speed, high-resolution converters in mixed-signal instruments are genuinely expensive chips that make a self-built equivalent uneconomic in low or medium volume,[1596] and the most costly catalogue converters run into thousands of dollars each.[1435] Between those extremes, the cheapness of a converter is often the point: bare 8-bit DACs in bank-note acceptors[525] and 12-bit DACs on Raspberry Pi expansion boards[735][979] exist because the function has become nearly free.
