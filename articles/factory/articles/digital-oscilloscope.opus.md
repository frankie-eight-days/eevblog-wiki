# digital oscilloscope

A digital oscilloscope samples an input waveform, stores the samples in memory, and then draws them on a display, in contrast to an analog oscilloscope that traces the signal continuously onto a phosphor CRT as it arrives.[601][86] Both instruments do the same underlying job — displaying voltage against time — and the controls map onto one another closely enough that the concepts carry straight across.[926][UJjMt2-k99c][159] The crucial difference is storage: because the waveform exists as data rather than as light on a screen, it can be frozen, zoomed, measured, and analysed after the fact, and single events that occur only once can be captured at all.[926]

## What storage buys

Pressing stop freezes the acquisition, and the captured record remains available for zooming in and out and for detailed analysis even after the probe has been removed from the circuit.[926] A 1 Hz signal that an analog scope can only paint as a slowly moving dot stays on a digital screen permanently.[926] Single-shot mode arms the instrument to wait for an event that happens once, and provided the trigger is set up correctly the event is captured and held — something an analog scope cannot do.[926] Digital storage also gives pre-trigger and post-trigger data, since the instrument is sampling continuously and the trigger merely marks a position within the record.[159]

Storage is the dominant consideration when choosing an instrument on a budget: a very low-end digital scope capable of single-shot capture is more useful than a top-of-the-line analog scope with dual time bases and high bandwidth.[1536] Roll mode is a further digital-only display behaviour, in which the trace does not retrace at all but scrolls continuously like a chart recorder.[UJjMt2-k99c]

## Automatic measurement

Modern digital scopes compute measurements from the stored record rather than requiring them to be read off a graticule: peak-to-peak voltage, average value, frequency, and rise and fall time among many others.[926] They can calculate true RMS mathematically, agreeing with a multimeter's reading to within a small error — 1.74 V against 1.79 V in one direct comparison.[926] Cursors can be placed on the waveform and the instrument reports the exact time between them, removing the need to count divisions by eye.[926] Phase difference between channels is likewise an automatic measurement on most modern digital scopes, found under vertical measurements despite having nothing to do with vertical voltage.[1751] Because these functions are essentially software, features that a digital scope lacks are often absent by omission rather than by any hardware limitation — XY mode operating on channel differences such as 1 − 2 and 3 − 4 is one such function that ought to be possible but is hard to find implemented.[1137]

## The apparent-noise question

Digital scopes have a persistent reputation for being noisier than analog scopes, and it is not correct.[601][878][442] A digital scope samples the signal and then displays it, so any noise present is captured in the acquisition and drawn.[601] An analog scope averages by brightness: noise that is uncorrelated with the sweep and the trigger appears dim or not at all, because those photons strike any given part of the phosphor too rarely to be visible.[601] The hidden content can be recovered from an analog display by photographing the screen at a long shutter speed, which reveals noise superimposed on the waveform that the eye could not see.[601]

The practical consequence is that the analog scope is the misleading instrument. High-frequency content genuinely present on the signal is hidden by phosphor persistence, and the digital scope is better at showing what is actually there.[601][610] Common-mode noise in particular is easy to pick up on a digital scope and easy to miss entirely on an analog one.[442] Switching noise measured on both instruments has the same amplitude; the digital scope simply displays it more readily by virtue of its sampling and its greater effective persistence.[442]

No fewer than six mechanisms, quite apart from the genuine noise floor of the input channel and its ADC, affect how much noise appears on a digital display: memory depth, analog bandwidth, boxcar averaging, the intensity-graded display, the waveform update rate, and the sample rate.[610] Turning the intensity control down on a digital scope makes noise vanish in the same way it does on an analog scope, and turning it back up makes it reappear — the data was there throughout.[442] Counter-intuitively, a slow-updating low-end scope shows more of this noise than a fast-updating one with variable persistence.[442] Intensity-graded displays exist precisely to simulate the analog scope's brightness weighting, and with the grading set to 100% a digital scope behaves like an old-school display with none.[601][610] Understanding these mechanisms is a prerequisite for using the instrument properly; the noise myth persists mostly among users who do not.[610]

Digital scopes do have one genuine noise disadvantage: they can radiate interference of their own — screen refresh switching, for instance, couples into a coax held in the hand — which corrupts low-level measurements in a way an analog scope will not.[441]

## Vertical resolution and acquisition modes

Most digital scopes use an eight-bit converter, and this is the clearest area in which the analog scope retains an advantage.[594][878] When a signal with large dynamic range is expanded — for example at 500 µV per division on a math trace — the individual quantisation steps of the converter become visible on screen.[594]

High-resolution mode, also called boxcar averaging, trades sample rate for effective resolution, yielding nine, ten, twelve or even as much as fourteen bits depending on the sampling settings.[878] It is distinct from mathematical averaging mode, and both are found under the acquire menu on essentially any modern digital scope alongside normal and peak-detect modes.[878] High-resolution mode is the more forgiving of the two.[878] Its absence is a real deficiency in an instrument — a scope offering only regular average mode and no high-resolution mode is limited in what it can clean up.[824] Where the mode is implemented in software rather than hardware, the waveform update rate suffers noticeably while it is engaged.[594]

## Triggering

Trigger holdoff works on a digital scope exactly as it does on an analog one, minus the physical retrace period, though there may still be processing time before the instrument can re-arm.[159] All modern digital scopes provide a holdoff setting in the trigger setup menu; a typical minimum default value is 500 ns, and raising it to roughly 45 µs is what allows a burst waveform with that much dead time between packets to trigger stably.[159] AC trigger coupling likewise behaves the same on digital instruments as on analog ones.[685] With DC trigger coupling the level has to be reset by hand for every new signal probed; AC trigger coupling allows a single zero-level trigger point to work across a wide range of input signals with differing DC offsets.[685]

## Bandwidth and front-end response

The classic rise-time relation of 0.35 divided by bandwidth applies to Gaussian-response instruments, which is to say traditional analog CRT scopes.[306] Digital scopes do not necessarily have a Gaussian response on their analog input channels; they typically use a maximally flat response, and the applicable constant is somewhat higher — roughly 0.4 divided by bandwidth, with the exact figure depending on the roll-off of the analog front-end filtering.[306]

## Construction

The digital front end downstream of the analog input is largely commodity. The HMCAD1511 ADC from Analog Devices appears in a large proportion of scopes on the market, and the manufacturer's typical applications list names digital oscilloscopes directly.[1038] Around the converter, the design is close to off-the-shelf application-note material; what a manufacturer must actually do itself is the analog front end and the FPGA work — the phosphor-emulation and sampling technology.[1038] Parts such as the 12-bit AD9235, offered in speed grades from 20 up to 65 megasamples per second, are similarly marketed for ultrasound equipment and low-cost digital oscilloscopes.[1109][892]

## Practical selection

Waveform update rate, expressed in waveforms per second, is a separate axis from responsiveness of the user interface; an instrument can feel fast to operate while being slow in updates per second.[3jDRH-6IvZc] Update rate is what determines whether an infrequent glitch is caught, and this is the case in which the digital instrument's superiority over analog is unambiguous — an analog display cannot accumulate enough photons from a rare event to make it visible, and turning up the intensity to extend persistence only goes so far.[3jDRH-6IvZc]

Memory depth on early digital scopes was measured in a couple of kilobytes, which is the main reason vintage digital instruments such as the HP 54600 series are of limited use today.[1022][809] LeCroy pioneered much of the digital scope art, with large sample memories, fast acquisition rates and high analog bandwidths, alongside Tektronix and Agilent.[217] Modern low-end digital scopes offer very large memory by comparison — 14 megapoints as standard on one representative instrument — though a large feature list does not guarantee competence in every function; FFT performance in particular can be inadequate on scopes that are otherwise strong value.[845]

Instrument-to-instrument consistency in the user interface is high enough that familiarity with one digital scope transfers to most others within about ten minutes of use.[474] Update rate and general performance nevertheless vary enormously between models at similar price points, and a sluggish instrument is a poor purchase regardless of its specification sheet.[480][1218] For a given bandwidth requirement such as 1 GHz, second-hand or refurbished instruments offer considerably better value than new ones.[1218]

An analog scope still earns bench space alongside a digital one for its higher update rate, lower noise, and ability to show signals that a low-end digital scope cannot capture.[51][196][200] Some instruments provide both: the Fluke combiscope switches between analog and digital modes, showing sample points, memory usage and record position when in digital mode.[3jDRH-6IvZc][1450]

The claim that analog scopes are hard to use and digital ones easy does not survive scrutiny — used in manual mode the two are operationally equivalent, and a case can be made that a well-laid-out analog scope is the easier instrument, the auto-set button on modern scopes notwithstanding.[86] Techniques often assumed to require a digital instrument, such as Bode plotting with a sweep generator, work on an ordinary analog scope as well.[396]
