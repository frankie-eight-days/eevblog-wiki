# current shunt resistor

A current shunt resistor is a low- or precisely known-value resistor placed in series with a current path so that the current can be determined by measuring the resulting voltage drop and applying Ohm's law.[72][929] It is the near-universal basis of current measurement: a multimeter's current ranges, a power supply's current-limit loop, an electronic load, and a smart energy meter all reduce to a shunt resistor and something that measures the voltage across it.[929][1381][1688] Its value is a fundamental engineering trade-off, because the same voltage drop that constitutes the measurement signal also subtracts from the circuit under test (burden voltage) and dissipates power as heat.[232][409]

## Operating principle and value selection

The measured quantity is simply V = IR, so the shunt value is chosen to give a convenient scaling between current and voltage.[72] For a direct 1 mV-per-unit mapping, 1 mV per amp requires 1 mΩ, 1 mV per milliamp requires 1 Ω, 1 mV per microamp requires 1 kΩ, and 1 mV per nanoamp requires 1 MΩ.[72] Choosing values that preserve a matching relationship between millivolts and milliamps avoids any conversion in the readout.[72] The shunt value also determines the voltage dropped from the circuit under test; with a 0.1 Ω shunt, for example, a 2 A load produces only a 0.4 V drop.[232]

Power dissipation follows I²R and frequently drives the design downward in value. A 100 A smart meter phase limited to 2 W of shunt dissipation requires a shunt of only about 200 µΩ — so impractical that such meters use current transformers instead.[409] At the other extreme, a 1 kΩ shunt suitable for microamp resolution would drop 100 V if the circuit under test suddenly drew 100 mA on waking from sleep, which is why no single shunt can cover microamps in the presence of amps and ranging is mandatory.[1190]

## Burden voltage

Burden voltage is the voltage dropped across a current-measuring instrument's terminals, comprising the shunt resistor's drop plus the resistance of the internal fuse and any switch contacts.[929][1604] In a typical multimeter the shunt produces about 50 mV at full scale on each range, which may appear on a datasheet as a maximum burden-voltage specification, but ranges that share a shunt across two full-scale currents can reach 500 mV.[929] Near full scale the drop grows, and in low-voltage circuits — a 3.3 V or 1.2 V logic rail — a half-volt or volt of burden can disturb or shut down the circuit being measured.[929][i069j6S80OU] In one demonstration, switching from a low-burden adapter to a meter's internal shunt raised the burden enough to change the behaviour of the circuit under test.[204]

The fuse contributes as much as the shunt: a 600 mA fuse is about 1 Ω, and on a 50 mA range a roughly 2 Ω fuse dominates over a 1 Ω shunt, so reducing the shunt value alone does not fix the burden on ranges routed through a small fuse.[929][121]

The standard remedy is a much lower shunt value followed by a precision amplifier. Dropping each shunt by an order of magnitude and adding a ×10 amplifier, or by two orders of magnitude with a ×100 amplifier, preserves the output scaling while cutting the burden proportionally; with ×100 gain, 10 mΩ, 10 Ω, and 10 kΩ shunts give 1 mV per mA, µA, and nA respectively with a 100× lower burden than a standard multimeter.[72][929] The amplifier's input offset voltage becomes the limiting error, which is why low-offset chopper amplifiers (e.g. the MAX4238/MAX4239 class, with offsets in the tens of microvolts) are used.[1328][232] A design guideline is to take the largest tolerable shunt drop and the lowest amplifier gain, since pushing gain higher to compensate for a tiny shunt pushes the measurement down into the noise.[232] The offset can coincide with the smallest signal of interest: 500 µA of resolution across a 0.2 Ω shunt is 100 µV, the same order as the input offset of a typical current-sense amplifier.[232]

## Shunts in multimeters

A typical handheld multimeter uses a 100 Ω shunt for the microamp ranges, a 1 Ω shunt for the milliamp ranges, and a 10 mΩ shunt for the 5 A/10 A ranges, selected by the range switch and the input jack.[929][853] Values are often combined so ranges sum to round numbers: a 0.99 Ω resistor in series with the 10 mΩ amp shunt gives a 1 Ω milliamp shunt, and the Fluke 27 uses a 4.995 Ω resistor in series with the 5 mΩ amp shunt to total exactly 5 Ω.[929][373] Manufacturers commonly mark low values with the "R" replacing the decimal point — 0R01 for 10 mΩ, R010, R100 for 0.1 Ω.[929][973][1174]

The 10 A range is limited by shunt dissipation and self-heating, which is why meters often allow only a 20 A peak for about 20 seconds before a cool-down is required.[929] Current accuracy is correspondingly hardest to maintain on the 10 A range, and datasheets typically show a worse current-accuracy figure there than on the lower ranges.[75] Because shunts three orders of magnitude apart can be left in series or parallel without materially affecting each other, cascaded decade chains (10 mΩ, 100 mΩ, 1 Ω, 10 Ω, 100 Ω) can be MOSFET-switched so that every range presents the same 50 mV burden with no amplifier needed, provided the sense amplifier's input impedance is high enough that upstream resistors in the tap path drop no voltage.[931]

## Protection

A fuse takes time to blow, and a gross overload can destroy the shunt resistor first. Multimeters therefore clamp the voltage across the current input with a diode bridge — sometimes a bridge plus additional series diodes — limiting the shunt voltage to a few volts so the fuse clears first.[373][376] In the Fluke 27 the clamp totals about 3.6 V, which across the 5 Ω milliamp shunt dissipates only about 2.5 W in a 4 W resistor, comfortably survivable for the fuse's clearing time.[376] The same protection philosophy separates the current jack with isolation slots and HRC fuses in CAT-rated meters.[373][853]

## Construction, tolerance, and tempco

Shunt construction ranges from a bent length of nichrome resistance wire in an ordinary handheld meter, through copper straps soldered at an exact point to set the value, hand-wound and heat-shrunk elements in analog meters, and tapped or notched adjustment mechanisms, to precision four-terminal SMD parts.[929][1097][634][745] Absolute tolerance is often unimportant because instruments calibrate the gain out in software; 1% shunts are common even in precision gear, and what actually matters is low temperature coefficient and low drift.[511][613][655][731] Keeping self-heating low also keeps tempco errors low: paralleling ten resistors to share dissipation (e.g. ten 10 Ω parts for a 1 Ω shunt, giving 5–10 W capability) means even low-grade resistors barely change with temperature, and provides survival margin if range-switching software ever leaves the full output current on the small shunt.[232][259] Precision low-value parts are disproportionately expensive: a 0.1% 10 mΩ shunt runs about $4–5 in thousand-piece volume, and 0.5% in a four-terminal package may be the best off-the-shelf option.[562][72]

At milliohm values the shunt resistance is a significant fraction of PCB trace, switch, and contact resistance, so the voltage must be sensed directly at the resistor junctions — the four-terminal (Kelvin) connection, with dedicated sense taps on the element itself.[72][931] Kelvin shunts and four-wire sense pairs appear in power supplies, soldering stations, electronic loads, and SMUs for exactly this reason.[814][655][472][1023][607] Specialised milliohm meters exist to verify such parts in or out of circuit.[1649]

## High-side versus low-side sensing

A shunt in the supply line (high-side) requires a differential or dedicated current-sense amplifier (e.g. the MAX4080 family with fixed gains of 5, 20, or 60) that tolerates the common-mode voltage and outputs a ground-referenced voltage.[232] A shunt in the ground return (low-side) is simpler to amplify but introduces a current-dependent offset between the load's ground and true ground, which is generally considered undesirable in power-supply outputs.[232][222] With 1 Ω shunts, a modest precision op-amp gives milliamp-class accuracy, but at 0.1 Ω general-purpose and even precision op-amps have too much offset and a proper instrumentation or current-sense amplifier is required.[222] Cheap differential-amplifier approaches built from jellybean op-amps are workable where cost dominates, as in some USB power supplies.[1561]

## In power supplies and constant-current circuits

The current-limit loop of a bench supply is an op-amp comparing the shunt voltage against a reference, driving the series pass transistor so the drop stays constant; the constant output current is then simply I = Vref ÷ Rshunt.[1688] A 1 V reference across a 1 Ω shunt regulates 1 A.[1688] The same trick turns an LM317 into a constant-current source, since its 1.25 V internal reference divided by the series shunt sets the current.[1688] Practical shunts in the pass path are very low (10–100 mΩ) to avoid dissipation in normal operation.[1688] Shunt voltage also eats regulator headroom: a 1 Ω shunt drops a volt per amp, which must be added to the pass element's dropout when sizing the pre-regulator tracking differential, though powering a regulator's control pin from the downstream side of the shunt can recover some margin.[260]

## Other measurement uses

- **In-circuit current checks**: measuring the voltage across an existing series resistor (e.g. a 1 Ω current-limiting resistor) yields current without breaking the circuit — a standard troubleshooting shortcut.[804]
- **Impedance and LCR analysis**: an impedance analyzer measures the generator voltage and the voltage across the device under test in series with a known shunt, deriving complex impedance from the two.[858][859][wjMIsM4sDw8] Component curve tracers use the same principle — a function generator through a series shunt with an XY oscilloscope display.[1137]
- **Battery and energy measurement**: watt-hour and amp-hour capacity rigs place a shunt (usually low-side) with the load.[141]
- **Femto/picoamp measurement**: the same principle scales upward in resistance — a 250 GΩ shunt serves a 2 pA range, with decade-spaced shunts for each range.[1017]
- **Analog meters**: the lowest current range of an analog multimeter is the meter movement itself with no shunt; a 50 µA movement corresponds to the classic 20 kΩ/V sensitivity.[1013]

## Failure modes

Shunt resistors fail open under overload: a 1 Ω shunt that has taken several amps of fault current can measure kilohms afterwards, while the surrounding circuit survives.[233] Deliberate protection design (diode clamps, adequate fuselet clearing, oversized or paralleled elements) exists precisely because the shunt is often the most thermally stressed precision part in the front end.[373][232] Intermittent or poor connections in current paths are likewise exposed by measuring across the shunt rather than breaking the loop.[777][1672]
