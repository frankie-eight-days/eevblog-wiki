# short circuit

A short circuit is an unintended low-resistance connection between two nodes that are meant to be at different potentials — most commonly between a power rail and ground, or between adjacent conductors on a board. Because it bypasses the intended load, a short allows a source to deliver essentially its full current capability: NiMH cells of ordinary AA size can supply many amps into a short circuit[1200], and shorting a load dissipates all available power in the source's own internal resistance, heating or destroying the source unless protection is present[1401]. Consequences range from a supply harmlessly shutting down, through overheated components, to open fire[398][1037].

## The short circuit in circuit theory

In ideal circuit analysis the short circuit is a defined element, not a fault. An ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance, so when applying the superposition theorem, each deactivated voltage source is replaced with a short circuit, while a current source is replaced with an open circuit[820]. The Thévenin equivalent resistance of a network is found by dividing its open-circuit voltage by its short-circuit current: with the output shorted, the entire source current flows through the short and none through the external resistance, giving the short-circuit current directly[1399]. In symmetric networks such as the resistor cube, nodes that are at equal potential can be deliberately treated as shorted together — or alternatively opened — to simplify the analysis, and both approaches give the same answer[1472].

Component behaviour also invokes the concept: an uncharged capacitor at time zero behaves as a short circuit, which is why capacitance near a switch conducts the instant power is applied[1439], and the zero position of a decade resistance box is implemented by short-circuiting the whole series chain of resistors to give zero ohms[1586]. A ×1 oscilloscope probe works by switching in a short across the 9 MΩ resistor and compensation capacitor of a ×10 network, leaving a direct connection from tip to scope input[453]. At the other extreme, very low-value inductors approach a short circuit closely enough that some multimeter-based inductance measurements stop working[1465].

## As a component failure mode

Many common components fail preferentially to a short. Multilayer ceramic capacitors that crack through their internal plates very often fail short circuit rather than open; an open failure merely kills the product, but a shorted MLCC placed across a power source capable of delivering substantial energy can ignite[1037]. The cracking is driven by mechanical stress — for example, torque from terminal screws rigidly soldered near the capacitor can produce a micro-crack that later fails short and burns the board[1036][1035]. Tantalum capacitors similarly develop internal shorts and can catch fire or explode, particularly with pulsed input[1061]. Film (metallised polyester) capacitors more commonly fail short circuit than by losing capacitance[379], and in some constructions an incorrect conductive epoxy can produce a short between terminations[9V99J22aiLE].

Semiconductors fail the same way: a shorted rectifier diode can dump a power rail into an analogue rail and take out downstream circuitry[538], and a spike on a supply rail can short multiple ICs on that rail simultaneously, leaving the rail at fractions of an ohm[405][398]. A failed lithium cell can measure under an ohm internally, shorting the cell and defeating its protection circuitry[1613]. Even powered-down equipment can present a short: a faulty electronic load measured under an ohm across its input terminals while switched off, repeatedly tripping the source's resettable overcurrent protection[-ir61ARd5T4].

## Causes in manufacture and assembly

Solder bridges between adjacent leads are a routine assembly defect, and automated optical inspection explicitly checks lead banks for shorts between leads[684]; bridges also survive into shipped products[1511]. Wave soldering of boards carrying tall through-hole parts is arranged to prevent short circuits between long protruding leads[684]. Other typical production causes include metal strands from braided cable bridging nearby pins[1010], ragged copper left when castellated holes are routed at the board edge[1681], copper pours brought out to a routed board edge where they can short against the surrounding metalwork[470], and exposed through-hole pins touching a metal chassis when a board flexes[593]. Solder burrs on battery terminals sitting close to a metal enclosure are enough to short the terminals to the case[1646]. Cutting apart panelised PCBs at mouse-bite breakaways with side cutters can accidentally short tracks that pass between the break points[552].

Design tools are expected to catch the schematic-level equivalents: electrical rules checking should flag two output pins shorted together or an output shorted to a power net as an error[5eJorvyA708], and boundary-scan testing on assembled boards verifies shorts and opens by writing a value to a pin and reading it back — a pin shorted to ground cannot be driven high[499].

## Consequences and protection

A short on a rail commonly drags the rail down so that the power supply current-limits or shuts down entirely, which is itself a diagnostic symptom[1061][398]. A supply in constant-current mode delivers no more than its set limit even into a dead short, so shorting the output of a current-limited bench supply or a purpose-built constant current source is harmless[1688]; consumer designs apply the same principle, with recessed terminals and built-in current limiting to tens of milliamps so that shorted output pads do no damage[205].

Where energy is not limited, shorts start fires. A coolant leak that caused a short circuit in an electronic component was identified as the root cause of a grid-scale battery installation fire[1422][1568], and decoupling capacitors shorting out have ignited PCB laminates, exploiting the fact that FR-4's flammability rating only limits, rather than prevents, burning[993]. Charring on a PCB is itself dangerous: carbonised board material is conductive, so a board that has arced once can turn from open to dead short on the next power-up even after apparently being repaired[682].

Batteries require particular care because their internal construction places opposite terminals very close together. In alkaline cells the positive terminal connects to the metal can, so the internal lead must be insulated to avoid shorting to the case[518], and cells in a 9 V battery must keep their insulating wrap so the positive cases of adjacent cells do not short together[2Y1tVvllklc]. A metal-cased NiMH cell can short if the outer insulation is pierced, releasing many amps from a high-capacity cell[1200], and clamping thin metal around a cell — relying only on the cell's wrapper to separate cut metal edges from the terminals — is an accident waiting to happen[751]. Shorting can also be a deliberate safety measure: explosive bridgewire assemblies are made safe by twisting the leads together into a dead short and grounding them, so no stray current can pass through the element[BUW6h88weXU].

## Locating shorts

The standard diagnosis of a shorted rail is a milliohm-level resistance measurement: a 3.3 V rail reading 0.11 Ω with either probe polarity — ruling out a semiconductor junction — confirms a solid short[398], and readings of 0.1–0.2 Ω on a 5 V rail similarly confirm one[1061]. Resolution matters: a 6½-digit multimeter with sharp probes can locate a short spatially, because the measured resistance falls as the probes approach the fault, a technique used to find hundreds of shorts on freshly wave-soldered boards where pins short to ground planes[717ampROkvc]. Comparing readings at several points on a board triangulates the fault — the point with the lowest resistance is nearest the short, and equidistant points read the same[398][1061][230].

When resistance alone is ambiguous, current-based methods apply. Feeding a current through the shorted net and tracing the magnetic field with a current probe follows the fault path even inside a multilayer board, where shorts in vias or on internal planes are otherwise notoriously difficult to find[296]. Cooling the board changes the fault current and can make a marginal short vanish temporarily, confirming the location[401], and thermal imaging reveals the shorted component as a hot spot when the rail is driven[401]. A cruder last resort is to force a heavy current — at the same voltage, from a high-current supply — across the short to burn it open or produce smoke and heat that identify the failed component; this can restore a board to operation without fixing the underlying cause[230][398]. On newly assembled boards, the routine precaution is to buzz out each power rail with a continuity check before first power-up[1306].

## Bench hazards

Creating an accidental short is one of the most common ways to damage equipment during measurement and repair. Probes slip on fine-pitch IC pins and bridge adjacent pins, potentially destroying circuitry that was previously intact; probing external passive components instead of the pins themselves, or using hook-type clips, avoids this[1322][1243][749]. On flat-flex connectors, probing from the recessed rear of the contact rather than the exposed front prevents the probe slipping across two pins[670], and crocodile clips should not be attached to pin headers where they can bridge neighbouring pins[1493].

Test instruments themselves can be the short. A multimeter on its current ranges is essentially a short across its terminals — about 0.2 Ω on the amps range and a few ohms on the milliamp ranges — so measuring a voltage while still connected for current is potentially destructive[1636]; pocket multimeters with fixed leads and current ranges are disfavoured precisely because the range can be selected accidentally[1083]. Soldering carries its own risks: a mains-earth-referenced soldering iron touched to the positive terminal of an in-circuit battery shorts the battery straight to earth and can destroy calibration data[427], and cutting both battery wires at once with metal cutters shorts the cell — leads should be cut one at a time[930][1613]. Other documented causes include a screwdriver bridging a terminal tab while tightening connectors on a live board[686], a metal temperature probe slipping between a diode and a capacitor and releasing the magic smoke[1122], exposed soldered-through pads shorting supply rails when the board is placed on a conductive surface[1483], and probes slipping while measuring a high-capacity battery[1636].
