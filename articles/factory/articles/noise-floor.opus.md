# noise floor

The noise floor is the residual signal an instrument or circuit produces with no input, and it sets the smallest thing that can usefully be measured: a signal buried beneath it cannot be recovered, and a signal near it is contaminated by it.[528][1638] Because it is the limit rather than a nuisance, it is the first specification worth checking on any measuring instrument, and it is checked by measuring the instrument against itself — a shorted, terminated, or disconnected input — before any device under test is connected.[529][502][359][1220] Noise floor is not a single number: it depends on measurement bandwidth, on front-end design, on converter resolution, and on how much post-acquisition processing is applied.[107][369][1638][587]

## Bandwidth dependence

The noise floor changes with the bandwidth over which it is measured.[107] For an oscilloscope this means the higher the instrument's bandwidth, the higher its residual noise floor, all other things being equal.[369] Engaging a bandwidth limit visibly drops the trace: switching a 200 MHz front end from full bandwidth to a 20 MHz limit brings the noise under one division with a floating input.[369]

The limit belongs to the physical front end, not to the setting. A 350 MHz analogue front end software-limited to 200 MHz is still noisier than a front end built for 200 MHz — 57 µV standard deviation against 45 µV in a direct comparison — because the noise is baked into the hardware design.[1612] Two scopes of different bandwidth are therefore only comparable once both are constrained: with both set to a 20 MHz limit, one instrument's mean fell from about 65 µV to 38 µV.[1223]

Bandwidth is also the lever on current-probe noise. The I-Prober 520's toroid attachment specifies 6 mA RMS over its full DC-to-5 MHz bandwidth but 1.5 mA RMS at the minimum 2 Hz bandwidth setting, which puts its practical floor at a few milliamps and rules out microamp work entirely.[296]

## Converter resolution and processing gain

Vertical resolution and noise floor move together. A 12-bit converter with a low noise floor produces a visibly cleaner trace than an 8-bit instrument on the same 50 Ω terminated signal at the same bandwidth.[1501] Going further, a true 14-bit ADC halves the noise floor relative to a 12-bit competitor; in one such instrument the floor sits around −109 to −110 dB, low enough that a 15 µV signal passed through a 12 dB attenuator is still visible poking out of it.[1638] High-resolution modes trade sample rate for depth by high-order averaging of the bits, giving a lower noise floor.[223] The distinction between a genuine converter and a computed one matters: a 14-bit ADC reaching 16 bits in high-definition mode is a different proposition from a 12-bit ADC averaged up to 18.[1638]

Digital signal processing after the converter buys a great deal. A plain 8-bit converter gives only about 50 dB of noise floor, whereas even an entry-level spectrum analyser is expected to deliver around 100 dB.[587] Digital down conversion followed by discrete Fourier transforms produces processing gain, which lowers the effective noise floor because the result is computed over a much smaller bandwidth; in one documented case this improved signal-to-noise by roughly 57 dB and yielded a 107 dB noise floor from an 8-bit converter.[587] The same mechanism appears in a scope FFT, where increasing the transform length lowers the noise floor as more data becomes available, and where enabling high-resolution acquisition drops it further.[1566][845][1188] Calibration-grade instrumentation exploits the same effect, using an FFT rather than a conventional converter for better dynamic range and a lower noise floor, particularly close in.[1041]

Averaging attacks the same problem in the time domain, pulling periodic content out of the noise: 512 averages will lift a peak that is otherwise invisible, and at very low signal levels a sine wave becomes recognisable only after tens of averages.[199][1744][523] Relating the noise floor to the full-scale input is what gives effective number of bits, and hence usable rather than nominal resolution.[107]

## Apparent versus real noise floor

A displayed trace can misrepresent the underlying noise. One scope appearing to have a lower noise floor than another turned out to differ only in update rate and memory depth; changing the memory depth changed the apparent noise.[797] The remedy is a numeric measurement — standard deviation of the acquired samples — rather than a visual judgement of trace thickness.[1223] At coarse vertical settings the floor may be visible as nothing more than the last few bits refusing to settle: three bits oscillating at 10 mV/div is the noise floor of that instrument, and it can equally be expressed in the frequency domain, around −74 dBV for the same hardware.[692]

Logarithmic display exists largely because of this dynamic range problem. With a noise floor at 10 µV against a 1 V full scale, a linear axis would need to be divided into 100,000 parts before the noise became visible at all.[49]

## Measuring low noise requires a lower noise instrument

Characterising noise demands a measurement chain quieter than the thing being characterised. A dynamic signal analyser used for op-amp voltage noise had its own floor of 31.3 nV/√Hz at 1 kHz — around 22 nV/√Hz by a later reckoning at the same frequency — which is not spectacular, and that floor was documented before anything else was attempted.[528][529] Measuring at these levels also requires shielding: a Faraday cage, typically a diecast alloy box, is the standard method.[528]

When the device under test is quieter than the analyser, the noises still sum, so the quieter device registers as a bump above the instrument floor rather than a reading in its own right.[528] Conversely a genuinely good op-amp measured 33.7 nV/√Hz — only a couple of nV above the analyser's own floor — meaning the instrument, not the part, is what is being measured.[528] The way out is external gain: a low-noise preamplifier of about 4 nV/√Hz placed ahead of the analyser lifts the signal well above the floor so the absolute performance of the part can be resolved, and characterising state-of-the-art op-amps requires exactly this, with the front-end amplifier itself carefully designed.[528] The same reasoning applies to a scope-based EMC probe: rather than buying a low-noise scope front end, a higher-gain preamplifier — 20, 30 or 40 dB — is fitted so the scope's own noise floor stops mattering.[1188]

Cascading two measurements does not multiply their dynamic ranges. Notching a −80 dB-capable source into a −80 dB-capable analyser does not yield 160 dB of range, because the noise floor and residual analogue distortion of the second instrument reassert themselves.[vwAhHz7Zpzk]

## Spectrum analysers

For a spectrum analyser the noise floor is the reference against which everything is judged, and it is set jointly by hardware and resolution bandwidth.[891][548] A direct comparison of two entry-level analysers at identical attenuation, detector and reference settings over a 1.5 GHz span showed −65 dBm against −85 dBm at 1 MHz RBW, a full 20 dB advantage, narrowing to 15–20 dB at 100 kHz RBW.[891] A better noise floor combined with a 10 Hz minimum resolution bandwidth against 100 Hz makes the difference between resolving detail and not.[891] Narrowing RBW lowers the floor, but there is a point beyond which reducing it will not expose an artefact caused by internal coupling.[891]

Absolute figures at the higher end run far lower: −156 dBm for one instrument against −161 dBm for a better-specified sibling, a difference traceable to the hardware rather than the firmware, while phase noise between the two was practically identical.[1101] A typical working figure with an unterminated input at 10 kHz RBW sits just above −115 dBm, rising with frequency.[1101] The input attenuator and preamplifier blocks are the parts that determine this, and are given their own shielded can inside an already shielded block for that reason.[470] A dedicated RF input, as opposed to a general-purpose analogue channel pressed into service, is what gives a mixed-domain instrument its low noise floor.[PcxEO3fA_Ls]

In EMC pre-compliance work the inherent noise floor of the analyser and its cabling is established first, with the vertical scale referenced to 1 µV, and pass/fail limit lines are set above it.[548] A misbehaving transmitter that generates broadband noise raises the noise floor across the band rather than producing discrete spikes, which is distinguishable from the natural floor simply by disconnecting the offending unit and watching the elevated region vanish.[767]

## Probes and signal conditioning

Attenuation ahead of the instrument costs noise floor. Using a 10:1 probe to gain bandwidth divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope input, raising the effective noise floor — an explicit trade-off when working at 50 mV/div.[1735] The same effect appears within a probe: switching a differential probe to its ×100 range raises its own noise floor to the point that a common-mode rejection plot goes flat and featureless.[Dez9KG6whb0] Measurements taken below the noise floor become visibly jagged and unreliable, which is the signature that the plot has run out of dynamic range rather than that the device has a real response there.[Dez9KG6whb0]

Where the gain stage sits determines the result. Placing gain ahead of the attenuating network, rather than a ×10 stage afterwards, buys a lower noise floor at the cost of an extra part, and selecting a quieter differential receiver — an AD8129A at 200 MHz in place of a 270 MHz alternative — trades a little bandwidth for lower noise.[1744] An active probe compared side by side against a competitor at the same 20 mV/div with the inputs shorted shows the difference plainly.[1715] An optically isolated probe reached roughly −80 dBm across its 200 MHz bandwidth, with roll-off beginning at the bandwidth limit as expected.[1557]

## Analogue circuits and audio

In analogue equipment the noise floor is often a property of one part rather than of the layout. Audible hiss from studio monitor speakers was isolated to the output power amplifier chip, and is inherent in that chip's noise floor irrespective of input termination — a reasonably common characteristic of powered monitors, some of which fit an auto-mute to cut the output entirely.[1128][1714] A microphone's noise floor is likewise a selection criterion, an instrument microphone being chosen over a lapel type partly for its lower floor.[a5g7-YKhveA] Acoustic instruments have the same limit expressed in dBA: a sound level meter with an 18 dBA noise floor is still not quiet enough to be irrelevant when the ambient reading is 33 dBA.[1745]

Noise floor is also one of the deliberate trade-offs in a measurement front end. In one power meter a residual offset of about 1.8 µA was left uncorrected because the design prioritised a lower noise floor and wider bandwidth over minimum DC offset; trimming the offset digitally would have degraded both.[1693]

Device-to-device variation in a parameter such as an op-amp's noise floor follows the same Gaussian manufacturing distribution as resistor tolerance, so a datasheet figure describes a probability of falling within a range rather than a guarantee for the individual part.[215]

## Practical significance

The distinction between a bench instrument and a cheaper alternative frequently comes down to noise floor alongside input protection, vertical amplifier performance, waveform processing speed and triggering.[13] The same is true when choosing between a modern low-cost analyser and secondhand professional equipment, where RF performance means noise floor and bandwidth first.[500] On any instrument the practical procedure is unchanged: measure the floor with no input, at the vertical setting and bandwidth actually to be used, before trusting anything measured above it.[196][369][1220]
