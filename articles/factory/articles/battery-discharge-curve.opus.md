# battery discharge curve

A battery discharge curve is the standard characteristic plot of cell voltage against time or delivered capacity, where the x-axis may be expressed as elapsed hours or as 0 to 100% of the battery's rated capacity, often written as C.[176] It is the single most useful piece of data for predicting how long a product will run and for choosing the voltage at which that product should give up, because it converts a chemistry into a number a designer can act on.[1331][772] Curves vary by manufacturer, by battery type, and with normal process variation in manufacture, but within a chemistry they are similar enough to design against.[176]

## The curve is only meaningful under load

A discharge curve must be measured with current flowing.[772] Specifying a battery voltage that is not under load is pointless when the subject is capacity or energy, because the open-circuit terminal voltage says nothing about what the cell can actually deliver.[772] This is precisely why battery manufacturers publish their discharge curves under load: it is the only measurement that matters, and it folds in the cell's internal resistance.[779]

That internal resistance is not constant along the curve. A AA or AAA cell sits at roughly 100 milliohms when fresh and around 300 milliohms by the end of its discharge curve, and manufacturers publish graphs showing internal resistance tracking the discharge.[779] The consequence is visible in the industry-standard application tests printed on cell data sheets — toothbrush, portable lighting, toy.[779] A toy drawing high pulsed current dies not because the chemistry is exhausted but because each pulse pulls the terminal voltage below the device's operating point and the cell cannot recover; measuring the terminal voltage inside the device while it operates shows a far lower figure than a bench supply sweep suggests.[779]

## Shape by chemistry

Alkaline cells have a distinctly non-flat characteristic, sloping continuously through their useful range, which makes it hard to place a cutoff that harvests everything.[772] Lithium ion is much flatter over most of its discharge, so cutoffs are easier to design for maximum energy extraction.[772] Older coke-anode lithium ion cells are conventionally treated as dead at about 2.5 to 2.7 volts, and even some graphite types are called dead at 2.7, which is what makes it practical to run a 3.3 volt circuit directly from a single cell.[176]

Whatever the chemistry, the end of the curve is abrupt. Alkaline cells drop off like a brick wall at 0.8 volts, and this is consistent across data sheets.[751] A coin cell rated 235 milliamp-hours to 2 volts simply falls away at 2 volts with essentially no energy remaining beneath it.[1383] A NiMH cell discharged at 1 amp holds up well — still 1.285 volts and nearly 2,700 milliamp-hours after 2 hours 40 minutes — then collapses at the 1 volt discharge point.[9FCzAgJhRdc] Lithium primary cells show the same signature in a more extreme form: a curve that barely moves from 3.6 volts down to perhaps 3.5 volts across thousands of milliamp-hours before dying at the very end.[1371] A D cell rated 17 amp-hours exhibits the same characteristic drop-off, just later.[1371]

## The 0.8 volt convention and what lies below it

Manufacturer characteristic discharge curves for AA and AAA alkalines stop at 0.8 volts, the industry-standard cutoff, on the general agreement that there is negligible energy left below it.[774] Curves for AA alkalines discharged at currents from a few hundred milliamps to 1 amp show very little area under the curve between roughly 0.5 and 0.8 volts — on the order of 5% of capacity at most.[1296] Data sheets typically also stop at load levels around 100 milliwatts, leaving the ultra-low-power region undocumented; dedicated testing at 100, 50 and 10 milliwatt levels is required to characterise what boost converters operating below 0.8 volts can actually reach.[774] As a practical bench rule, discharging an alkaline to 0.8 volts is treated as sufficient, with 1.0 volt often adequate, rather than pushing to 0.5 volts.[1296]

## Wasted capacity and cutoff design

The gap between a product's cutoff voltage and the true end of the curve is wasted energy, and the curve is how that waste is quantified.[772] Across the entire practical current range, from 5 milliamps to 1 amp, a product cutting out at 1.0 or 1.1 volts — which many do — leaves only about 10 to 20% of the available energy on the table, and that is the absolute best case.[772] Products designed around rechargeable cells already accept a maximum of about 1.1 volts, so the recoverable margin there is negligible.[772]

This arithmetic is what defeats the Batteriser, a boost converter sleeve claimed to extract eight times a cell's life.[751][ixbLmBWUqcQ] Its patent argument rests on the observation that a battery takes longer to fall 0.1 volts at low voltage than at high — so a constant-current discharge takes far longer to go from 1.2 to 1.1 volts than from 1.5 to 1.4.[751] Counting 0.1 volt steps down to 0.6 volts inflates the result, because there is no capacity down there to count.[751] The claim compounds the error by using open-circuit voltage to reason about remaining energy.[772] The underlying device is an ordinary DC-DC converter that steps up a falling cell voltage, and it does recover the small area under the curve below the normal cutoff — a bit more energy, not eight times more.[ixbLmBWUqcQ]

## Measuring a curve

The practical method is a programmable electronic load — constant current, constant resistance, or constant power — logging to a PC, which yields the discharge curve directly.[772][393] A data-logging multimeter, a standalone logger, or pen and paper at one-minute intervals will also do.[772] A long test generates a great deal of data; a run may produce on the order of 100,000 points that must be decimated before plotting.[772] Integrating voltage against accumulated energy over time does not produce a straight line even at fixed current — the accumulated-energy series carries a slight downward bow that is a real product of the mathematics, not a plotting artefact, and should not be fudged with a straight-line fit.[772]

Real measured curves can be compared directly against the data sheet curve to judge fit.[772] Instruments with sufficient resolution reveal effects far below normal discharge rates: with a load of greater than 10 gigohms drawing only about 900 picoamps, a cell's discharge is still observable.[515] At those levels alkaline cells show an initial voltage drop, a change of slope over some fifteen minutes, and partial recovery when the load is removed; Duracell and Energizer AA cells behave almost identically, and applying mechanical pressure to a cell measurably changes the slope.[515] Mechanical squeezing has been shown to alter the discharge response outright — a squeezed cell reaching a given voltage after 1,220 seconds where an unsqueezed one reached it after 36 seconds, a large increase in the area under the curve.[865] Capacity claims frequently fail such tests; a cell may be flat, at 144.6 millivolts under 36 milliamps after under eight hours, well short of its specification.[141]

## Consequences for circuit design

The discharge curve sets the input range a power supply must tolerate. Four AA cells in series give a 6 volt nominal rail that falls substantially over the discharge, so a 5 volt regulator — even a low-dropout part — will only hold regulation for part of the curve.[385] Where the curve is nearly flat, the opposite is true: a multimeter drawing 1.5 milliamps and holding accuracy down to 2.6 volts extracts almost the whole cell, and a Duracell curve at 5 milliamps constant current running past 700 hours implies 800 to 1,000 hours of instrument life at that lower drain.[1731]

The curve is also the basis of battery-life estimation before a product is built. Given a measured sleep and operational current, the type-specific discharge curve supports an estimate of run time without the hundreds of hours a real endurance test would take.[1331] Purpose-built battery emulators can substitute for the cell entirely, and the natural extension is for such an instrument to replay the discharge profile of a selected chemistry so that cutoff behaviour and end-of-life performance can be exercised deliberately rather than by tweaking a bench supply down by hand.[1331]
