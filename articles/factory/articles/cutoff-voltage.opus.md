# cutoff voltage

The cutoff voltage of a battery-powered product is the terminal voltage at which its circuitry shuts down, switches off automatically, or raises a low-battery warning.[1732][779] It is the single design parameter that most directly determines how much of a battery's stored energy a product can actually use, and it heads the list of factors that affect delivered battery capacity, ahead of temperature and discharge rate.[140] Because a cell's discharge curve slopes continuously rather than holding flat to exhaustion, every tenth of a volt of unnecessary cutoff strands capacity that is physically present in the cell but electrically unreachable.[140][1732]

## Effect on usable capacity

On an alkaline AA the penalty is severe. A product that cuts out or warns at 1.1 V per cell discards the entire area under the discharge curve below that point, and one set at 1.2 V wastes roughly half the battery's capacity.[140] A well-designed product therefore sets its cutoff as low as it practically can; for an alkaline cell that floor is about 0.8 V per cell, and a superbly designed product operates all the way down to it.[140] The same reasoning scales to multi-cell packs: a two-cell 3 V design should work down to 1.6 V, a nominal 6 V system down to 3.2 V, and a standard 9 V battery down to 4.8 V.[140] Instruments that cut out at 6.5 or 7 V on a 9 V battery — and some are higher still — throw away a large fraction of the pack.[140]

Lithium-ion behaves differently because its discharge curve collapses at the bottom. Taking a cell down to a 2.8 V stop rather than a more conservative 3 V yields only about 15 mAh of extra capacity, because terminal voltage drops like a brick wall at 2.8 V and practically no energy remains below it.[1732] Setting the cutoff too high on lithium-ion is nonetheless costly in the other direction: on one aged 680 mAh-class cell, a 3.3 V cutoff left only 220 mAh available.[1732] The general rule holds for any chemistry — the lower the voltage a product can be designed to run down to, the more capacity is extracted.[1732]

## Calculating wasted capacity

Wasted capacity can be read off directly by logging a constant-current discharge and plotting voltage against elapsed time alongside a cumulative remaining-energy curve on the same time axis.[772] A horizontal line drawn at the chosen cutoff voltage is dropped vertically where it meets the voltage curve, then extended horizontally to the remaining-energy axis, giving the energy left in the cell at the moment the product declares itself flat.[772] For a Duracell copper-top AA discharged at 100 mA with a 1 V per cell cutoff, the remaining energy at shutdown is under 5% — negligible.[772] The logging itself needs only voltage samples at a known constant discharge current; the current need not be measured if the load holds it constant.[772]

This method bounds how much energy any voltage-boosting sleeve can recover from an exhausted cell. Across discharge currents from 5 mA to 1 A, a product with a 1 V or 1.1 V cutoff leaves only about 10–20% of the battery's energy on the table as an absolute best case.[772] Recovering the frequently claimed 80% requires the host product to be extremely poorly designed, with a cutoff under load of around 1.4 V.[772] Any product designed for rechargeable cells already has a cutoff of at most 1.1 V, so the recoverable margin there is negligible.[772] The underlying premise is real — some modern electronics do have cutoff voltages too high to reach the last part of the discharge curve — but the magnitude is small.[ixbLmBWUqcQ]

## Measuring a product's cutoff voltage

Only the voltage present on the product's battery terminals matters, because the product's own internal voltage reference is what decides when to shut down, and that reference sees nothing but the terminal voltage.[779] It follows that a bench power supply is a valid instrument for determining a product's cutoff voltage: it forces the chosen voltage directly onto the terminals with effectively zero source impedance, so the value at which the product quits is its true cutoff voltage.[779][789] The battery's internal resistance is a red herring in this measurement, which is also why manufacturers publish loaded discharge curves in the first place — terminal voltage is the only thing obtainable.[779] Open-circuit battery voltage is not a substitute, since a power supply has no electrochemistry to model.[789]

The contrary claim, that using a power supply to show a battery-operated device's cutoff voltage "ignoring the battery's internal resistance is wrong and misleading at best", rests on failing to probe at the terminals.[789]

## Bench practice

Electronic loads with a battery-test application accept a stop voltage directly: the load draws a programmed constant current until the terminal voltage reaches that threshold, then cuts out automatically and reports accumulated amp-hours and watt-hours.[789][-ir61ARd5T4] Typical settings include 1 V for a nominal single-cell NiMH or alkaline test, 1.8 V for two alkaline cells in series (0.9 V per cell), and 2.8 V for an aggressive lithium-ion run.[9FCzAgJhRdc][XDjyY48u0PU][1732] A separate voltage-on threshold, commonly set around 0.5 V, gates when the load engages.[RFb3TwWzza0][9FCzAgJhRdc] Where a test must run for a fixed duration regardless of voltage — such as a 24-hour leakage run — the voltage stop is simply disabled in favour of a time stop.[RFb3TwWzza0]

Remote or Kelvin sensing at the cell terminals is necessary for the stop voltage to mean anything, since drop in the leads and contacts otherwise triggers the cutoff early.[1732][9FCzAgJhRdc] A discharge logged with a multimeter instead of an electronic load has no programmable stop at all, so the test duration and sample count must be calculated in advance from the expected capacity.[774]

Cutoff voltage is also what makes an accumulated milliwatt-hour measurement meaningful: a battery's watt-hour capacity is only defined against a stated cutoff, which is what allows a logged energy consumption figure to be converted into expected runtime.[1693] In systems with more than one cutoff in series, the lowest one governs — a portable power station's 9 V load-side cutout tripped before the station's own low-battery shutdown, with the pack still indicating 2%.[lYKjScnkeq0] Low-power devices can likewise be characterised by discharging their storage element until the circuit stops running, in one case at about 3 V.[S-UqkM8iDKM]
