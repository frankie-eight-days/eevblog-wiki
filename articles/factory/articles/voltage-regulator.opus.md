# voltage regulator

A voltage regulator takes an input supply of uncertain or varying value and holds an output rail at a fixed or set voltage, and it is one of the few parts that appears in essentially every powered design regardless of what else the design does.[500] Two families exist: linear regulators, which drop the surplus voltage across a pass element, and switching regulators, which convert it; catalogue search categories split them further into complete regulators with an integrated pass transistor and switching controllers that drive an external one.[1475][1180] Regulators are ubiquitous enough that a product with none at all — a toy running its microcontroller straight off the cells to save every possible cent of bill-of-materials cost — is itself the noteworthy case.[1111][534]

## How a linear regulator behaves

A three-terminal linear regulator has only three legs, so every milliamp entering the input pin must leave either through the output pin or through the ground pin; the ground-pin component is the quiescent current, and it is wasted regardless of load.[1285] The classic implementation puts an output voltage divider feeding back into the regulator, which drives a pass transistor to whatever conduction the loop demands.[804] Where a monolithic part cannot supply enough current, an external series pass transistor is added around it, and instruments built this way give each regulator channel its own pass transistor and current-sense resistor.[1116][803]

Regulation requires headroom. The dropout voltage — the minimum input-to-output difference below which the loop can no longer hold the rail — is not a single number but a function of output current, and datasheets accordingly specify it at several currents.[1438] A common jellybean adjustable part is specified at up to 1.2 V of dropout at 800 mA.[1438] A modern low-cost SOT-23 part measures about 300 mV at full rated current and roughly 150 mV at 100 mA.[1147] Below dropout the output simply follows the input down and the circuit dies; in a thermoelectric-harvesting supply the load kept running on stored charge for a moment once the source fell under the regulator's dropout, then faded out entirely.[664]

Dropout is what makes battery-powered design awkward. A 5 V regulator fed from four AA cells in series has only about 6 V nominal to work with, and the cell voltage falls steeply, so even a low-dropout part will not regulate across much of the discharge curve.[385] A design whose supply survives all the way down to about 3.3 V is therefore evidence that the rail is not a 5 V linear regulator at all.[385] Where a microcontroller supervises the supply, an ADC input reading the incoming source voltage lets firmware determine in advance whether the regulator is about to drop out.[232]

Seen from the battery, a regulated circuit is not a resistive load. Because the regulator holds a constant voltage across the downstream circuitry, the current drawn from the cell is essentially constant regardless of cell voltage, which is a constant-power load and behaves very differently from a fixed resistor during a capacity test.[141][ILIO5b1BliE] One handheld meter carries three regulators for this reason, at 4 V, 3.6 V and 3.3 V, the last for its internal ARM processor.[ILIO5b1BliE] A lithium-ion charger in its constant-voltage phase does exactly what a linear regulator does, holding 4.2 V while the current tapers away.[176]

## Dissipation, heat sinking and layout

Everything a linear regulator drops it dissipates. Turning 12 V of battery into a 3.3 V project rail throws most of the pack's energy away as heat, which is a strong argument against linear regulation in portable equipment.[258] Where linear regulation is retained, the heat must go somewhere: bench-supply designs mount the regulators on a rear heat sink with the board deliberately extended so the back panel slides behind it and keeps the heat outside the enclosure.[258] Instrument builders bolt regulator tabs to the chassis or to an internal aluminium plate with insulating pads, using the case itself as the heat sink and mechanically restraining the parts against vibration.[738][695][208] Free-standing TO-220 regulators inside an extruded enclosure need no insulating washer provided the case is not being used as the heat path.[644]

Under-sizing that heat path is a real failure mode rather than a theoretical one: one bench supply shipped with a heat sink too small for its regulator, pushed the part past its maximum junction temperature, and had to be re-engineered with a much larger sink.[861] The alternative to brute-force heat sinking is to keep the drop small in the first place. A tracking pre-regulator — a switch-mode stage whose output follows the required linear output — holds the drop across the output regulators to a minimum and allows a surprisingly small heat sink for a supply rated at ±25 V and 6 V at 3 A.[875]

Heat sinking also constrains the board. With fixed regulators mounted on a rear heat sink, the circuitry associated with them belongs at that edge of the board, because running the power traces the length of the board to reach it destroys the layout.[244] Regulators bring their own passive furniture: an input capacitor, an output capacitor, and the bulk supply capacitor that sits at the regulator output and serves the low-frequency charge demands of everything downstream.[158][974][1085]

## Ripple, noise and precision

Despite the name, regulators are poor filters. They hold the DC output precisely, but attenuation of tens or hundreds of millivolts of input ripple is limited, and a low-dropout part is a weak tool for removing ripple.[1116] A capacitance multiplier — a pass transistor whose base capacitor is multiplied by the transistor gain — removes ripple far more effectively, but it has no error amplifier and no feedback loop, so its output voltage moves with input voltage, temperature and load; it is not a regulator and is best used ahead of one.[1116]

Regulator noise matters where the load is sensitive. In condenser microphone bias supplies a regulator is likely to be noisier than a zener-derived rail, and a zener with a soft knee is quieter still.[609] For precision analogue sections the answer is local regulation: instrument designers do not feed op amps directly from the main ±18 V rails but regulate them again on each card, and the same reasoning puts dedicated local rails beside a precision ADC and beside an oscilloscope's analogue front end.[538][1126][1639] Fast digital loads impose a different requirement — 1 ns comparators take large gulps of current when they switch, so they are fed from a fast transient-response regulator placed locally.[587]

A regulator is not a voltage reference. Initial accuracy is typically a couple of percent, and an adjustable part trimmed by hand reaches perhaps 1 to 1.5 percent, which is often not enough where an ADC threshold depends on it.[1438] Where a low-current digital section needs better, it can be powered directly from a precision 2.5 V, 3.3 V or 5 V reference of 0.1 to 0.2 percent, available for about a dollar, provided the load stays inside the reference's current rating.[225] The vocabulary follows the distinction: dropout voltage is a regulator specification and does not normally appear in a voltage reference datasheet.[500] A zener and series resistor will regulate a rail adequately if efficiency is unimportant, but the resistor value becomes a compromise as soon as the input voltage and the load both vary.[908]

Temperature affects regulators as it affects every other component, which is why an oven test is worth running on a finished product; an Arduino board's rails measured 5.015 V and 3.407 V at room temperature before being cycled over range.[101]

## Identifying and checking a regulator

A small three- or five-pin SOT-23 or SOT-223 device with capacitors to ground on two of its pins and disproportionately wide traces running to it is almost certainly a regulator; a SOT-223 package in particular is a reliable giveaway.[1475][630][918] The absence of bypass capacitors on the input and output is equally informative, and rules a part out as a regulator.[1708] Whether it is fixed or adjustable can be read off the feedback path: a resistor divider back to a feedback pin means the output is set by those resistors, while a bare output with only a filter capacitor points to a fixed part, which must then be replaced with the same voltage rather than an adjustable equivalent.[1475][1541] Distinguishing linear from switching requires looking at the surrounding components — an inductor nearby means switching.[1475][775]

Regulators are the first thing to measure. The rule is "thou shalt test voltages", and on an unknown or dead board that means finding the regulator-looking packages and probing input and output before consulting a service manual.[918][1418] The same check is the first step in bringing up a new board: measuring 3.3 V across a bypass capacitor near the microcontroller confirms the regulator, and confirms there is no short pulling the rail down.[1306][357] Many boards make this easy because the regulator tab or can is tied to the common ground, so one probe can stay put while the other roams.[630]

## Failure modes

Regulators are usually reliable, and the usual way they die is over-voltage on the input — most often caused by a failed upstream supply.[538] When that happens the damage is rarely confined to one part: a single failed mains supply on one instrument took out at least three separate regulators across several boards, so finding one dead regulator is a reason to check the rest.[538] The signature of a destroyed linear regulator is a shorted pass transistor, measurable as a fraction of an ohm between input and output — 0.3 Ω in one case.[538] A regulator with a shorted input-to-output path leaves an unregulated rail at whatever the input happens to be.[538] In supplies where diodes and regulators share the same rail, the diodes generally fail first.[1364]

Marginal thermal behaviour produces subtler faults than outright failure. A supply whose regulator overheated continued to read 5 V on a multimeter while its AC content changed enough to reset the digital board beside it — the DC value looked correct and only an AC-coupled scope trace at 100 mV per division revealed the intermittent dropout.[512] Thermal imaging is useful here: three regulators sitting at 70 °C in a repair stand out immediately against the neighbouring parts.[593]

Reverse energy is the other hazard. If a battery is left connected to the output while the input supply is switched off, current flows backwards into the regulator and can destroy it, which is why traditional linear supplies fit a reverse-protection diode across the pass element.[233] A zener used to protect a regulator against input over-voltage needs a series resistor to limit its current, without which it simply destroys itself.[104]

## Jellybean parts and system cost

The staple parts are worth stocking by the handful: the fixed 78xx series and the adjustable LM317 belong in any basic component inventory.[168] The 7805 appears in analogue boards of every vintage alongside comparators and op amps, with the 78L05, 7809 and 7812 covering other rails and package sizes.[275][141][304][1049][314] Negative counterparts — the 79xx series and the LM337 — cover the negative rails.[536][538] The LM317's usefulness comes from its internal 1.25 V reference, which sets the lowest output it can produce and makes it suitable for the low rails of modern parts; tying its adjust pin to its output through a shunt resistor converts it into a constant-current source or current limiter.[1438][1688]

Modern digital designs multiply the count. High-density logic wants 3.3 V, 2.5 V, 1.8 V, 1.2 V and sub-volt core rails, so half a dozen supply rails in one product is unremarkable, and three to five regulators per unit is common.[1147] An FPGA design illustrates the ratchet precisely: 3.3 V for I/O, a separate 2.5 V rail because the auxiliary supply pin demands it and nothing else in the design does, and 1.2 V for the core — each an extra regulator, extra bill-of-materials cost and extra board area, and each worth planning for at the outset.[193][636] Even a part that runs from a single supply in operation may need a different voltage during programming, forcing another rail onto the board.[193]

Cost per regulator therefore matters at volume. Generic parts from Asian catalogue suppliers reach roughly 1.5 cents apiece, around a fifth the price of the Western-branded equivalents, and one such part tested well on accuracy, load regulation, dropout, stability into capacitive loads and recovery from short circuits.[1147] At the other extreme, the most expensive regulator listed on a major distributor was $1,653, held in stock in quantity.[1435] Parametric search is the practical route to selection, filtered by output voltage, current, package, dissipation, price and availability — though the category taxonomy is a trap, since a regulator and a regulator controller are listed separately.[400][1180]
