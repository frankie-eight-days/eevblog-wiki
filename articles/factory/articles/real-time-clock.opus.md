# real time clock

A real-time clock (RTC) is the circuit that keeps calendar time and date running in a piece of equipment while the rest of it is switched off. It is almost always a dedicated low-power counter — either a separate chip or a hardware block inside a microcontroller — clocked from a 32.768 kHz watch crystal and held alive by a backup battery or capacitor when the main supply goes away.[485][909][1360][615] Its presence or absence is a design decision with visible consequences: equipment without one cannot time-stamp anything and cannot even tell you what day it is after a power cycle.[955][1566]

## Recognising one on a board

The RTC is one of the easiest functions to identify by inspection, because it comes with a standard set of companions: a small package, a 32.768 kHz watch crystal beside it, and a battery or backup capacitor nearby.[485][470][603][339][1477] The crystal is the giveaway — a 32 kHz can next to a small IC is a real-time clock until proven otherwise.[295][417][454][746] The reasoning runs in both directions: a watch crystal on a board with no obvious clock function still implies an RTC somewhere, because nobody fits one for fun.[712] Conversely, a crystal at any other frequency is not an RTC crystal, and a product with no plausible need to know the date has no reason to carry one.[1275][973]

Backup power sits with the crystal. Coin cells are the norm, sometimes a rechargeable cell where a fixed lithium primary would be inappropriate,[1360] and sometimes a super capacitor instead: one oscilloscope used a 4.7 F, 2.7 V super cap as an outright battery replacement for its real-time clock.[1231] Elsewhere a large reservoir capacitor keeps time and date alive for an extended period after the power is disconnected.[687] A super cap rated to only 1.6 V is still enough to run an RTC chip, which is a far softer requirement than most other loads on a board.[1242]

Placement matters. Putting the RTC chip and its crystal at one end of the board and the backup battery at the other is poor layout practice.[1360] The 32 kHz crystal is physically fragile and gets mechanically secured: gunked down with potting compound on older machines,[116] soldered down rather than left standing on its leads on newer ones — a nice touch when it is done, and visibly bodged when it is done badly.[1231][360] The crystal traces should be kept short with the load capacitors returning to ground close by.[1034] Some low-power RTC oscillators integrate their load capacitors and even the feedback resistor, the latter switched off the enable pin so that disabling the chip opens the loop and stops the oscillator.[1614]

## Dedicated chips

The dedicated RTC part is old, cheap and abundant. Historic examples run from the Motorola MC146818 and MC68HC68,[452][339] the 4543,[942] and the Dallas parts that dominated the category — including a battery-backed variant that is pin-for-pin compatible with an SRAM chip of the same size but carries a built-in lithium primary cell with a guaranteed ten-year factory life from the date of purchase.[615][613][187] Later and current parts include the DS1307 and DS1302,[C1on-LaIsCA][1018] the TI BQ32002,[1431] and the Microchip MCP7940.[1034] On PC-era hardware the function was frequently absorbed into combination chips: a floppy drive controller with the real-time clock built in,[639] or the multi-combo IO cards that folded serial, parallel, joystick and RTC onto one expansion card to stop each function eating a slot.[1054]

Interface is usually I2C or a simple serial protocol, undemanding enough to bit-bang. A 1999 magazine project built a video caption generator around a PIC16F84 and a DS1307, with the main crystal and a separate 32 kHz crystal for the RTC, bit-banging an I2C port and fitting time, date and overlay into just 1 k of program memory.[C1on-LaIsCA]

The parts cost almost nothing. Bulk Chinese catalogue suppliers list real-time clock chips at around 10 cents in quantities of five, which makes paying substantially more than that hard to justify.[1132]

A common design error is over-protecting the backup cell: a series diode between the coin cell and the RTC chip is unnecessary, and so is a 1 k series resistor, since a coin cell holder inserted backwards causes no harm.[1034] Where the battery is wired straight to the RTC's supply pin, there is no way to monitor its state without an extra line to the microcontroller, and any sensing arrangement — even through a large series resistor — puts additional drain on the cell.[121] The consequence of accepting that trade-off is that a dead cell announces itself only by behaviour: the clock resets on every boot.[121] The same symptom on a PC-class board points at the same cause.[MqECOT5j-cE]

## Integrated RTC in microcontrollers

Modern low-power microcontrollers include a hardware real-time clock and calendar, and the current it draws in standby is one of the headline numbers in the datasheet. A PIC24F specifies 400 nA in real-time clock and calendar operation.[900] An STM32L part draws 800 nA in stop mode with RTC plus 8 kB of SRAM retention, against 400 nA in stop mode alone,[1242] with other devices in the family quoted at 270 nA standby and 400 nA stop before the RTC is added.[1539] Ultra-low-power parts elsewhere reach 900 nA in deep sleep with the real-time clock still running, and 20 nA in full shutoff where nothing at all is happening.[266] A stop mode measured at 620 nA covers the 32 kHz oscillator, the real-time clock and some RAM retention together.[290]

The internal RTC is not free of constraints. It needs its own 32.768 kHz source, which on a microcontroller means either an external watch crystal or an internal 32 kHz oscillator.[1538][900] Where the RTC counter is fed from the secondary oscillator, that same secondary oscillator may be the only low-frequency source available to a general-purpose timer, so the two functions compete unless a mux allows the 32.768 kHz watch crystal to drive both the RTC counter and the timer input.[1538] Vendor configuration tools expose the RTC alongside the other peripherals, including its calibration values.[269][900] Selecting a microcontroller for a battery-powered product, a hardware real-time clock and calendar is a desirable feature but not usually a deal breaker, since an external chip can always be added.[1538]

Integrated RTCs are also as prone to silicon errata as anything else: one PIC24F series carried an errata list covering the brownout reset, the real-time clock, I2C, UART, JTAG, SRAM, ADC, SPI and core functions.[3]

## What it is actually for

Anything that logs needs to date and time stamp its records, which is why data loggers and instruments carry an RTC as a matter of course.[603][614] A smart meter with 2.1 MB of non-volatile memory recording ten years of 30-minute interval data has a built-in real-time clock as part of that capability.[409] An access control terminal with a large battery backup tracks the time and date of every card entry.[673] A defibrillator carrying a 32.768 kHz watch crystal and backup battery is almost certainly recording the time and date of the incident — though for a device that may sit installed for years after a single factory or installation-time setting, the date is likelier to be right than the time.[909] Bench instruments use it for date-stamped screen captures and saved files, and a scope without one gets the date and time in the corner of every capture wrong.[800][1566] Test equipment of all kinds — multimeters, spectrum analysers, function generators, oscilloscopes, source measure units, power supplies — carries the standard battery-plus-crystal cluster for exactly this reason.[485][470][1431][384][187][607][1402][1477]

RTC functionality is also routinely folded into power management and application processors rather than provided separately: PMICs combine an RTC with battery charging, coulomb counting and the DC-DC converters that power the processor cores,[189][109][1761] and application processors for cameras and recorders list a real-time clock among their built-in peripherals.[672][399]

## Accuracy and its absence

A crystal-referenced RTC should hold time to something on the order of a minute a month at worst case, across temperature variation, with any crystal at all.[4vY5-obAx6w] A hybrid solar inverter fitted with a proper RTC chip and a 32.768 kHz watch crystal that nonetheless loses time is broken, not normal, and telling the owner to enable an internet time-sync function is not an answer to that.[4vY5-obAx6w]

Omitting the RTC entirely is a cost decision that shows up in use. A low-cost 12-bit oscilloscope with no backup battery has no real-time clock and no way to set the clock for anything, so its stored captures are stamped with the wrong date and time.[1566] The Raspberry Pi has no real-time clock, which is why add-on boards advertise one as a feature.[1190] Older machines make the omission plainer still: the first portable computer had no battery backup and no real-time clock, so it could not keep time when switched off,[955] and the HP 9845B had no real-time clock at all — one had to be plugged in as an expansion card alongside the serial, plotter, printer and mass storage interfaces.[1404] Historic timing hardware could also impose odd constraints: on a Tandy 1000, the timer chip serving as the real-time clock required a fixed 2.38 MHz signal, so a turbo board had to synthesise that signal regardless of the processor clock speed selected.[32]

The distinction between having a real-time clock and not having one had real administrative weight during the Y2K remediation effort, where every project containing a microcontroller — including those with no real-time clock in them — still needed a written report certifying Y2K compatibility.[1376]
