# crystal oscillator

A crystal oscillator is a frequency source built around a slice of quartz that is driven into mechanical vibration and used as the frequency-determining element of an electronic oscillator.[646][61] Inside a typical package is a circular sliver of quartz with an electrode on either side; applying a signal across the electrodes makes the disc vibrate, and that mechanical resonance sets the frequency.[646] Almost any active chip needs an oscillator of some kind to run at all, and for most digital systems that oscillator is a quartz one, either a bare crystal working with circuitry inside the chip or a complete oscillator module.[1614] Quartz is what makes accurate frequency cheap: precise time measurement with a high-stability crystal oscillator is easy and inexpensive, which is why entire measurement architectures — the multi-slope integrating converters in bench multimeters among them — are built on timing rather than on precision analogue.[485]

## Crystal versus oscillator module

The two words are routinely confused, and the distinction is real. A bare crystal — the classic HC49U can, or its surface-mount equivalents — is a passive two-terminal resonator that does nothing on its own and needs external circuitry to oscillate.[1089][677] An oscillator module, by contrast, contains a crystal plus the sustaining amplifier and output stage, and drives out a finished clock signal directly.[677] A typical module in a 14-pin DIP outline uses only four pins and carries small bumps on its underside to stand the metal case off the board so it cannot short exposed pads or vias.[1089] Package clues distinguish them in a teardown: a part with one large pad on the end is a bare crystal rather than an oscillator.[1336]

Microcontrollers illustrate the split. Most contain an oscillator circuit and bring out two pins — the crystal connects across them, and the same pins can instead accept an external clock input.[842][912] A 4000-series CD4060 ripple counter is the same idea in discrete form: a built-in oscillator usable with either an RC network or an external crystal, feeding a binary divider chain.[831][1245] Where a device has no crystal at all, an internal RC oscillator is usually doing the job.[903]

Oscillator modules can also hide a divider. A part marked 1 MHz may in fact contain an 8 MHz resonator divided by eight, with pin straps selecting the ratio, which means the real internal activity is eight times higher than the label suggests and can leak into ground planes and radiate.[1089] Small surface-mount oscillator modules are cheap enough that replacing a bulky crystal with one is a normal design move — a 3.579545 MHz oscillator in a 2.5 mm by 3 mm package runs well under thirty cents in volume.[1614]

## Accuracy grades

Crystal oscillator accuracy is specified in parts per million rather than percent.[1300] A plain XO sits in the region of 10 ppm, equivalently one part in 10⁵, with ordinary parts spanning roughly 10 to 100 ppm once the temperature range is taken into account.[61][235] Temperature-compensated types — TCXO, and the digitally compensated DTCXO in which performance is characterised at the factory and compensation values are programmed in — reach about an order of magnitude better, in the region of 0.5 to 5 ppm with around 1 ppm aging.[235][574] Oven-controlled crystal oscillators, an old technology, hold a quartz crystal at a fixed elevated temperature so that ambient variation in the laboratory no longer matters, reaching roughly 0.1 to 0.01 ppm, or 10⁻⁷ over the span of a year.[61][235] Rubidium standards move into primary-standard territory at better than 10⁻⁹, one part per billion.[61] Ovenised units can be very good over short intervals: one instrument oven oscillator specifies better than 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ over 24 hours.[265]

The same hierarchy shows up as instrument options. Frequency counters and RF signal generators typically ship with an ordinary quartz or temperature-compensated oscillator, with an OCXO offered as an extra-cost module — a card-edge daughterboard plugging into the main board in one signal generator,[823] a plug-in module in a timer counter whose price was in the hundreds to a thousand dollars,[265] and a temperature-compensated 10 MHz reference in another generator where the oven option cost a great deal more.[261] Where an oven module replaces a socketed crystal, the instructions matter; one such module carries the note "Please remove the crystal when you're installing this oscillator module."[265] Aftermarket ovens exist for popular counters and improve accuracy and stability by a couple of orders of magnitude for a modest sum.[647] At the low end of the oscilloscope market the reference is deliberately ordinary: one scope generation moved from a 25 ppm standard time base to 1.6 ppm,[702][701] while cheaper models simply do without a precision crystal oscillator.[1477]

An oven costs power and time. The heater in an oven module can draw 5 to 10 W, and a counter with an internal oven fitted draws around 33 W total.[265][647] Ovens are wrapped in hard-cell foam to insulate them against outside thermal shocks, and the assembly typically includes a heating coil, a temperature sensor and a trim adjustment.[265][1139] The crystal itself is nothing exotic — a regular quartz crystal of a particularly stable cut, held at temperature and insulated.[1139] An oven indicator LED may take fifteen minutes or so to signal stabilisation.[647] Calibration practice reflects this: a precision temperature-controlled crystal oscillator is left powered up for 48 hours before its time base is measured, whereas for an ordinary crystal or RC time base, waiting more than an hour is wasted effort.[424]

## Drift, aging and environmental sensitivity

Quartz crystal oscillators drift, and not only with temperature.[61] They carry a temperature specification, an aging figure describing how far they move per year, a voltage dependency, and sensitivity to physical shock and vibration.[646] The drift is inherent to the quartz itself, arising from physics deep enough that the practical engineering treats it as a characteristic to be measured rather than explained.[61] Linear drift can be measured against a GPS-locked rubidium reference and corrected in software, flattening the curve — but a crystal oscillator is never actually flat over time, even at a perfectly fixed temperature held constant for days, weeks and months.[457][61] The drift characteristic of a quartz oscillator can also be reset by subjecting it to a suitable shock or vibration, a result Dave Jones established while working on ocean-bottom seismic recorders where stability in the region of 10⁻⁸ was required.[646][61]

Orientation matters too. Because the quartz element vibrates, its behaviour depends on how it is oriented relative to the gravitational field; rotating the piece of quartz changes the frequency, and flipping it fully over produces the largest change.[646][hE45DSUdD58] The effect is typically in the order of 1 to 10 parts per billion, so an 11- or 12-digit counter is needed to see it at all, which is why it is not normally a problem in oscilloscopes.[JobJswHcnLE] It is well known in the seismic industry, and it has a direct bench consequence: an oven oscillator should not be trimmed while sitting outside its instrument in a different orientation from the one it will be used in, and the reason is gravity rather than any thermal effect.[647][JobJswHcnLE] Shock mounting of quartz oscillators is a design discipline of its own, and materials evaluation for it has extended to cotton wool, foam, sorbothane and stranger candidates.[131]

## Mechanical mounting and layout

A crystal is a mechanical part, and it fails like one.[1520] Free-standing crystals flapping in the breeze are poor practice: catch the right vibrational mode and the part will shear off its leads.[589] Well-built assemblies glue or solder the can down to the board — an epoxy adhesive on a shockproof emergency beacon,[368] cans soldered to the board specifically to prevent vibration issues,[169] a glued-down 4 MHz crystal in a multimeter,[1578] soldered-down crystals even in inexpensive pocket meters.[1083] Rugged equipment that leaves a 32 kHz watch crystal unsecured is an outlier worth noting, since an unrestrained crystal can move and the leads can shear if the unit is dropped.[334]

Layout practice for a bare crystal is short traces from the chip to the crystal, with the load capacitors returning to ground close by.[1034] There is generally no reason to fit a through-hole crystal on a board that is otherwise entirely surface mount.[1323] Crystals also radiate: probing across one with a near-field loop and a spectrum display shows the fundamental plus harmonics — a 12.28 MHz part producing a clear peak near 36.9 MHz at three times the fundamental — which makes the crystal a natural first stop when baselining a board's emissions.[694][1188] Shielding is sometimes added specifically over the crystal region to keep digital hash out of adjacent analogue circuitry.[427][964]

## Frequency multiplication and system clocking

Modern digital systems rarely run at the crystal frequency. A PLL or clock-management block multiplies a modest external reference up to the internal clock rate: an FPGA running its logic at a couple of hundred megahertz from a 20 MHz external crystal,[496] a 400 MHz application processor fed by something like a 20 MHz part,[334] a 24 MHz crystal boosted to the 600 MHz a reader's processor requires,[295] a 34 MHz crystal multiplied by pin-strapped ratios to produce roughly 200 MHz of clock,[491] and a 25 MHz main crystal generating everything a function generator needs.[497] The PLL, not the crystal, is often the weak link — a scope's 5 microsecond jitter problem was traced to the PLL generating the sample and FPGA clocks, with nothing wrong with the lower-frequency crystal itself.[699]

Certain frequencies recur for historical reasons. The 3.579545 MHz NTSC colorburst crystal was the cheapest and most common part available, salvaged out of televisions when crystals were expensive, and it turns up as a main clock in vintage computers.[633][1662] The 32.768 kHz watch crystal is the standard real-time-clock element and appears alongside the main oscillator in devices needing timekeeping.[334][271][409] Odd values are common too — 4.9152 MHz, 12.288 MHz for audio sample-rate generation, 106.25 MHz driving a bench multimeter's timing, 74.1758 MHz likely custom-ordered.[1520][1322][485][1097] Older machines derive everything from one crystal by division: 9 MHz divided by three, 15.667200 MHz halved for the system clock, 10.6445 MHz divided by six.[382][414][645]

## When a crystal is not warranted

Accuracy costs money, and not every application needs it. An RFID jamming card with a crystal fitted is over-specified when the internal RC oscillator of the microcontroller would have sufficed.[890] At the other extreme, cost-down manufacturing substitutes a ceramic resonator for a crystal to save a fraction of a cent, which is unacceptable on a high-speed USB device.[12] Super-regenerative receiver modules are not crystal controlled at all — they are hand-tuned LC tank circuits, with the frequency accuracy that implies.[767] For general project work a crystal oscillator can simply be assumed to be close enough; needing a high-accuracy frequency counter to verify it means the design already has requirements the engineer would know about.[400]

## Failure and troubleshooting

Because a crystal is a mechanical resonator, a dead crystal is a plausible cause of a dead microcontroller, and checking for a clock is a standard early step after checking supply rails.[1520][1245] Confirming oscillation at the expected frequency clears the crystal and points the investigation at the chip.[1520] Two cautions apply. First, identify which chip the crystal actually serves — replacing a crystal belonging to a multimeter's analogue front-end chipset makes no difference to a fault in the main processor.[1522] Second, crystals in the classic cans are physically difficult to remove; hot air and dual irons can both fail to release one, and forcing it lifts the pad.[1520] A crystal circuit that shows no oscillation at all is a legitimate suspect during instrument repair, though accessible marked test points would usually find the real fault faster.[536]
