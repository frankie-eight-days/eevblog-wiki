# sextant

A sextant is a hand-held optical navigation instrument used to take sightings — "shots" — on stars, from which a navigator determines position.[fK2KBDo7ISY][854] It was the primary means of fixing position at sea and in the air before electronic navigation, and its function is essentially to track the stars and work out where the observer is.[854]

## Night flying in the pre-GPS era

Celestial navigation with a sextant dictated the operating pattern of early long-distance aviation: in the days of Kingsford Smith, aircraft flew mainly at night because that was when the crew could get star shots.[fK2KBDo7ISY] The requirement to see the stars was a hard constraint, not a preference — daytime flight removed the references the sextant needed.[fK2KBDo7ISY]

## Mechanisation in the astro compass

The role of the pilot working a hand sextant was later automated by airborne star-tracking equipment. The B-52 bomber's automatic astro compass — part of the aircraft's inertial navigation system, with optical telescopes sighting stars from the top of the airframe — is functionally an electronic sextant: an all-mechanical star-tracking flight guidance computer performing the same task a navigator once did by hand.[854] Such astro compass systems were how aircraft navigated before GPS, the hand-held sextant being the method's predecessor.[854]

## Displacement by GPS and continued practice

GPS made the sextant obsolete as a practical navigation tool; with that technology available, choosing a sextant for real navigation is difficult to justify on utility grounds.[818] Jack Ganssle nonetheless still navigates his sailboat by sextant, explicitly to keep in practice, while admitting he sometimes takes the time from the GPS — a hybrid that concedes the older method's dependence on accurate time.[818] He regards retaining the skill as satisfying in its own right, likening it to still knowing how to use a slide rule years after either instrument ceased to be necessary.[818]
