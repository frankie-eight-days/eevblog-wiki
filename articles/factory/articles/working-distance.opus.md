# working distance

Working distance is the clear vertical gap between the objective lens of a microscope or camera and the surface it is focused on. For electronics work it is the single parameter that decides whether a soldering iron, tweezers, or a fume extractor nozzle can physically fit between the optics and the board.[390][282][1616] It trades directly against magnification and field of view: the optics that resolve the finest detail sit closest to the work, and the ones that leave room to work under see the least.[390][FLsCLAf-ahg]

## Why it governs bench soldering

A usable working distance is defined by iron angle rather than by any optical figure. Around 80 mm under a stereo microscope is enough to bring an iron in at 45 degrees and probably closer to 60 degrees; dropping to 50 mm under a higher-power objective closes that angle down.[390][282] This is why a lower-power objective is preferable for general soldering work even when a higher-power one is fitted to the same head — on a Mantis Elite the times four lens gives about 80 mm, the times eight about 60 to 65 mm, and the times ten about 50 mm, making the times four and times eight combination the practical pairing.[390]

Working distance also has to be shared with everything else that reaches over the board. A fume extractor hood positioned as close to the work as possible without fouling the microscope lens still sat about 130 mm above the soldering surface, and having a large working distance on the scope is what makes that placement possible at all.[1616] Where no microscope is involved the extractor can simply be brought right down to the work.[1616]

Around 50 mm is roughly the closest approach that still admits an iron and hand tools, even on systems that can optically focus down to about 2 cm.[1640] Below that the optics may still resolve and inspect fine, but the space is no longer workable.[1640]

## The trade against field of view and magnification

Field of view collapses as working distance shrinks. On a 100 mm-working-distance lens run at its widest angle and full zoom capability, the visible span across the board is about 19 mm — adequate for ultra-fine work but not for general-purpose use.[1143] Backing the same head off to 250 mm opens the view to roughly 37 to 38 mm, and a 0.35 Barlow lens pushing the working distance out to 330 mm yields about 72 mm of working width, enough to take in the full width of a board.[1143] At an intermediate 150 mm the zoom range starts to run out and the autofocus begins hunting.[1143]

Magnification is bought with distance in the same currency. A 200x optical system reaching 100 mm of working distance is long-bodied by necessity; the same platform fitted with different end lenses gives over 200 mm of working distance at 100x.[FLsCLAf-ahg] Long-throw optics have the corollary benefit of enormous depth of field: at 200 to 250 mm a board can be tilted to around 70 degrees and still be inspected with the focus region held on a chosen component.[FLsCLAf-ahg] Where a long working distance is forced on the design by a high optical zoom ratio rather than chosen — as on a 16x visual presenter — it becomes an awkwardness rather than a feature, and a secondary objective lens is the usual remedy for halving it.[278]

On systems without electronic zoom, magnification is changed by physically lowering the head, so every increase in zoom consumes working distance directly.[1640] Screw-in objective lenses on a camcorder-based rig impose a fairly narrow working range that shifts with zoom, forcing a height readjustment each time.[282]

## Typical figures

Long-throw video microscopes cluster in the 200 to 330 mm range. A Tagarno head is variously worked at 250 to 300 mm, at 300 mm for teardown detail, and at 30 cm for solder joint and switch contact inspection.[1125][989][1361][e98cYNPUxcg] A fixed-height Tagarno FHD ZIP runs at 230 mm with a maximum field of view of 27 cm at minimum zoom.[590] A 250 mm working distance is comfortable for probing and narrating over a populated instrument board.[521] Stereo microscopes intended for repair reach similar figures — a Motic SS41, sold under the Swift brand with a times one objective and times ten and times twenty wide-field eyepieces, gives about 220 mm.[1209]

Mid-range digital systems sit around 100 to 200 mm. An Eakins-type inspection scope with its stock lens focuses at about 100 mm, which is enough to get an iron underneath and even to flex a large board under the head, with the stand needing no further adjustment once that distance is set because focus then tracks from flat up to the full height of the fixture.[1125] A fixed-focus HD USB soldering microscope measured about 140 mm against a claimed 150 mm, with no zoom and no second focal plane.[590] A 150 to 200 mm working distance is described as the decisive advantage of such a unit.[RmpYQBg864M]

Cheap USB microscopes are the opposite case. At usable zoom levels they offer roughly 25 to 30 mm of working height, which only works at all because the barrel is slim enough to let an iron in alongside it; the supplied stands are generally unusable for low-zoom work.[566] Working distance alone is sufficient reason to prefer a purpose-built HD soldering microscope over that class of instrument.[590]

Fixed-focus instruments state the figure as a tolerance rather than a range. A benchtop thermal camera focuses at 70 mm plus or minus 10 mm, which confines it to small products; something closer to 30 cm with a correspondingly large depth of field would be needed to work on larger assemblies.[988]

## Ergonomics

Beyond tool clearance, working distance is part of what makes screen-based and ergonomic-viewer microscopes tolerable for long sessions. Peering into conventional stereo eyepieces for hours of soldering is fatiguing; an ergonomic stereo head typically pairs a more comfortable viewing posture with a larger working distance.[817] Video heads are usually mounted looking straight down, with the work placed just forward of the base plate, though the head can be tilted for an oblique view.[590] Even a 0.5 mm pitch part can be soldered under a 30 frame-per-second live video feed without difficulty.[590]

The practical judgment across the range of instruments is consistent: working distance is the specification that separates a microscope one can work under from one that can only be looked through. "The working distance is the killer."[RmpYQBg864M]
