# sram

Static RAM is volatile read/write memory that holds each bit in a latch rather than a capacitor, so it needs no refresh circuitry and no controller to keep it alive.[382] Two consequences follow, and between them they account for nearly every appearance of the part: it is fast, which makes it the memory of choice wherever an FPGA or a sampling front end has to write at full rate,[810][842] and its standby current is so small that a coin cell can hold its contents for the shelf life of the cell, which made it the storage medium of every pre-flash pocket computer, organiser and calibrated instrument.[643][1209] The penalty is cost and density: SRAM has always been the expensive way to buy a kilobyte, which is why early home computers used dynamic RAM instead and could afford 16 K when SRAM of that size was out of reach.[645][320]

## Speed grades and part marking

Old SRAM is marked with a speed suffix, and reading it is a routine part of identifying a board. A dash and a number on the end of the part number is an access time: `-70` is 70 nanoseconds, while `-15` may mean 15 nanoseconds or 150 depending on the process the device was built on.[410] The range encountered in practice is wide. A Pyramid Semiconductor device of 256 bits by 4 ran at 15 nanoseconds, small enough that its only plausible role is a buffer or fast counter.[410] Fairchild 81C78A parts in a signal averager were 45 nanoseconds, sitting alongside Sony devices in the same instrument at 100 nanoseconds for the slower, already-serialised data path.[452] The same 45-nanosecond MB81C78A, 64 kbit organised as 8 K bytes, served as video memory in the Sharp X68000.[746] At the other end, a home-built logic analyser used 650-nanosecond SRAM purely as a capture buffer behind a binary address counter.[747] IBM's 1991 multi-chip processor module carried 128 kbit SRAMs with 10-nanosecond access time, a large and fast part for its date.[1341]

## Acquisition and display memory in instruments

Digital oscilloscopes divide their memory into two kinds, and the division is visible on any teardown. Bulk sample memory is DDR or SDRAM because it is cheap per bit; the fast working memory bolted to the acquisition or display FPGA is SRAM because it must accept writes at full sample rate with low latency.[674][842] The Rigol DS1054Z pairs a 512 Mbit DDR2 sample buffer with a Cypress 9-megabit SRAM in a 256 K by 32 configuration, used to accumulate the frames that produce intensity-graded display.[674] The Uni-T UPO2104CS uses a Cypress CY7C1360, a 9-megabit 256 K by 32/36 pipelined SRAM, for the same job.[1038] The Siglent SDS2000X carries an 18-megabit NetSol part, 1 meg by 18, feeding the 256-level intensity graded display,[864] and the Siglent 1000X a 9-megabit synchronous pipeline burst SRAM on the acquisition FPGA.[800] The Rigol DS2000 uses two Cypress CY73 1380C 18-megabit pipelined SRAMs on the display side alone.[360] The Rohde & Schwarz HMO1202 uses three ISSI 9-megabit synchronous SRAMs running around 200 MHz, just over a megabyte per chip.[842]

Where the whole sample memory is small enough, SRAM does that job too. A Micsig handheld with 240 K per channel puts two ISSI 256 K by 18-bit, 4-megabit SRAMs next to the Cyclone FPGA, because SRAM is easy to drive directly from an FPGA and fast; the parts are wide enough that the memory could have been doubled at no layout cost.[810] Fluke's PM3370B Combiscope builds its sample memory from eight 2 K high-speed SRAMs, four per side, with an extra two pads per footprint and 32K/2K jumper links so that 8 K parts can be fitted in place of the 2 K ones for 32 K of sample memory.[1452] Logic analysers follow the same rule, using SRAM rather than DRAM for the trace buffer.[1018][410]

## Battery backup and retained data

Before flash was cheap, the working memory of a pocket organiser, diary or handheld computer was also its only storage, so the SRAM was never allowed to lose power.[1209][1376] These machines were built with one or two operational cells plus a separate backup cell, and removing all of them at once destroys the data, since the only remaining charge is what the main capacitor holds.[1209] The current involved is minuscule — retention life is effectively the shelf life of the battery — which is why such machines are often still holding their contents decades later.[643] Cambridge's Z88 backs its 32 K internal SRAM and RAM packs with a supercapacitor so cells can be swapped without loss.[382] The Casio FX730P steers its batteries with diodes so that during backup only the 8 K 6264 SRAM is powered, not the whole machine.[1102] The Apple Newton stores everything in five Mitsubishi 128 K by 8 SRAMs — 640 K in total — with no flash at all, which is why its backup cell is critical; later Newtons moved to flash.[418] Tandy's portables expose the arrangement as a front-panel memory protect switch that gates the DC-DC converter feeding the memory board while leaving the trickle-charged backup path intact.[1662][1376]

Parts intended for this duty are specified for it: Samsung KM68-series SRAMs are nominally 5 V devices but retain data down to 2 V, which is what allows a 3 V lithium cell to hold them.[942]

## Non-volatile RAM modules

The battery can also be inside the package. Dallas non-volatile RAM and real-time-clock parts are pin-for-pin compatible with an ordinary SRAM of the same size but contain a potted lithium primary cell — not rechargeable — with a guaranteed ten-year factory life.[615][969] Because the cell is internal and encapsulated, its voltage cannot be measured; the device is simply replaced as preventive maintenance, after which the instrument must be recalibrated.[1203] Failures announce themselves through self-test: a Prema 6047 reports error 8 when the checksum stored in the Dallas SRAM no longer verifies, and the part can be substituted with a standard A6116 2 K by 8 SRAM of the same pinout.[615] Prema also wrote the original factory calibration constants into EEPROM, so a flat backup battery does not cost the instrument its calibration — a switch on the rear panel copies the factory values into the replacement SRAM at power-up.[615] Working practice with any such instrument is to read the contents out and archive them before the cell fails, along with the program ROM.[615] Device programmers of the era handle SRAM and non-volatile RAM as distinct families, and the SRAM entries are test routines that write to the part, so reading a battery-backed device intact requires selecting it as non-volatile RAM rather than as generic SRAM.[615][411]

## Security keys

Volatility is occasionally the point. Secure microcontrollers built for payment terminals keep encryption keys in SRAM and expose a self-destruct pin, so that breaking the tamper mesh removes the retention supply and the keys evaporate.[687] The same result is achieved discretely: a terminal with two soldered-in cells for redundancy routes their supply through traces that a drill will sever, erasing the key-holding SRAM.[942] The SRAMs used for this are not always the hardened cryptographic parts that were available; in at least one such design they are ordinary commercial devices.[942]

## On-chip SRAM

In microcontrollers, SRAM is the working memory quoted alongside flash, and its size largely determines what can be attempted. Figures span from 300 bytes on small PICs — enough for practical C given a good compiler[63] — through 2 K on a CH32V003 RISC-V part,[1524] 8 K on an 8051-cored measurement chip,[417] 16 K on an STM32F103[669] and on a PIC24F used in a pocket calculator design,[130] 32 K on a Cortex-M0,[722] 64 K on a Cortex-M4 tweezer,[1336] 96 K in an automotive vision controller,[444] 256 K on a Tiva Connected LaunchPad,[642] and 512 K embedded in a Cypress EZ-USB FX3 to buffer USB 3.[592] DSPs carry their own: a TMS320C5504 has 256 K of internal SRAM alongside 128 K of ROM even where external SRAM is also fitted.[271] Modern low-power design leans on the same memory: a microcontroller in storage mode does nothing but retain its internal SRAM and can do so at around 100 nA while remaining wakeable on interrupt or watchdog.[1242] Selecting a part therefore means budgeting SRAM explicitly against flash rather than taking the largest available, since memory drives package size and price — 64 K SRAM LCD controllers are both expensive and physically large, with a 44- or 64-pin QFN as the smallest option.[1242][1539]

On-chip SRAM is not exempt from silicon errata. The PIC24F series shipped with a long list of documented bugs that included the SRAM, where the reported size was simply wrong.[3]

Structurally, SRAM arrays are recognisable on a decapsulated die as large regular blocks of repeated cells with routing running straight over the top of them.[1380] At the extreme, a Tesla training tile aggregates 1.3 terabytes of high-speed SRAM against 13 terabytes of high-bandwidth DRAM at 1.1 exaflops, the same two-tier arrangement that appears on an oscilloscope board scaled up by six orders of magnitude.[1504]

## Practical notes

SRAM is forgiving of supply decoupling in a way that is easy to underestimate: an entire vintage computer board had every bypass capacitor removed except the main input filter, including the ones adjacent to the SRAM, and continued to run.[1081] It is not forgiving of overvoltage. Finding 7 V on the backup-battery SRAM of a Toshiba T1000LE — a 5 V rated part with an absolute maximum in the region of 6 V — identifies a fault serious enough to explain a dead machine, and is the kind of measurement worth taking before chasing the power supply further.[1527] Common vintage part numbers recur often enough to be recognised on sight: the 6116 2 K by 8,[695][1017] the 6264 8 K by 8,[334][1102] and the 62256 32 K by 8, which was sold in volume as PC cache memory and fitted into sockets beside the processor.[747][1376]
