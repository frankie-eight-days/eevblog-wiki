# soldering tip

The tip is the consumable working end of a soldering iron, and it is the component that decides whether heat actually reaches the joint. Two stations of nearly identical wattage can perform very differently because of tip geometry, tip thermal mass and how the tip couples to the heating element and temperature sensor.[1063][1064] The judgment that follows from this is blunt: "Tips are everything on your soldering iron"[596], and an iron purchase is really a purchase of the tip ecosystem behind it.[5vbg8QEZXfY]

## Geometry

The chisel or wedge tip is the general-purpose shape for both through-hole and surface-mount work, and is available in a range of widths such as 2 mm, 2.5 mm and 3.2 mm.[180][1063] A wedge tip is useful in roughly 95% of soldering applications, and if only one tip can be had it should be a wedge.[180] The conical or round tip supplied as standard with most irons is of little use: a fine point does not have the surface area to transfer heat into the component.[180][168][954] A fine point is still worth keeping for the occasions where a chisel physically cannot fit.[180]

A chisel only works if it is used flat. Tilting the iron so that only an edge touches means the flat of the chisel is not in contact with the pad and little heat enters the joint.[aQ2AVLs8_7k] The chisel point should touch the pad and the pin at the same time.[183] Where a pad sits at an awkward angle, rotating the board rather than the iron is the way to keep the tip flat.[1205]

Well or wave-base tips hold a reservoir of solder whose surface tension drags solder away along a row of pins, which is the basis of drag soldering.[1306][954] A well tip will also wick excess solder back out of a bridged row.[997] Drag soldering does not require one; a standard chisel does the same job.[186] Well tips are also used for reflowing a fine-pitch package in place, where the self-wicking action cleans up as it goes.[9s9LXOBknck][1699]

At the other extreme, a very large chisel spanning most of a package lets an entire row of leftover pins be scraped off at once after chip removal, and can be laid on an exposed thermal pad to heat a whole chip from the copper outward.[1322] For lifting SMD chips, a large thermal mass iron with a decent chisel is required; a conical tip will not get the heat through.[688] Thinner tips earn their place in rework by fitting between tall components without cooking the parts around them.[1106]

## Thermal mass and power

Thermal mass and power are separate properties, and "don't confuse thermal mass with power" is the operative rule: within one tip line, the high-thermal-mass premium tips and the standard tips can carry the same 130 W rating, and the difference is only what heat is retained in the physical tip.[1106] The premium tips are visibly thicker with a bulge in the shank; retaining more heat means the tip temperature dips less when it is applied to a joint.[1106]

A physically thin tip is also a bottleneck for power delivery. A large-capacity tip can accept essentially 100% of the station's output because there is no thin section in the path, while a small bevel or conical tip cannot, even when the base unit is rated at 100 W.[1646][5vbg8QEZXfY] On one such 100 W-rated portable, the supplied small bevel tip appeared to reach only around 50 W peak.[5vbg8QEZXfY] Where the heating element sits behind the tip rather than inside it, the shortfall is worse still.[1646]

The consequences appear at the bench as failures on high-thermal-load work: TO-220 tabs, D-pack packages, large resistors and any pad tied to a ground plane are where small tips run out of capacity.[1645][1646] A miniature cartridge with almost no thermal mass, rated 14 W per tool, is excellent for 0201 and fine-pitch work but will not solder ground planes.[QbeTv41l2EQ] A $5 USB iron with a very thin fine-point tip has virtually no thermal mass, and its limit on heavy wire is set by the shape of the tip as much as by the wattage.[1113] Where a station offers no larger-capacity tip in its range at all, that ceiling cannot be raised by the user.[1645][1646]

Comparative thermal testing is therefore done with identical tips fitted to both irons — for example the same 3.2 mm chisel at the same set temperature, heating identical pads on a board with a ground plane.[1063][596] Tests of that kind show that a 5 W difference in iron rating counts for far less than the design of the ceramic heater element, its heat path and the tip design.[1063]

## Cartridge tips versus separate tips

In traditional construction the tip is only a tip: it is physically separate from the heating element and the temperature sensor, so heat must cross a contact interface and whatever air gap the fit leaves, adding thermal resistance.[1064] In cartridge or direct-heat construction the element and the temperature sensor are built into the tip itself, right at the tip, so power can be delivered faster and the control loop is much tighter.[1106][1064] Such a tip carries multiple contacts — an outer, a middle and a tip contact — to serve the heater and the sensing element through one connection.[400] Because the tip is the heater, the shaft stays cool enough to hold, heat-up is a matter of seconds, and tips can be changed quickly.[358][1065]

Traditional tips are the cheapest option but may not last as long, because without a setback system that cools the tip between joints they sit at full soldering temperature; integrated tips cost the most but can last longer precisely because setback works with them, and recovery from the reduced temperature takes only a couple of seconds.[1065] Some stations add a sensing stand that cuts power when the iron is docked, and the same sensing can be used for hot tip changes.[1106][472][400]

## Plating and tip life

The working surface of a tip is a plating, and its loss is what ends the tip's life. With use the plating pits and wears away, and once it is gone the tip only produces bad quality joints and should be discarded.[183] The plating must be matched to the solder: a tip that is not a lead-free tip should not be used with the corresponding solder, because the wrong combination eats the plating away very quickly and can eat holes in the tip.[183]

Plating thickness is a deliberate design trade-off. Thicker iron plating buys longer tip life at the cost of thermal performance for a given power input, while thinner iron plating gives better thermal performance and a correspondingly shorter tip life — the difference between the Pace and JBC tip philosophies.[1106]

Temperature is the other lever. Holding a tip at full soldering temperature for ten or twenty-four hours a day shortens its life and the plating lasts a great deal less, which is why decent stations implement automatic temperature setback on the stand.[1645] Some implementations reduce power after a delay; others switch the element off entirely and fall to room temperature.[1106][1645]

Routine care is cleaning and tinning. Wiping on a wet sponge while rotating the tip leaves it clean for the next joint, and brass or steel wool cleaners serve the same purpose in a stand.[183][1063] A freshly tinned tip is kept tinned as a matter of habit.[1281][1699] A film of solder on the tip also improves thermal contact, and briefly applying solder to the iron before touching the joint heats that joint faster than a dry tip does — though this must not become the only technique, since solder is properly fed to the far side of the joint rather than to the tip.[183]

## Ranges, compatibility and cost of ownership

Because tips are consumables bought repeatedly, their price and the breadth of the range dominate long-run cost. Standard tips for one 130 W station start at about $10–11 and its high-capacity ultra performance tips run about $14–17, roughly a third of the price of the equivalent JBC tips; combined with longer life from thicker plating this gives a much lower cost of ownership.[1106] Tips for one portable station cost about $20 each with no high-capacity option in the range at all.[1646] One station ships without any tip in the box.[1106]

Cross-compatibility is common and worth checking. T12-style Hakko tips are available in genuine and copy versions.[1254] The FX951-pattern tip also appears on TS100- and TS80-class irons and their clones.[D2PANd9Hu3U] A successor portable iron may be fully backward compatible with the previous model's tips, protecting an existing investment.[1319] Several stations and desoldering tweezers are built around JBC-compatible cartridges, in the tweezers' case clamped by grub screws that should not be over-tightened.[OvGdE5hC1Ro][1650]

Tip quality should be matched to the iron. On a decent station only genuine tips are worth trusting, and cheap marketplace tips are not; but on a $16 clone iron the cheap tips are the sensible choice, since one or two genuine tips cost more than the whole iron.[596] A four-dollar assortment for a budget lab will not last long but will do the job, and should include chisels, a well type for drag soldering and larger tips for bigger work.[954] A working bench keeps at least two irons and a range of tips.[51]

Tips have also been made by hand: a thin piece of copper tubing pushed over the element, flattened and filed to a point, with the solder taken up between the tubing and the element.[874]
