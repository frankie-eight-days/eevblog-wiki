# bandwidth

Bandwidth is the range of frequencies over which a system passes a signal within a defined amplitude tolerance, conventionally bounded by the −3 dB point where the response falls to 0.707 of its midband value.[977][692] It is one of the primary figures of merit for oscilloscopes, probes, amplifiers, and signal sources, because it sets the fastest signal content the instrument or circuit can reproduce without attenuation.[600][487] Bandwidth also carries an unavoidable cost: wider bandwidth admits more noise, a trade-off that recurs in scope front-end design, probe selection, and low-noise amplifier specification.[601][528]

## Measurement and the rise-time relationship

For a system with a Gaussian response, bandwidth can be estimated from the 10–90% rise time of a step or fast pulse using BW ≈ 0.35 / tr, and the same relation applies to fall time.[70][433] Some derivations use BW = 1/(π·tr), which makes an 8 ns slew rate equivalent to roughly a 40 MHz signal — relevant when judging whether a probe or isolator can reject fast common-mode edges.[1119] In practice the constant matters: applying 0.4/tr to a measured 630 ps rise time yields 635 MHz, but that figure still omits the rise time of the measuring instrument itself, and different scopes implement different rise-time measurement methods, so pulse-based bandwidth estimates can overestimate by tens of megahertz if the instrument's own rise time and method are not accounted for.[311] The −3 dB point can also be found directly by sweeping frequency and watching amplitude fall to 0.707 of the reference level, for example 707 mV out from a ×100 amplifier driven with 10 mV in.[572][977]

## Op-amp bandwidth and gain-bandwidth product

An ideal op-amp has infinite bandwidth; a practical device might offer anywhere from 100 kHz to 100 MHz, and its closed-loop bandwidth falls as gain rises, governed by the gain-bandwidth product.[600] Gain-bandwidth product is not perfectly constant — it varies between individual units and with supply voltage, test frequency, and input and output voltage — so measured bandwidths rarely land exactly on datasheet predictions.[572]

Splitting gain across cascaded stages raises system bandwidth. A single op-amp at ×100 gain measured 13.4 kHz bandwidth; reconfiguring the same total gain as two ×10 stages (each stage then having 145 kHz bandwidth) pushed the system to roughly 100 kHz, close to the 93.3 kHz predicted by the cascaded-bandwidth formula.[572] The same principle underlies multi-stage compound amplifiers, where two ×10 stages give ×100 total gain with more bandwidth than a single ×100 stage.[1609] The technique is a useful fallback, but the preferred engineering practice is to select an op-amp with adequate gain-bandwidth product for the job in a single stage.[572]

Chopper (auto-zero) amplifiers illustrate the opposite end of the trade: they essentially eliminate 1/f noise and DC offset, but their bandwidth is limited to a couple of kilohertz because the chopping frequency is typically only 10–15 kHz, and overload recovery can take 5–10 ms.[24] Wideband op-amps make the complementary trade, with input bias currents of microamps because the internal architecture is optimized for bandwidth and slew rate rather than input current.[479]

## Oscilloscope bandwidth

Oscilloscope bandwidth is commonly set in software rather than hardware. Many scopes ship with the full analog bandwidth present in the vertical front-end amplifiers, with license keys limiting the lower-priced models: the base 100 MHz HMO1202 contains the same front end as the 300 MHz version, and the bandwidth limit can be altered by sending commands over the SPI bus to the front-end chip.[879][e4wvxWWMla0] The Rigol DS1052E similarly converts to the 100 MHz DS1102E specification by changing the model number so the firmware selects the higher mode.[70] The Keysight 1000X EDU model is artificially limited to 50 MHz bandwidth and 1 GSa/s through a product-configuration setting, versus 2 GSa/s and higher bandwidth in the standard version.[978][977] Whether a given model is hackable depends on whether the hardware is shared — the Siglent SDS1104X-U, for instance, is 100 MHz only with no 200 MHz option and a different ADC/memory configuration, making a bandwidth hack unlikely.[1355]

Bandwidth interacts with sample rate: a four-channel scope sharing one ADC may deliver full sample rate on one channel but halve it with two channels and quarter it with four, which caps the usable bandwidth when multiple channels are active.[703][1563]

## Probe bandwidth

A ×1 oscilloscope probe has dramatically lower bandwidth than the same probe on ×10 — typically an order of magnitude or more. One Rigol probe is specified at DC–8 MHz on ×1 versus DC–350 MHz on ×10, a consequence of the lossy transmission-line resistance built into the ×1 probe's coax and the input capacitance it presents.[441][453] Simulation of a complete probe-plus-scope system shows the effect of the scope input: a probe alone might model at 7.8 MHz, dropping to about 6.5 MHz when the scope's nominal 15 pF input capacitance is included.[1445] Because a ×1 probe bandwidth-limits the measurement, it can yield a lower-noise reading when measuring wideband noise, but it is unsuitable when the specification under test (e.g., a power supply's 0–20 MHz noise bandwidth) exceeds the probe's 8 MHz passband.[441]

High-voltage differential probes trade bandwidth for safety and isolation; typical units offer only 20–70 MHz, and their common-mode rejection, while excellent at 50 Hz, collapses by 10 MHz, leaving them unable to reject fast common-mode edges equivalent to tens of megahertz.[932][1631][1119] Current probes are similarly constrained: the AIM-TTi I-Prober 520 positional probe is DC–5 MHz, adequate for viewing waveforms but marginal for 1–2 MHz switching-regulator work, while clamp probes range from 100 kHz at the low end through 800 kHz and 2.5 MHz models, with ~50 MHz units costing several thousand dollars.[296][1413] Injection transformers for control-loop measurements are an exception, achieving near-flat response from 1 Hz to 10 MHz — performance not available in off-the-shelf transformers.[1104]

## Bandwidth and noise

Displayed and measured noise scale with bandwidth. A wide-bandwidth scope shows a thicker, fuzzier trace on a DC signal than a narrow-bandwidth one, and engaging a 20 MHz bandwidth limit visibly cleans it up.[223][601] In a direct comparison, dropping two scopes from full bandwidth to 20 MHz reduced measured noise from a 65 µV mean to 38 µV, and this is the correct way to compare noise floors between scopes of unequal bandwidth.[1223] The noise penalty is baked into the hardware: a front-end ASIC designed for 1.5 GHz is inherently slightly noisier than one designed for 700 MHz, even when the wider front end is software-limited to 20 MHz.[1529] The same physics applies to amplifiers — broadband HEMT low-noise amplifiers used in deep-space receivers admit noise across the whole spectrum, which can mask a narrow signal of interest.[1547]

Noise specifications are therefore always quoted over a defined bandwidth. Op-amp peak-to-peak input noise is conventionally specified over 0.1–10 Hz, while bench power supply output noise is typically specified over 20 MHz.[528] Voltage noise density in nV/√Hz converts to total RMS noise by multiplying by the square root of the measurement bandwidth.[528][1325] Effective number of bits calculations likewise depend on the noise floor over the bandwidth of interest.[107]

## Bandwidth in other instruments

Multimeter AC bandwidth varies enormously with price and architecture. Handheld true-RMS meters based on simple converter chipsets manage only 1–2 kHz, causing short pulse currents to read low, while extended-bandwidth true-RMS chipsets are reserved for higher-end models.[1007][1731][cBNOxg8Px8M][Q_RYG_5cQk8] Bench meters do far better: the Agilent 34461A offers 300 kHz AC bandwidth using a digital RMS technique, while the U1253A holds 0.4% accuracy only over a limited band and degrades to about 3.5% across its full 100 kHz bandwidth.[489][56]

Arbitrary/function generators in the hundreds of megahertz are a distinct and expensive class — a 500 MHz, 2.5 GSa/s four-channel unit sits near the top of the market at around $5,000–6,000, with most function generators topping out near 100 MHz and anything faster normally being an RF signal generator.[cziiWo6Uh5M][1679] USB instruments show bandwidth as a system property: the Digilent Analog Discovery's combined DAC-plus-ADC loop measures 10 MHz at −3 dB against a 5 MHz claim, and a source-measure unit's 1.7 MHz bandwidth with a 4 MSa/s converter sits near its Nyquist limit.[692][607]

On spectrum analyzers, resolution bandwidth governs sweep speed: a wider resolution-bandwidth filter sweeps faster, the same trade-off as on any swept instrument.[795] A spectrum analyzer's low-frequency limit is set by its AC-coupled input — 9 kHz minimum on the Rigol DSA815 — distinguishing it from DC-capable dynamic signal analyzers.[391]

## Bandwidth in communications and RF systems

In RF and communications work, bandwidth is the resource that buys information capacity and resolution. Glass delay lines achieve fractional bandwidths of 80% or more of their carrier frequency, passing substantial information through what is essentially a piece of glass with transducers, with the most linear response obtained when the transducers are tuned with LC loads rather than plain resistive terminations.[386] Traveling-wave tube amplifiers remain in service in radar and satellite systems because they combine high bandwidth and high gain with low noise figure and high reliability at power levels solid-state devices struggle to reach.[XqakD0dXdjM] Moving to frequencies above 110 GHz in 6G research is motivated precisely by the bandwidth available there, which translates directly into data throughput and, for radar, into finer range resolution.[o2NxHu5Bsnk] The underlying principle is that a pure sine wave occupies a single frequency with no sidebands, so many independent signals can be packed into the same total bandwidth on different frequencies and separated by sharp filtering — the foundation of RF telecommunications.[1417] The term extends to data networks, where link bandwidth limits throughput and fiber infrastructure removes the ceiling imposed by copper.[uO4cxVw8wfU][9iDRynyBl0c]
