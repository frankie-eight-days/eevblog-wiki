# bridge rectifier

A bridge rectifier is an arrangement of four diodes that converts an AC input into a pulsating DC output, steering current through the load in the same direction regardless of input polarity. It is the standard front-end rectification stage in both linear and switch-mode power supplies, and it appears throughout electronic equipment in roles ranging from mains conversion to multimeter input protection. Functionally, "all the diode bridge rectifier is doing is current steering where the current needs to go."[1482]

## Operation and characteristics

In a full-wave bridge, two diodes conduct on each half-cycle, so the current path always includes two diode drops in series. Each drop is nominally about 0.6 V, rising toward 1 V at higher currents, so a bridge can waste around 2 V of the available headroom.[711][735] Because the output polarity is fixed regardless of how the AC (or unpolarised DC) input is connected, equipment fitted with a bridge on its input can be hooked up either way around with no concern for polarity.[682][735]

Full-wave rectification doubles the ripple frequency seen by the following filter capacitor: 50 Hz or 60 Hz mains produces 100 Hz or 120 Hz ripple, whereas a half-wave rectifier leaves ripple at the mains frequency itself.[594][222] The DC produced from rectified mains relates to the AC figure by the 0.707 factor: 350 V DC corresponds to roughly 245 V AC, which permits a rectified-DC-fed supply to be substituted directly for mains in some equipment.[1657]

At power-up, the discharged bulk capacitor acts as a short circuit, and inrush current is limited only by wiring resistance and the equivalent DC resistance of the bridge itself; this is why such supplies commonly use slow-blow rather than fast-blow fuses.[1406]

## Role in power supplies

The canonical offline power supply front end runs mains through a fuse, common-mode choke, X- and Y-class suppression capacitors and MOVs, then into the bridge rectifier, whose output feeds the high-voltage bulk capacitor.[268][533][1355][e98cYNPUxcg] That bulk capacitor must be rated for the rectified peak — 400 V or 450 V parts are typical on 240 V mains.[e98cYNPUxcg][dLlhoUHlnjE] In supplies without power factor correction, the bridge feeds the bulk storage capacitor directly, and the resulting short conduction angle is precisely the condition a PFC stage is inserted to correct.[273]

Linear supplies follow the same pattern at lower voltage: transformer tap, bridge, filter cap, then a linear regulator such as a 7805, LM317 or LM723 with pass transistor.[804][613][621][C_tXWwcyZlc] Multi-channel instruments typically give each isolated channel its own transformer tap, bridge rectifier and filter, with separate smaller bridges for control circuitry.[440][1174][621][829] A centre-tapped arrangement with two series filter capacitors produces a split rail — each capacitor sees only half the total voltage, giving, for example, ±24 V from a 48 V overall rail.[1714]

In a crude capacitive-dropper supply, the simplest possible regulated mains supply is a series capacitor or resistor feeding a diode bridge and a Zener diode; the bridge alone does not regulate — a regulation element is required.[1482][1481] Undersized dropper capacitors starve the bridge of voltage, a failure that leaves a 24 V relay seeing only 9–13 V and unable to pull in.[1481]

## Power dissipation and thermal design

Bridge rectifiers are power semiconductors and their thermal treatment is a visible marker of design quality. Heavier units are bolted to dedicated heatsinks, sometimes with temperature monitoring.[268][30][314] Some designs mount several bridges — main and auxiliary — on a single shared heatsink.[1402] Airflow matters: placing the bridge directly in the fan stream, ahead of the main heatsink, is regarded as sound thermal layout.[452] Cheaper practice omits the heatsink entirely, relying on heat-shrink sleeving and extra lead length for a little dissipation.[462] A lab power supply bridge measured peaking over 90 °C under full load is cause for concern.[828]

Small ceramic capacitors placed directly across each diode inside the bridge are a recognised technique for suppressing higher-frequency transient noise generated by the diodes, even though the fundamental is at mains frequency.[1156] High-voltage isolation slots routed into the PCB around the bridge are standard practice on mains-connected boards.[674][1355][1042]

## Multimeter input protection

A bridge rectifier is a core element of multimeter current-input protection. Connected across the current shunt resistor, it clamps the fault voltage to a few diode drops so that a gross overload — voltage applied to a current jack — blows the HRC fuse rather than destroying the precision shunt.[373][929][372] The bridge configuration is used precisely because the applied fault may be of either polarity, or AC; the clamp works either way.[373] In the Fluke 27, the bridge plus four additional series diodes gives a 3.6 V nominal clamp, the extra diodes raising the clamp voltage to suit a higher burden voltage; where burden voltage is under two diode drops (about 1.2 V), the bridge can simply be shorted out.[376][373] Variants using four, five or six discrete diodes in bridge configuration serve the same purpose, and a surface-mount DF10S-type package rated at 1,000 V is typical.[929][1692]

The protection diodes need be neither fast nor high power — ordinary 1N4007 parts suffice — because the energy only has to be withstood for the time the fuse takes to open: "Fuses don't blow instantly. They take seconds to blow."[373] The 10 A range is normally excluded from bridge protection, since its heavy shunt and direct current path take care of themselves.[376][931] A meter lacking a PTC, MOVs, HRC fuses and a diode bridge on its inputs is, as engineering judgment, not well designed.[373]

## Other applications

- **Average-responding RMS meters** use what is effectively a full-wave bridge to produce the absolute value of the input — flipping the negative half up — before the averaging and calibration stages.[1448]
- **Transient clamping**: bridges across balanced audio-amplifier inputs clamp extreme transients to the supply rails.[354]
- **Earth-leakage breakers**: one implementation places the trip solenoid in series with the AC side of a bridge, with the thyristor across the bridge output, so that tripping shorts the bridge and removes its own power, latching everything off.[1164]
- **Solenoid drive**: automatic transfer switches power their changeover solenoids through individual diode bridges wired essentially across the mains.[1500]
- **Energy harvesting**: integrated converters for piezoelectric sources embed a full-wave bridge to rectify the AC element output, followed by a buck converter.[534]
- **Synchronous rectification**: a controller driving four MOSFETs in the bridge arrangement replaces the lossy diodes; "the low on-resistance of the MOSFETs eliminates the loss," recovering the roughly 2 V a diode bridge would otherwise drop, with parts such as the LT4320/LT4220 handling up to 72 V peak at 60 Hz, or 600 Hz for the LT4320.[711]
- **LED retrofit tubes** exist in a type carrying a bridge rectifier off each pin; these are hazardous to install live and the fitting should be powered down first.[362]

Cost-reduced products are frequently identified by what is missing: fake USB chargers substitute a single half-wave rectifier for a full bridge,[388] and cheap mains lamps use a capacitive divider into a bridge with no constant-current control at all.[1278][1191]

## Testing and failure modes

A bridge is checked in or out of circuit with a diode test: from the negative output terminal to each AC input, and from each AC input to the positive output terminal, each junction should read one diode drop, about half a volt; the output terminals should also be checked for a short.[682][1726] Bridges usually fail open, which presents plainly as absent output voltage.[804] A rarer and harder-to-find mode is intermittent thermal failure, in which the bridge drops the rectified rail under load once warm and recovers on cooling — repeatable, and capable of dragging down every derived rail in a multi-tap linear supply.[804] In vintage equipment the apparent culprit is often not the bridge at all but decades-old dry solder joints with inadequate flow-through on its pins, cured by reflowing or replacement as a matter of course; pads on 35-year-old boards may lift during the work.[804]

Where a bridge feeds an under-voltage symptom, the bridge itself is rarely the root cause: a starved dropper capacitor or collapsed upstream rail is more likely, and the rail voltage at the bridge output should be measured first — Dave Jones's own stated troubleshooting rule is "Thou shall measure voltages," offered after he replaced a heater's bridge rectifier unnecessarily before finding the real fault in the dropper capacitor.[1481] When measuring capacitance in circuit, a test signal of 0.1 V RMS rather than 0.5 V RMS is used specifically so that bridge rectifiers and other active junctions in the surrounding circuit are never turned on.[1474]
