# multimeter

A multimeter is a hand-held or bench instrument that measures multiple electrical quantities — at minimum voltage, current, and resistance, hence the name "multi".[926][643] It is fundamentally a voltage measurement device: even current measurement is performed by passing the current through an internal shunt resistor and measuring the resulting voltage drop.[UJjMt2-k99c][731][72] It is the primary first-line diagnostic instrument in electronics, but every measurement it makes disturbs the circuit under test to some degree, and most of the practical knowledge around multimeters concerns understanding and controlling that disturbance.[1604][1747]

## Architecture

A typical handheld multimeter is built around a dedicated multimeter chipset that integrates the processor, analog front end, and ADC, with an input voltage divider, multiplexers, and shunt resistors ahead of it.[1598][912][853] Analog multiplexer chips such as the 74HC4051 are extremely common inside multimeters for range and signal switching because they cost only cents each.[931] A change of chipset can be recognised externally by a change in range layout, such as AC and DC volts moving onto a single dial position.[5v8qX9PrjnA] Single-chipset designs are typically bare-bones and lack closed-case software calibration, since the internal processor has no EEPROM to store calibration constants.[853]

The current-measurement front end typically consists of a shared milliamps/microamps jack and a separate amps jack, each feeding a high rupture capacity (HRC) fuse and then a shunt resistor referenced to the common terminal — in amps mode a multimeter is essentially a current shunt input to ground.[929][731] HRC fuses in quality meters are rated at 1,000 V so they can safely interrupt high-energy faults; some cheap meters use HRC fuses with inadequate voltage ratings.[929] The voltage and resistance measurement circuitry is effectively an entirely separate circuit from the current front end.[929]

## Counts, digits, resolution, and accuracy

Multimeter displays are specified either in digits or counts, and the two are interchangeable. A 3½-digit meter displays ±1999, where the "half" means the most significant digit can only be 0 or 1; this is a 2,000-count meter, while a 4½-digit meter is a 20,000-count meter.[26] Marketing terms such as "three and three quarter digits" have no real standard behind them.[26] Count and digit figures describe resolution only — they have nothing whatsoever to do with accuracy, and conflating a "10,000 count resolution" with "10,000 count accuracy" is a basic error.[26]

The basic DC volts range is the most accurate function on any multimeter — more accurate than resistance, current, or capacitance — and sometimes the millivolt range is better still.[75] A good meter will have ohms accuracy close to its DC volts accuracy.[75] DC current accuracy is hardest to achieve because it depends on the shunt resistor, and accuracy figures are typically worst on the 10 A range and best on the microamps range.[75] This is also why low-current measurement instruments feed their output into the multimeter's voltage range rather than its current range: the voltage range is the most accurate.[72]

## Burden voltage

Every multimeter measures current by dropping a voltage across an internal shunt resistor, and that drop — the burden voltage — is subtracted from the circuit under test. It is the single largest hidden error in current measurement, affecting every multimeter on the market.[2][1604] A good meter such as the Fluke 87 exhibits 1.8 mV of burden per milliamp, so measuring 200 mA drops 360 mV — enough to push a 5 V circuit out of specification, let alone a 3.3 V or 1.2 V circuit.[i069j6S80OU][2] Burden voltage can reach several hundred millivolts to a volt at full-scale reading, and it changes with both the current being measured and the range selected: the microamps range has a much higher internal shunt resistance than the milliamps range, so switching ranges changes the measured current.[1604] Inserting the meter adds the shunt resistance in series with the circuit, so a 1 V source across 100 Ω no longer delivers 10 mA once the shunt is included.[1604]

The practical mitigations are: switch to the amps jack, whose much lower shunt resistance minimises circuit disturbance at the cost of resolution (on the order of 0.1 mA on a typical handheld); or measure the voltage across an existing series resistor in the circuit instead of breaking into it.[324][1604][804] Burden voltage can be measured directly by connecting a second multimeter across the current terminals of the first.[1604] Dedicated current-to-voltage adapters such as the µCurrent exist specifically to solve this problem, amplifying the drop across a very small shunt (e.g. 10 mΩ) so the meter reads it on its accurate voltage range with minimal circuit impact.[2][72][1318] Redesigning the conventional front end to lower burden voltage across both shunt and fuse is achievable at little or no additional cost, but most manufacturers do not bother.[929][931]

## Input impedance and circuit loading

On voltage ranges, a digital multimeter presents approximately 10 MΩ input resistance — around 11.1 MΩ on some designs — on almost every range, AC or DC.[286][1399][1747] In high-impedance circuits this load forms a divider with the circuit and corrupts the reading: as an order-of-magnitude rule, a meter impedance one order above the circuit impedance causes roughly 10 % error, and two orders roughly 1 %.[1399][286] The effect is easily demonstrated by placing a second 10 MΩ meter in parallel with the first, which visibly drops the reading.[584] The same load explains why a 9 V battery's measured terminal voltage sags under even the light load of a meter, an effect of the battery's internal ionic resistance rather than temperature.[515] As a rule of thumb, the meter's impedance should be at least an order of magnitude above what is being measured, or the disturbance becomes too great.[286] Precision meters offer a high-input-impedance mode (gigaohms, effectively infinite), but usually only on the millivolt and lower voltage ranges; handhelds may only offer it on the millivolt range, and some bench meters drop back to 10 MΩ above the 10 V range.[584][1399]

A related trap is measuring voltage "behind" a rectifying or blocking element: leakage through a diode combined with the meter's 10 MΩ input resistance can produce a reading of several volts where zero is expected.[1747]

## Resistance measurement

An ohms range works by sourcing a known current through the unknown resistance and measuring the voltage drop across it, so anything else in parallel with the resistor — or any external voltage fed into the nodes — invalidates the reading.[1622] Measuring resistance in-circuit, even on an unpowered board, gives wrong values whenever other components provide parallel paths, and measuring on a live circuit can inject currents and voltages that scramble the reading entirely; manufacturer manuals themselves warn that ohms mode on a live circuit gives incorrect readings.[1622] The open-circuit voltage a meter sources on its ohms ranges is unspecified and varies with range and battery: one meter produced 2.8 V on its 60 MΩ range but 7.3 V on its 600 Ω range from a 9 V battery.[1622]

Some meters offer a low-ohms range that outputs less than 0.6 V, below the threshold needed to turn on semiconductor junctions, allowing in-circuit resistance checks without diode conduction corrupting the result; the feature was very common on 1980s multimeters and appears by default on some modern ones.[1622] Very low resistance measurement requires milliohm resolution for tasks such as tracing shorted power rails — a healthy 3.3 V rail will not read 0.1 Ω in both directions with no capacitive charge-up, and chasing such a short needs at least a 4½-digit and preferably a 5½-digit meter or an LCR meter with good low-ohms capability.[398][230][1061][401]

Diode test ranges have their own ceiling: a meter that tests to only 15 V cannot forward-bias high-voltage devices such as a 36 V zener or diodes with forward voltages up to 52 V at rated current, since the range only pushes a couple of milliamps.[1395][1452]

## Safety and input protection

Measurement-category (CAT) ratings define the energy a meter can safely survive. In a high-energy test at roughly 4 kV — about half the 8 kV used for CAT III qualification — a properly CAT III rated meter survived and failed safe, while non-compliant meters smoked, burned, and appeared ready to catch fire.[84] Well-designed ohms-range protection will survive 240 V mains applied directly to it, though recovery to an accurate reading may take many minutes; surviving 240 V on the ohms range is a standard benchmark of input protection quality.[252][1622][91] Metal-oxide varistors inside the meter clamp overvoltage transients extremely quickly, limiting applied surges to around 1.8 kV even from multi-kilovolt sources.[1016]

Fusing practice has subtleties: meters with a 600 mA range marked "fused" are commonly shipped with a 400 mA fuse rather than 600 mA.[1377] Replacement fuses must be selected against the average time-versus-current curve — HRC fuses broadly share the same ballpark curves, and a substitute should be checked against them, e.g. blowing in about 20 ms at 5 A.[376] Properly certified meters avoid internal rechargeable batteries with USB ports, because exposed external metal would fail third-party safety certification.[D2PANd9Hu3U]

The low-impedance (LoZ) voltage mode, designed to eliminate ghost voltages, doubles as a safe discharge tool for charged mains filter capacitors, which can hold 300–400 V for tens of minutes where no bleed resistor is fitted.[1655][631][816][1388] Conversely, the amps mode serves as a fuse-protected shorting probe — useful for pulling reset lines low or discharging nodes — presenting only the fuse resistance plus the shunt, on the order of a couple of ohms, while giving a direct readout of any current flowing.[777][1702][1322][710]

## Additional functions and lesser-known capabilities

- **Capacitance and inductance.** Capacitance mode is common but only accurate to a couple of percent at best. Because multimeters excite the component at a range-dependent test frequency rather than a fixed one, the capacitance range can be pressed into service to indicate inductance values — a little-known trick that gives usable indications for reasonably sized inductors, provided range-dependent frequency and unit conversions are handled carefully. A proper LCR meter remains far superior, offering selectable test frequencies (100 Hz to 100 kHz) plus ESR, Q, and dissipation factor.[1465]
- **Temperature.** Temperature-capable meters almost universally use K-type thermocouples, and more advanced models include an internal sensor reporting the meter's own temperature.[1392]
- **True RMS and AC behaviour.** AC voltage ranges physically AC-couple the input, stripping DC offset, and are bandwidth-limited (a handheld may require the signal to be reduced to 1 kHz); "AC+DC" mode is distinct from true RMS.[1223] Budget meters are typically average-responding rather than true RMS.[91]
- **4–20 mA process loops.** Any meter's current range reads a 4–20 mA loop directly, but a few meters add a dedicated mode displaying the loop as 0–100 %.[DX_f0Cg6pHo]
- **Data logging and dual measurement.** Logging meters allow watt-hour and amp-hour battery capacity measurements by recording voltage and current to the cut-off point; specialised meters measure voltage and current simultaneously and integrate energy directly.[141][774]

Fundamental limitations remain: a multimeter cannot display time-varying signals, which is the entire reason oscilloscopes exist, and its update rate cannot track rapidly changing voltages.[926] Very short current pulses are oscilloscope territory; meters respond to them with sluggish, inconsistent decay behaviour.[cBNOxg8Px8M] A meter measuring DC voltage is not measuring power, and a regular ohms range cannot measure loudspeaker impedance.[1633][1519]

## Calibration and traceability

Calibration is fundamentally about measurement confidence and uncertainty — a lab expresses a confidence level (e.g. three sigma) that an instrument is within spec, built on the demonstrated long-term stability of its standards; nothing is ever 100 % certain.[500] At the top of the chain, 8½-digit meters such as the HP/Agilent/Keysight 3458A serve as transfer standards in calibration facilities, and dedicated boxes of precision resistors such as the Fluke 5450A served as resistance transfer standards for calibrating multimeters.[1330][544]

Formal paid calibration is not always warranted. A practical alternative is to cross-reference two or three meters against each other and against inexpensive voltage-reference chips or off-the-shelf ~$50 reference standards, continually verifying volts, ohms, and amps; a meter known to meet spec from the factory anchors the group, and agreement among several meters gives confidence none has drifted.[374] Some workflows mandate two meters for any critical measurement in case one is out of spec, with cross-checks typically following the manufacturer's recommended interval of around 12 months.[26][75] Certain professional meters add a self-check mode so the instrument can be verified immediately before a critical measurement.[4UY3uI6tl1E] Out-of-the-box spread across a batch of identical meters is small enough that batch comparison against a reference is meaningful.[852] Instruments should also be respected outside their specified environment: consumer meters run at 2 °C — well outside spec — will drift from their reference readings.[101]

## Bench practice and selection

Anyone serious about electronics needs at least two multimeters: voltage and current frequently must be measured simultaneously, and two meters can be compared against each other to detect drift.[75][26][168] Dave Jones's standing bench rule is that a good lab needs four multimeters, because characterising a power supply or converter requires input voltage, input current, output voltage, and output current at the same time; multiple meters also allow hands-off monitoring of several rails at once, which matters for safety around high voltage.[110][67][664][803]

On selection: as a rough rule, over about $100 buys a decently engineered, safety-compliant meter, while under $50 is largely poor-quality territory; sticking to established brands — Fluke, Keysight/Agilent, Gossen Metrawatt, Brymen, Extech, Amprobe, BK Precision, Hioki, Yokogawa — is the safe path.[75][168][1636] For electronics work specifically, a microamps range is essential — milliamps alone is insufficient — along with volts, ohms, capacitance, continuity, and reasonable DC current accuracy.[168][91] Electrical-trade meters differ from electronics meters in lacking microamp ranges and low capacitance ranges.[973] Price and capability decouple sharply at the low end: a $20-class meter outperformed the $42 Fluke 101, which omits current measurement entirely, while a $100-class Brymen offers independent UL listing and CAT IV rating.[1574][1636] At the bench end, second-hand 6½-digit meters can be found from roughly $200–500, and the 6½-digit class reveals effects — like battery ionic resistance under tiny loads — invisible to lesser instruments.[1184][515][1680] Metrology-grade handhelds have reached 7½ digits (Prema 6047, Keithley DMM7510), and Gossen produced what is believed to be the only 6½-digit handheld.[615][731][1680]

Combination instruments extend the format: oscilloscopes with built-in multimeters (from the 1975 Tektronix 213's 0.1 % true-RMS meter to modern tablet scopes with 60,000-count meters), insulation-resistance testers with full multimeter functionality, and graphical logging meters with multiple channels.[628][1761][894][WDYOSddZwto] Scope-integrated multimeters implemented by simply switching the oscilloscope's own ADC to another channel deliver notably ordinary specifications compared with dedicated meter front ends.[430]

Environment and robustness complete the picture: IP67-rated meters survive 3 m drops and immersion, and electromagnetic susceptibility is a real differentiator — conducted or coupled RFI at around 10 MHz and 10 V peak-to-peak drove one meter's current reading off by orders of magnitude while other meters were unaffected, a flaw serious enough to require a manufacturer remediation program.[868][933][987]
