# kickstarter

Kickstarter is a reward-based crowdfunding platform on which a hardware creator offers pledge levels against a funding goal, collects money from backers only if the goal is met, and then delivers a product.[581][noNyZ2A3k4Q] For small electronics manufacturers it functions as a way to get a production run commission-prepaid — proof that people will actually buy the thing before a thousand pieces are committed to.[1759] It is not a retail channel: pledge levels are minimum contributions to a project rather than prices in a catalogue, and the platform was deliberately not designed as a store.[121]

## Funding mechanics

Funding is all-or-nothing. A campaign that fails to reach its goal collects nothing, in contrast to Indiegogo's flexible funding, where the creator keeps whatever is raised regardless of whether the target is met.[noNyZ2A3k4Q] Because backers pledge rather than buy, a pledge at a given level may exceed that level's minimum by any amount, which leaves the creator with a database of mismatched pledges to reconcile manually against actual rewards at the end of the campaign.[121]

Fees are material to a hardware budget. On a campaign raising $116,575, Kickstarter's fixed 5% cut plus roughly 3.1% taken by the credit card merchant came to just over 8% of the total.[581] Payment is not immediate: charges are placed on backers' cards when the campaign closes, the money sits with Kickstarter for approximately two weeks while charges are verified, and only then is it released as a single lump sum.[581][4tcC_TdhzUU] For a campaign ending 31 December, funds arrived on 15 January.[581] That delay is a real constraint on a hardware project, since parts cannot be ordered until the money lands — purchase orders raised in anticipation of the transfer carry the creator's own risk.[4tcC_TdhzUU] Some platforms behave differently: on the Australian site Pozible, funds were released to the creator as soon as the target was reached, and PayPal was accepted, which Kickstarter did not do.[581]

Payment plumbing varies by region. Australian campaigns did not use the Amazon payment system used for US backers; the Australian arm collected through a third-party credit card merchant and held the money itself.[581]

## Campaign management

The backend supports the logistics of fulfilment reasonably well. Backer surveys collect shipping addresses, responses update live so a creator can watch addresses come in and message backers who have not yet responded, and the whole backer list can be exported to CSV as many times as wanted — sorted by pledge level or by backing order, so that the earliest backers can be shipped first, and imported into a spreadsheet for sorting by country and feeding to a label printer or mailing house.[581] The principal limitation of the survey system is that it can only be sent once.[581] Project page formatting is minimal, with no formatting available inside the pledge descriptions.[581] The comment system does not support nested comments, so a campaign owner can post a global comment but cannot reply to an individual backer's comment.[121] Kickstarter also hosts live video from within a campaign page, with backers and non-backers viewing it on the Kickstarter site rather than on an external streaming platform.[121] A Make 100 campaign format exists as a way to gauge interest in a kit at small volume.[1690]

## Discovery value

Kickstarter's own traffic contributes less to a campaign than is often assumed when the creator already has an audience. For the µCurrent Gold campaign, platform statistics attributed 22% of pledges to Kickstarter itself, with the remainder arriving from external referrers — 279 pledges direct, 262 from the creator's own website, 211 from the video channel.[581] Because some of that 22% consisted of people who already knew about the project and simply went to Kickstarter directly rather than clicking a link, the realistic figure for genuinely incremental sales is closer to 10 to 15%.[581] The pledge curve is characteristically front- and back-loaded: a strong opening driven by an existing audience, a long flat middle, and a rise at the end as people move before the campaign closes.[581]

## Delivery risk

Something on the order of 60 to 70% or more of hardware projects on Kickstarter fail to deliver on schedule, which is unsurprising to anyone who has taken a product through electronics manufacturing.[467] The majority of failures are not fraud; they are well-intentioned projects that do not deliver.[467] A common failure pattern is running out of money during the transition to production — reaching a headline funding figure that still leaves the project in the valley of death, short of what volume manufacture actually costs.[1186] Another is a complete absence of logistics capability: a campaign that raises a large sum and then has six weeks to deliver, without appreciating that building kits requires a warehouse of parts to pick from and floor space measured in tens of thousands of square feet.[wuFqB9ZeqKI] Anyone starting a campaign without at least the basics of manufacturing and logistics lined up beforehand should not be running one.[4tcC_TdhzUU]

Schedule slip is routine even for competent projects. A backpack strap light backed in early 2016 with promised June 2016 delivery shipped in July 2017, over a year late.[1010] Long stated timelines are themselves a warning: a campaign whose own roadmap runs through prototype version five, engineering validation, design validation, consumer testing and packaging finalisation is a year away at minimum, with a great deal of mechanical and production risk still ahead.[1251] For the 121GW multimeter, choosing a December delivery date proved to be the mistake, and the campaign itself was launched roughly a year and a half into a development that had already been re-spun mid-project.[e9cpKN69Avk] Pre-production lifecycle testing on that project was carried out under time pressure to get the Kickstarter units out, without the luxury of a month to qualify a test jig.[Bh3gcBr0DKs]

## Vetting and the scam problem

Kickstarter requires a demonstrable working prototype before a project can be listed, a rule that pushes projects with nothing tangible toward Indiegogo, which has fewer requirements.[467] Kickstarter is also nominally reviewed: each project is verified and approved by staff, of whom roughly 70 to 75 of 148 Brooklyn-based employees work on the community side.[HfqpuhYiR3o] The vetting is not technical. Free-energy and over-unity projects have been approved and funded on the platform, including one seeking $1.6 million for a machine whose own campaign video describes a closed loop of perpetual motion two minutes in.[HfqpuhYiR3o] The remedy is straightforward — retain or employ an engineer competent to evaluate technical campaigns, which non-technical staff working from a checklist cannot do.[HfqpuhYiR3o] Reporting projects does sometimes work: a graphene infrared heater campaign claiming eight times the efficiency of conventional heaters was eventually shut down after being reported.[HfqpuhYiR3o]

The physics of the failures is usually elementary. A panel radiator with graphene paste smeared in it is still a panel radiator, and no amount of graphene changes the number of BTUs needed to heat a room against its leakage — a 200 W panel does not produce as much heat as a 1,500 W radiator, and a campaign whose own test showed 440 W consumed for a nominally 300 W panel has not verified its own specifications.[1186] Once a creator is committed, every test they run will be slanted toward a favourable result; only properly controlled A-B comparisons settle it.[1186] Linking a patent application in which the examiner dismisses the applicant's entire argument is a self-inflicted wound for an over-unity campaign.[aTpm62ToNOM] Claims of harvesting usable phone-charging energy from Wi-Fi signals recur on the platform and do not survive an energy budget calculation, RF harvesting being viable only for ultra-low-power sensor applications.[729]

Marketing convention is a further tell. Crowdfunding videos are now routinely shot and edited by professionals with multiple cameras and off-axis cut-ins, so production quality carries no information about the product.[1163] An as-seen-in or as-featured-in logo strip is a staple of Kickstarter and Indiegogo campaigns and frequently cites outlets with no technical standing.[1290] Buzzword loading is similar: Bluetooth connectivity added for its own sake because that is how money is raised, blockchain tokens bolted onto an idea with no coherent basis, graphene invoked as a mechanism.[1238][1099] An open source hardware gear logo on a campaign page conveys nothing verifiable either, since it may mean full compliance with the community definition or merely a schematic released as a PDF.[921]

The blunt test for a backer is whether the thing already exists: confronted with a fancy product on Kickstarter, ask whether it can be bought somewhere already, and what the device actually does when stripped of the marketing.[1454]

## Secondary predation

A successful campaign attracts an immediate flood of unsolicited email from marketing, fulfilment and manufacturing intermediaries offering to handle supply chains, upsell conversion and backer happiness.[4tcC_TdhzUU] Some of these services — logistics, manufacturing liaison — are genuinely useful; the volume and the sales approach are not, and an auto-filter is the practical response.[4tcC_TdhzUU] Indiegogo separately solicits successful Kickstarter campaigns to move over to its in-demand program, and traffic between the platforms is predominantly in that direction.[lKCNS85RDdA]

Outright campaign cloning also occurs. The 121GW campaign was copied wholesale onto Indiegogo — backer levels, photographs, text, profile picture and the announcement video — by an unaffiliated party who had raised $1,000 from six backers before the fraud was flagged, plausibly the product of a bot scraping successful Kickstarter campaigns.[lKCNS85RDdA] Campaigns cannot be fully deleted from Kickstarter; a cancelled project's name remains even after the owner deletes their account and photo.[U_ftKolqq80]

## Representative hardware campaigns

The µCurrent Gold campaign raised $116,575 against a $9,900 goal from 1,268 backers.[581] The 121GW multimeter raised A$644,000 from 2,296 backers, having passed $113,000 with 417 backers and $147,000 with 539 backers during the campaign, against a goal of $42,000 that was chosen more or less arbitrarily.[4tcC_TdhzUU][121] Its predecessor campaign had drawn a little over a thousand backers for a lower-cost product with correspondingly broader appeal.[121]

Elsewhere on the platform, the Pebble Time smartwatch raised $20 million from about 78,000 backers, twice the sum raised by the original Pebble, which had 68,000 backers.[758] The uArm four-axis desktop robotic arm reached $164,000 with 856 backers against a $5,000 goal, with the funds earmarked for hiring software developers — a well-founded priority, since software tooling is the binding constraint on a small robot arm whose serial command set is otherwise sufficient for users to write their own control code.[586] The Giroptic 360cam raised around $1.5 million.[734] Digilent's OpenScope MZ raised roughly $107,000 from just over 1,000 backers.[1056] The Chronos high-speed camera, developed over about ten years by a single designer and priced at $2,500 against comparable instruments costing five to ten times as much, was funded from a genuine working prototype rather than renderings.[947] The Edison educational robot raised around $112,000 against a $20,000 target and delivered.[711] Cartesian Co raised US$140,000 for a desktop PCB printer, selling nearly a hundred units, and was among the first projects on the Australian arm of the platform.[614][VGwCZ8_EzqI]

Small campaigns are common and viable: a T-slot construction beam raised about $27,000 against a $12,000 ask,[715] a set of manual SMD tapes trays for hand pick-and-place met a sub-$1,000 goal and took a few thousand,[722] and a student space group raised $15,000 for laser driver research hardware.[1110] A USB-C power supply adapter campaign drew almost 1,800 backers and $141,000 and delivered.[1749] The Vion multimeter raised over half a million dollars across simultaneous Kickstarter and Indiegogo campaigns, delivered late after repeated delays, and demonstrated that the ability to run a campaign and keep backers updated is unrelated to the ability to implement basic instrument functionality.[1238]
