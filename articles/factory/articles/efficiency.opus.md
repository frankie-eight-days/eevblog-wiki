# efficiency

Efficiency is the ratio of useful output power to input power, expressed as a percentage, and it governs how much of the energy entering a circuit does the intended job rather than becoming waste heat.[1401] Any time power is dissipated, efficiency is the question that follows, because the shortfall has to go somewhere and almost always ends up as a thermal problem.[1401][895] The concept is arithmetically trivial and practically unforgiving: it is the single figure that decides whether a converter runs cool on a scrap of aluminium or cooks itself, and whether a battery-powered product lasts hours or minutes.[895][139]

## The definition and its two forms

Efficiency in percentage is output power divided by input power, times 100.[1401] Because the difference between the two is loss, the same quantity can be written as output power divided by output power plus losses, times 100 — the same number approached from the loss side rather than the input side.[1401]

A pure resistor is, by this definition, 100% efficient: one watt in is one watt of heat out, and heat is exactly what a resistor is for.[1401] Efficiency only becomes less than 100% once the intended output is something other than the heat being produced.[1401] The equation applies cleanly when both sides are electrical — electrical power in, electrical power out.[1401] It does not apply directly when the output is a different form of energy altogether: an LED producing lumens rather than watts has a lumens-per-watt figure and its own component-level conversion efficiency, so its losses sit outside the power-in-power-out equation.[1401] The same caveat holds for mechanical output such as force, where conversion losses, internal resistances, and wiring losses all subtract from the useful result.[1401]

## Efficacy is not efficiency

The distinction between efficacy and efficiency is a persistent trap in lighting.[50] A figure such as 132 lumens per watt typical efficacy is not an efficiency number and tells nothing about how efficient the LEDs actually are, nor how much waste heat the design must handle.[50] Extracting a true efficiency figure requires going to the theoretical response curves rather than the headline specification.[50] The two terms are often used interchangeably by people unaware of the difference, and depending on the luminous efficiency and the context they can genuinely coincide, which is what makes the confusion durable.[50][362] The practical consequence is thermal: an LED emitting on the order of 0.8 watts of light energy into a couple of square millimetres will burn a finger placed directly on it even while the heatsink and base beneath remain cool to the touch.[50]

## Efficiency curves and how they mislead

For a switching converter, efficiency is not a number but a curve — efficiency in percent on the Y axis against output current on the X axis — and it is the first thing to look at when selecting a part.[139][259] A headline claim such as a 96% efficient synchronous boost converter, or 94% for a competing part, describes one point on one curve under one set of conditions.[139]

Datasheets carry different efficiency curves for different output voltages, and reading the wrong one is a common and costly error: a curve showing 85% at 1 mA still holding 85% at 100 mA before tailing off may turn out to be plotted for a 1.8 V output rather than the 5 V output actually being designed for.[139] The correct procedure is to find the curve for the intended output voltage, where the family of traces for various input levels shows how much output current is available — a higher input voltage supports more output current, so a 3.6 V battery input may allow a full amp out where a 1 V input allows only 200 mA.[139] Parts must be checked by exact part number, since devices in the same family carry different graphs, parameters, and specifications.[139]

Some converters offer more than one operating mode, with a normal mode that preserves efficiency at very low or near-zero load currents alongside a PWM mode intended for higher continuous loads.[139] Substituting a different brand of external component around a switcher can move operation to a different part of the efficiency curve, alongside effects such as increased output ripple.[1475]

## Measuring it on the bench

Measurement is a matter of instrumenting both sides simultaneously — input voltage, output voltage, and output current — and stepping the load across the range of interest.[110] Sweeping 0 to 100 mA in 10 mA steps produces a workable performance curve for a small converter.[110] Constant power mode on an electronic load is useful when plotting efficiency against output power rather than output current.[324] The arithmetic is direct: 52.4 W out from 69.7 W in gives roughly 75%, and the 17.3 W difference is what the heatsink must dissipate.[895]

Real curves share a characteristic shape. Efficiency rises from a poor value at very low current, peaks somewhere near the current the designer optimised for, then declines.[110][324] A small unregulated 2 W brick specified at up to 80% delivers 80% or better across the upper half of its range from 1 W to 2 W, around 75% at half a watt, and drops away sharply at very low loads.[324][957] A general-purpose brick that stays above 80% from 20 mA to its rated 100 mA but never exceeds 90% anywhere is unremarkable but adequate.[957] Quiescent behaviour is part of the picture: a module drawing 0.390 W with its output switched off draws 0.6 W switched on with no load at all, and that difference is part of its efficiency story.[1031]

Plotting the results requires a scatter plot rather than a line chart if the X axis is to be scaled correctly, and expanding the Y axis rather than starting it at zero exposes detail that would otherwise be compressed into the top of the frame.[1031]

## What real hardware achieves

Characterisation of a wide-range buck module illustrates how strongly efficiency depends on the operating point rather than the part.[1031] With 15 V in, a 12 V output reaches roughly 96% right up to 3 A, and 5 V out from the same input peaks near 90%, tapering to a still-respectable 82% at low currents.[1031] The same module at 1.5 V out from 15 V in peaks at only about 68% and is worse at high current, despite delivering less total power — 4.5 W against 15 W for the 5 V case.[1031] With 30 V in, the losses grow and the 5 V output falls under 80% at 3 A.[1031] At 40 V in, the 32 V output again returns about 95%, which is where the designers evidently optimised it, since that is the maximum-power worst case of about 70 W they must design the thermals around.[1031] Efficiency at the bottom of the current range is poor across every configuration, but at low output current it matters little because very little power is being dissipated in absolute terms.[1031]

Other measured examples fill in the range. A wide-range DC-to-DC regulator running from 48 V delivers around 75%, but the same unit at 15.2 V in and 5 A reaches about 86%, running the heatsink at a much cooler 56 °C as a result.[895] A 360 W bench supply measured about 86.8% efficient at 60 W output, dissipating around 9 watts without getting hot.[1265] A discrete switchmode design built as a teaching example peaked just under 60%, which is unimpressive but not inherently wrong.[110] At the other end, an LLC resonant converter reference design measured about 97.5% and held 94% at worst case across its input voltage range.[1294] An isolated brick specified for 1000 V isolation and 2 W output claims up to 80%, typical for that class; parts optimised for a specific load and exceeding 90% exist but cost considerably more.[324]

## Efficiency as a design constraint

The 80% figure of a small isolated converter propagates straight into a system budget. A USB port supplies at most 500 mA, so 500 mA in through an 80%-efficient converter yields about 400 mA out, which is why a 2 W converter is the correct match for a nominal 2.5 W USB port.[324]

Component choice sets the achievable number. Schottky diodes have a much lower forward drop than ordinary PN junction diodes — around 0.142 V against roughly half a volt for a 1N4001 or 1N4148 — which is precisely why they are used in switchmode supplies where efficiency matters and power should not be thrown away in diode losses.[1747] Magnetics matter as much: 90%-plus efficiency is achievable in a DC-to-DC converter if the inductor is chosen correctly, and changing an inductor to 47 µH in a working design raises the peak above 65% while moving the peak to a lower current and causing a steeper drop beyond it.[895][110] Inductor series resistance and output filtering diode choice both feed into the resulting curve.[110]

Efficiency also decides topology. A Zener diode as a shunt regulator is acceptable for powering a circuit only where efficiency is not a concern; its more defensible uses are in niche roles inside larger circuits, such as reference voltages.[908] Where a design already has a modification applied for another purpose, such as passive power-factor correction with added inductors, the unknown efficiency impact is a reason for caution — switching-transistor losses and IC heating cannot be assumed unchanged without full characterisation.[1253]

In battery-powered products, a DC-to-DC converter makes the load essentially constant power rather than constant current, so battery-life estimation should be based on constant-power discharge curves — with the converter's efficiency across the load range folded in.[1533] Higher converter efficiency directly minimises waste from the battery, which is why it ranks high even when many other selection parameters can be ignored.[139] The same logic scales up: drawing DC directly from a battery pack rather than passing through an inverter to 240 V AC avoids the inverter losses and yields higher efficiency.[WFhk1aRqLD8]

Digital control of power conversion has widened what is achievable, allowing correction of anomalies that analogue control could not address and, in high-performance industrial inverters, digital characterisation of individual transistors to extract maximum efficiency.[1032]

## Efficiency as a debunking tool

Because efficiency is bounded and multiplies through a chain, it is a fast test of extraordinary product claims. Ultrasonic power transfer through air loses about 50% over one metre from air absorption alone, leaving 25% at two metres before any other loss is counted, and that figure already assumes 100% efficient transducers, no nonlinearities, and perfect focusing.[n4NBUruLyoo] A venture still designing acoustic experiments to measure efficiency, output pressure, and transducer performance five years in is not close to a practical product; the basic engineering guarantees it cannot be made practical.[rLly4WdRAjY]

Similar reasoning applies to a battery-extending boost converter: even granting that useful extra output can be extracted in favourable circumstances, the converter's own efficiency — perhaps 90% at the optimal point on the curve but more realistically 70 to 80% — plus a few hundred microamps of quiescent current, sets a hard ceiling well below marketing claims of large percentage improvements.[751] Even a mechanical energy source is bounded the same way: an average person cycling flat out might generate around 500 W, and conversion efficiency reduces that to a couple of hundred watts of usable charging power.[89]

Efficiency claims for whole systems deserve the same scrutiny. A solar-powered electric car priced at 267,000 US dollars is not dramatically more efficient than far cheaper alternatives — one competitor at roughly a tenth the price is only about 40% better than a conventional production EV — and no amount of on-body solar makes all-day solar driving realistic.[1480]

Efficiency is also conditional on operating point in non-electrical systems. A heat pump is only efficient within a narrow window of flow rate; too slow, too fast, or too large a temperature differential all degrade it.[tA2qm1-BVCY]
