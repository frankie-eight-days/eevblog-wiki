# solar roadways

Solar roadways are road, cycleway, or pavement surfaces built from photovoltaic panels protected by a load-bearing glass, resin, or polymer top layer, intended to generate electricity from land already used as roadway. The concept matters as a recurring case study in engineering feasibility analysis: multiple heavily promoted trials in the Netherlands, France, China, Germany, and the United States were built between 2014 and the early 2020s, and every one of them underperformed, degraded, or was removed, confirming predictions made from simple back-of-the-envelope energy and cost calculations before any were constructed.[681][1047][1233][1603] The consistent measured result across all installations is roughly one-half to one-third the energy yield of an ordinary rooftop or solar-farm installation at several times the cost, before accounting for the severe durability and maintenance problems of driving on glass.[850][1047][1603]

## Concept and claimed features

The archetypal proposal, from the US company Solar Roadways founded by Scott and Julie Brusaw in 2006, was to replace roadways, parking lots, sidewalks, driveways, and bike paths with interlocking hexagonal glass panels containing solar cells, LED lighting for lane markings and signage, pressure sensors, wireless communications, and internal heating elements intended to keep the surface free of snow and ice.[1738][sJS2paVW-kc][1050] The company claimed the panels could support 250,000 lb vehicles and that converting all roads in the lower 48 US states could produce over three times the country's annual electricity consumption.[1738] Other implementations took different forms: the Dutch SolaRoad project used large prefabricated concrete modules with cells embedded under a translucent gritty top layer, and the French Colas Wattway used thin (~7 mm) resin-polymer-covered cells glued directly onto an existing road surface, the most engineering-credible approach of the three.[850][1047][1276]

## Measured energy yield

Real test data from the major trials converge on the same figure: about half the output of a comparable conventional installation.

- A first-principles estimate for the Solar Roadways concept, using a high-end panel laid flat with realistic system losses, gives a best case of 380 Wh/m²/day, equivalent to roughly 9 US cents of electricity per square meter per day, or about $33/m²/year.[632]
- The 70 m × 1.75 m (122.5 m²) SolaRoad cycleway in the Netherlands was specified by its own developers at 50 kWh/m²/year (137 Wh/m²/day); its first full week produced 100 kWh, or 117 Wh/m²/day, closely matching that expectation.[681] Over its six-month trial it generated 3,000 kWh, or 24.5 kWh/m², against an average of 47.2 kWh/m² from nearby rooftop systems monitored over the identical period — almost exactly double.[743]
- The 1 km Colas Wattway installation in Normandy (2,800 m² of panels, 336 kW nominal, €5 million) delivered 82 kWh/m²/year against 146 kWh/m²/year for ordinary consumer rooftop systems — 56% of rooftop output — and averaged 288% less per unit area than a modest home installation over 11 months of measured data.[850][1047] It was projected to produce 150,000 kWh/year but produced 80,000 kWh in 2018 and 40,000 kWh in 2019, and a cumulative 366 MWh over roughly eight years — about 61 MWh/year, enough for only a handful of average households.[1603]
- Solar Roadways' own SR4 installation in Sandpoint, Idaho (30 panels, 1.5 kW nominal) produced 0.46 kWh on a day when a nearby angled 4.4 kW rooftop array produced 10.85 kWh — a 13:1 advantage to the rooftop per unit of installed capacity on the same day.[1269]
- A later Dutch solar cycleway installation achieved only 275 full-load hours, 70% less yield than regular solar panels of the same capacity.[1556]
- The Peachtree Corners, Georgia installation was expected to produce about 1,300 kWh/year feeding an EV charger — less than half the annual output of a comparably sized 3 kW home rooftop array.[1356]

## Loss mechanisms

The shortfall is not incidental; it follows directly from the configuration. Panels laid horizontal rather than tilted lose roughly 20–30% of yield relative to angled mounting; reference data for the Wattway location show a flat 336 kW system producing 288 MWh/year against 345 MWh/year tilted, a 22% penalty from orientation alone.[743][1047] The thick textured protective glass or resin layer required to survive traffic attenuates light by a further 10–15%, and an opacity test on an actual recovered fragment of the Dutch surface confirmed that covering a cell with the road-surface material destroys most of its output.[743][1276] Ground-embedded panels lack the airflow beneath a rooftop array, so they run hotter, and photovoltaic efficiency falls with temperature.[743] On top of this come shading by vehicles, dirt, grime, and leaf litter, which also create non-uniform illumination across panels that perform best under uniform light.[632][1603]

## Durability failures

Driving on solar panels proved to be the decisive problem, exactly as predicted.

- The original 2014 Krommenie SolaRoad cycleway strip (90 m × 1.7 m) had to be redone in 2016 after surface damage; a recovered fragment shows the gritty resin top layer peeling away from the roughly 3 mm base.[1276]
- The Wattway road in Normandy deteriorated within three years: panels cracked, delaminated, and splintered, sections were declared unrepairable and demolished, and the surface was noisy enough to require a 50 km/h speed limit. A 400 m section was rebuilt in 2019, and the installation was being removed entirely by 2024.[1603][1234]
- The SolaRoad heavy-traffic bus-lane pilot in South Holland (100 m, opened 7 March) was closed within days after surface damage; the official finding was that "the solar panel and the top layer release from the underlying concrete of the road element", causing distortion and progressive failure, and that repair was not possible, so the province discontinued the pilot.[1234]
- The 2 km solar highway in Jinan, China, built with "transparent concrete", was damaged within days of opening in late December, with investigators concluding the panels were broken by rocks and debris carried in tire treads or thrown from vehicles.[1050][1233]
- A solar bridge installation in Hengalow showed its resin grip coating completely worn away across entire patches under traffic.[4GUygSdf92A]

Vehicle loading also induces microcracking in the cells and flexing that lifts panel edges and admits water.[1603][1234] Colas Wattway's managing director conceded in 2019 that "Our system is not mature on long-distance traffic," announcing a retreat to small 3–9 m² modules for powering CCTV cameras, bus-shelter lighting, and e-bike charging instead of roads.[1603][1234]

## Economics

The cost structure is as unfavorable as the energy yield. A rooftop system runs about €230/m² installed, while a road-based system starts at €300/m² and carries maintenance burdens rooftops do not have; on measured output the rooftop is at least four times more energy-productive per euro, the difference that makes rooftop solar pay back in roughly six years while a solar road can never pay for itself.[681] Applying the Dutch developers' own verified figures, the SolaRoad prototype's revenue works out to about €10/m²/year against its construction cost — a nominal payback on the order of 1,500 years for the €3 million, 70 m prototype.[681] Wattway's management admitted costs around €6 per watt-peak with a best-case aspiration of €3/Wp; the Normandy road ultimately delivered one-third the capacity of a conventional installation for nine times the cost, a figure later revised to fifteen times on the observation that the €5 million spent on the 0.336 MW road could have built a 5 MW solar farm producing reliably for decades.[850][935][1047][1603] Solar Roadways' SR3 panels cost about $2,000 per 44 W panel — 22 times the cost per watt of commercial rooftop solar — and even a hypothetical tenfold cost reduction would leave them four to five times more expensive, before maintenance and the road-specific infrastructure of cable pits, drainage, and wiring.[935]

Ancillary electrical loads make the picture worse. By Solar Roadways' own measurements, the LED lane markers and supporting circuitry consume about a quarter of the energy the tiles produce per lane-mile, for LEDs not visible in daylight.[935]

## The snow-melting claim

The flagship Solar Roadways promise of heating the panels to keep roads snow- and ice-free fails on basic latent-heat arithmetic. Snow at about 10 lb/ft³ covering a 4.4 ft² tile to a depth of six inches is 22 lb of ice per tile, requiring about 930 Wh per tile merely to transition to water — 27.9 kWh for the 30-tile Sandpoint array. That array is a 1.5 kW system, so even eight hours of full-sun maximum output supplies less than half the energy required for the phase change alone, in a location and season where actual insolation is far lower.[1269] In practice the panels had to be cleared by shoveling, and the heating elements produced only small melt holes in the snow cover.[1050][TL94sJ3Ykgs][wnp3FnAGiyw]

## Engineering assessment

The consistent engineering judgment, borne out by every built trial, is that the concept takes existing photovoltaic technology — already a marginally economic proposition even in optimal installations — and places it in its worst possible configuration: flat, shaded, soiled, covered by lossy protective layers, thermally isolated, and mechanically abused by traffic.[632][1047][1234] No amount of development changes the comparison, because conventional rooftop and ground-mount solar improves in cost and efficiency in parallel, preserving the gap indefinitely.[681][1356] The correct deployment of the same panels is on rooftops, on poles, as canopies over car parks and canals, or in solar farms; the frequently cited counter-example is the 32 km South Korean cycleway covered by an elevated solar canopy, which keeps panels clean, angled, unmolested by vehicles, and maintainable for decades while sheltering the path beneath.[1356][681][743][sJS2paVW-kc] Niche decorative applications such as park lighting were the only use ever conceded as potentially defensible, and even there a panel on a pole beside the path outperforms one embedded in it.[681][743]
