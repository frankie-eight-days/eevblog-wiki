# electrolytic capacitor

An aluminium electrolytic capacitor is a polarised capacitor built from a rolled sandwich of aluminium foil and dielectric soaked in a liquid electrolyte, sealed inside a metal can.[33][1714] It is by a wide margin the worst-behaved passive component in common use — lossy, temperature-sensitive, polarity-sensitive, and finite-lived — and simultaneously unavoidable, because nothing else delivers comparable capacitance for the volume.[33] Power supply filtering, bulk decoupling, energy storage and low-frequency AC coupling all still depend on it.[33] It is also the single most common failure point in mass-market electronics: high ESR in electrolytics is probably the number one failure mode in consumer products, and a dead plasma TV, set-top box or DVD player is odds-on a dried-out capacitor in the power supply.[33]

## Construction and polarity

The interior is a large roll of foil and dielectric that can be unwound by hand from a sacrificed part.[1714] The electrolyte is a liquid, and the can is sealed — which introduces both a seal that can fail and a fluid that can escape.[9V99J22aiLE][1636] A cross-shaped score mark pressed into the top of the can is a deliberate weak point: if internal pressure builds, the can bursts there rather than at the seal or the base.[1636][1727][857] The presence or absence of that vent is the quickest way to tell a wet electrolytic from a solid polymer part on a board.[976][1405][1288][1152]

Polarity is marked on the body, conventionally by a stripe identifying the negative terminal, and on the leads, the longer being positive.[1448][aQ2AVLs8_7k] Tantalums invert the convention and mark the anode instead, which is a standing trap when working across both types.[1448] Reverse voltage is tolerated only to roughly one volt; above about a volt to a volt and a half, internal self-heating vaporises the electrolyte, pressure builds, and the part leaks or explodes.[42] A 470 µF 10 V part connected backwards across a 15 V supply capable of 40 A vents violently and boils its electrolyte out.[857] The one-volt threshold is used deliberately in design: a single diode placed across an electrolytic clamps any reverse excursion below the damage point.[909] Layout can also protect the part, as when a charger places its main electrolytic filter cap downstream of the reverse-polarity switching transistor so a backwards input never reaches it.[397]

## Wear-out and failure

Electrolytics fail by drying out. Internal ripple current flowing through the equivalent series resistance heats the part from the inside, external heat sources add to it, and the electrolyte gradually escapes or evaporates, driving ESR upward.[763][682][347] The process is self-accelerating: higher ESR means more internal heating, which dries out more electrolyte, which raises ESR further, until the part bulges, vents and stops working entirely.[347] Life is inversely proportional to operating temperature — the hotter the part runs, the shorter its rated life — and heat is correctly described as the enemy of the electrolytic capacitor.[33][1714]

The visible signatures are a domed top near the vent, a ruptured vent seam, ooze on the can, and corrosion of the surrounding board where the escaped electrolyte has eaten tracks and component leads.[347][763][1073][1203] Most failed parts do bulge.[365] Not all do, however, and a capacitor with no external sign at all can still be electrically dead, so visual inspection alone is not a test.[1474] A dielectric breakdown mode also exists that leaves no external trace: micro-arcing between adjacent layers of the roll under ordinary operating voltage, which in an audio amplifier presents as intermittent popping from the speaker.[1714]

## Temperature ratings and placement

Ratings of 85 °C and 105 °C are the common grades, with 125 °C parts appearing in better-specified equipment.[347][1091][764] An 85 °C part is generally not good enough where a 105 °C part is warranted, and a bill of materials that stops at 85 °C is a reliable indication a product was built down to a price.[347][1313] The rated life on the data sheet is quoted at a stated temperature and is easily thrown away by placement: mounting a capacitor beside a heat sink, a hot regulator, a power resistor dissipating its full rating, or between two heat sinks all raise the can temperature and derate its life.[33][105][191][52] The thermal design task is not only keeping semiconductor junctions within their data sheet limits but keeping the temperature-sensitive parts nearby — electrolytics above all — inside theirs.[1347] In one bench-supply investigation, filter caps sitting adjacent to a heat sink running near 130 °C reached about 70 °C on the cans with no airflow, and a nominal 5 V rail run that hot leaves no design margin at all; 105 °C-rated parts in that position will still die in short order.[512] An enclosure that permits 80 °C internally will shorten capacitor life regardless of the rating printed on the can.[1298]

Cold is not a free win either. ESR of a wet electrolytic rises sharply at low temperature.[33]

## Ripple current and paralleling

Every electrolytic has a maximum charge-discharge or ripple current on its data sheet; exceeding it heats the part internally and shortens life.[33] This is why the unregulated input-side capacitors in a linear supply fail before the output-side ones — they see far more ripple, work harder, and self-heat more.[1203] Series designed for the job are specified accordingly: the Nippon Chemi-Con KMH range is intended for linear supplies and high ripple current, rated 2,000 hours of continuous operation at 105 °C at full rated ripple current.[440]

Placing two or more electrolytics in parallel, rather than one large part, is a reliability technique rather than a packaging accident: the parallel bank shares ripple current, shares the resulting heat, and lowers the combined ESR.[33] Beyond that, a properly specified capacitor position carries a whole set of requirements — capacitance, voltage, ESR, ripple rating and more — of which several are more easily met by several parts than by one, and the practice is common enough in power supplies to be worth understanding on sight.[742][859]

Series connection solves the opposite problem. Ten 1,000 µF 200 V parts cannot sit directly across a rail derived from 240 V mains, so they are stacked in pairs to give 500 µF at 400 V.[268] A 600 V supply likewise stacks 450 V parts in series, with balancing resistors across each to force equal voltage sharing rather than relying on the capacitors' own internal ESR to balance them.[814]

## High-frequency behaviour

An electrolytic makes a poor high-frequency bypass. Its internal parasitic inductance and ESR mean that placing one directly at a chip's supply pin and ground barely improves the supply noise; it is useful for bulk decoupling and nothing more, with the high-frequency work left to a small film or ceramic part.[1085] Impedance sweeps bear this out: a 47 µF electrolytic self-resonates somewhere around 8 to 9 MHz and rises in impedance above that.[859] Large capacitance on the output of an op-amp is separately troublesome and can provoke oscillation, so a stabilising bulk capacitor often belongs on the input side instead.[577]

## Testing

The instrument for testing an electrolytic is an ESR meter, not a capacitance meter, since capacitance can read close to nominal while ESR has risen far enough to stop the circuit working.[33] Because the test signal is small, polarity is irrelevant when measuring ESR and good capacitors are not harmed by connecting the leads either way round.[365] Large-value electrolytics are also, conveniently, exactly the class of part that can be measured usefully in circuit — small values cannot be, because their reactance at the test frequency is too high — and an in-circuit reading is good enough for most diagnosis.[1474][1636]

Typical numbers from bench practice: a healthy 1,000 µF 10 V secondary-side filter cap measured about 0.01 Ω at 100 kHz against a rough chart figure of 0.1 Ω; a 470 µF 35 V part reading 0.15 Ω against an expected 0.1 Ω is marginal but ambiguous; ordinary, non-low-ESR 50 V 47 µF parts sit near 0.28 Ω, and readings climbing into whole ohms are cause for concern.[630][1714] A part measuring 25 Ω at 100 kHz in circuit is unambiguously dead.[1203] Capacitance failures can be equally dramatic — a nominal 220 µF reading 3 µF, or a capacitor so far gone that an LCR meter in auto mode will not identify it as a capacitor at all.[1527] Side-by-side comparison of identical parts on the same rail is a useful discriminator, though two parts that measure the same are more informative than one part that measures oddly, since intermittent faults do not always show up as gross outliers.[630]

## Replacement and selection

When replacing, capacitance and voltage may be increased freely but never reduced: go up in voltage without concern, up in capacitance without concern, and never down in voltage.[365][347] For switch-mode supplies the replacement should be a low-ESR type and 105 °C rated.[347] Higher-value substitutes are physically larger and may not fit the original footprint.[347] For a full refurbishment of old equipment intended for long service, every electrolytic in the unit is replaced as a matter of course; a targeted repair often gets away with recapping only the unregulated input side.[1203]

Brand matters more here than for almost any other passive. The reference names are Panasonic, Nippon Chemi-Con, Nichicon, Rubycon, Elna, Cornell Dubilier and, historically, Sprague.[763][1457][1314][803] Counterfeits and low-grade parts are widespread, so data sheets and reputable supply channels are part of the specification, not an optional extra.[33] Panasonic's FR series parts carry no Panasonic branding at all on the can, which can lead to a good capacitor being wrongly suspected.[763] Brand choice is also read as a proxy for a product's overall build standard — bottom-tier capacitors in an otherwise well-engineered instrument stand out precisely because the rest of the design is careful.[347][1146][823][828][864][591]

## Alternatives and adjacent types

Solid polymer aluminium parts and hybrid electrolytics eliminate the liquid seal and the wear-out mechanism that goes with it, and are identified on a board by the absence of a pressure vent in the top.[9V99J22aiLE][1288][1405][1357] Tantalums are themselves a form of electrolytic despite being spoken of as a separate family, and are inherently more reliable than the aluminium system as well as far smaller, which makes them the preferred choice where a bulky aluminium part would not fit — negative gate bias rails, for instance.[9V99J22aiLE] Boards built without any wet electrolytics simply do not have this failure mode available to them.[217][1145]

The dominance of the aluminium electrolytic in older designs is partly a materials-availability artefact. Before large-value ceramics existed, 1 µF or 10 µF was an enormous ceramic part, so anything larger had to be electrolytic; a 100 µF ceramic is taken for granted now, if still expensive.[1209] Well-made older parts have proven durable — the electrolytics in a 27-year-old portable computer showed no leaking or bulging at all.[116]

## Handling

A large electrolytic holds its charge. Without a bleeder resistor across it, a mains-side filter cap can retain several hundred volts and take tens of minutes to decay on its own, and a supply's main capacitor may still be charged after the regulator has switched off.[631][804] Connecting a charged electrolytic to an LCR meter can destroy the instrument, which is why such meters warn to discharge the device under test first.[757] The low-impedance voltage function on a multimeter, intended for suppressing ghost voltages, doubles as a safe way to bleed a capacitor down before working on a board.[631]

Mechanically, tall cans are glued or bonded to the board with silicone to stop them flapping in the breeze and vibrating, and a can left unsupported is a legitimate criticism of a design.[814][738][1714] Electrolytics also survive desoldering well enough to be salvaged from scrap boards and reused, provided the donor equipment has not been thermally stressed.[542][619][1302]

## An oddity

Heating an aluminium electrolytic with a hot air source causes it to generate a current of its own — tens of microamps, enough to be measured plainly and observed across parts from entirely different manufacturers.[1455] The effect explains reports of LED power indicators glowing faintly on boards passing through a reflow oven, and it does not appear to damage the capacitor: measured parameters, including series resistance, are unchanged afterwards.[1455]
