# ribbon cable

A ribbon cable is a flat, side-by-side bundle of conductors used to carry many parallel signals between two boards inside a single enclosure. In its classic form it terminates in insulation-displacement connectors on 0.1-inch headers, an arrangement old enough to appear in early-1980s portable computers and still common in modern instruments[116][334][261]. It is the default answer whenever a design is split across more than one PCB — front panel to motherboard, processor board to display, power supply to everything else — and much of what can be learned about a product's internal architecture can be read off where its ribbons go[903][880][810].

## Role in multi-board architecture

Splitting a product across boards is usually forced by mechanics or manufacturing rather than by electronics, and the ribbon cable is what stitches the pieces back together. A keyboard instrument may be divided in two simply because nobody wants a board that long going through a pick-and-place machine, with a ribbon at each end of the split[1108]. A processor board that cannot fit everything gets a secondary board connected by a ribbon underneath, a board that disappears entirely once the design is consolidated with surface-mount parts[695]. Where a connector has to sit flush with a front panel, the connector goes on a front panel board and its signals come back over a ribbon[1612].

The same cable is routinely used as a distribution bus. Power supply outputs are brought over as a ribbon rather than discrete wiring because it is simply a neater way to do it[536][591]. Instruments daisy-chain ribbons from board to board, and an omitted link in the chain is itself a clue — a supply built with a spare connector deliberately left unpopulated is a partly populated version of a larger design[755]. Because a ribbon presents every rail on an accessible row of pins, it also makes a convenient troubleshooting point: pulling the cable from a power supply board exposes all the output voltages for measurement in a way that probing the assembled motherboard does not[538].

The choice of what to route over a ribbon defines the boundary of a design's fast circuitry. High-speed analog paths are carried on purpose-designed, expensive board-to-board interconnects; the ribbon is reserved for the lower-bandwidth run from the acquisition engine to the display[790]. In a high-security electronic lock the reasoning is different but equally deliberate: only a few three- to five-pin ribbons cross to the keypad assembly, because putting no active electronics on the exposed outside face minimises the attack surface[1484][1483].

## Current, decoupling and grounding

Ribbon conductors are thin, and treating them as a power bus has consequences. A design that omits local bulk decoupling and instead draws large current pulses through a long ribbon from another board forces those pulses down thin wire shared with low-power digital lines, and the power and ground rails bounce accordingly at currents of a couple of hundred milliamps[1365]. The remedy is local bulk capacitance — hundreds of microfarads where the current is actually consumed[1365].

Where a ribbon is the whole power path, pin count is the capacity. A standard 0.1-inch ribbon carrying an entire instrument's supply is questionable practice; multiple pins are paralleled, but the working rule of thumb is roughly an amp per pin[475]. Instruments that do this properly use a wide cable dedicated to the job, such as the 10- or 12-way ribbon that feeds an analog section from its rectified secondary-side capacitors[731].

Return paths through ribbons are easy to misread. Reversed wire colouring on a supply harness, combined with a ribbon carrying the return current, produced a measured current of only tens of milliamps and an incorrect picture of where the current was actually flowing[512]. Mechanically, a power-carrying ribbon connector that allows insertion at an offset angle can end up misaligned on its pins, which is enough to blow a supply fuse on reassembly[905].

## Emissions, shielding and isolation

A long unshielded ribbon carrying a parallel memory or expansion bus off to an external box, with no metallised shielding in the case and no chokes on the I/O lines, is an emissions problem by construction[645]. Where designers take the issue seriously, the ribbons are shielded, earthed, and the earth connection properly terminated rather than left loose — a decision that has to be taken up front because it drives assembly labour[969]. Implementations range from foil shielding over the runs to the touch panel and LCD[648], to a ferrite around the cable to the front panel[1174], to shielded ribbons in a gold-foil pouch[969]. In an oscilloscope, an entire shield plate can be present over a ribbon with no board beneath it at all[384].

Creepage and clearance apply to the cable as well as the board. The distance between two pins across the surface of a ribbon connector is creepage; clearance is the air-to-air distance, and only applies where the boards or cable fold to create a physical air gap[388]. A charger that runs primary and secondary across the same ribbon cable with an inadequate gap fails on that basis[388].

## Failure modes

Ribbon cables fail mechanically far more often than electrically, and the failures cluster in a few patterns. Conductors break, most commonly at the ends where flexing and stress concentrate, and a break in the middle of an undamaged cable is unusual enough to be suspicious[1672]. A cable can shear off entirely where the assembly it is bonded to comes unglued[sqB-PnvQfO4][o4S-TkTuHZs]. Micro-cracks produce intermittents that resist wiggle testing[afErPINq8qc], and a repeatedly reseated or dislodged cable can be the entire fault[uDzj3xdqi7Y]. Repeated flexing across a hinge or removable assembly is a known long-term reliability risk; one early portable computer's removable keyboard ribbon was replaced in later revisions by a curly cable with a right-angle attachment[955].

The first diagnostic step is therefore to reseat the cable at both ends, since the fault may be mechanical rather than electrical at all[1376]. When continuity does fail, the cable is not automatically the culprit: a break traced through two ribbons and at least six jumper links turned out to be in a PCB trace, not in any of the cables[1672][1673]. Cables are also easy to damage during repair — excess heat will melt flat flex[808] — and the reassembly hazard is real enough that designers add plastic retainer clips to stop a cable flapping loose and being crushed as the case slides on[1185], or tape it down so it does not get pinched under the metalwork[1394][497].

## Orientation errors

Many 0.1-inch ribbon headers are unshrouded, unkeyed and carry no pin 1 marker, which makes reversed insertion a recurring and destructive mistake — in one case releasing the magic smoke from a chip almost instantly[513]. The same error appears as a non-working prototype that looks electrically dead[313], as a display fault chased at length before pin 1 was verified against the far-end pin[1602], and as damage to a driver board plugged into an Arduino[688]. Cables fitted backwards during a previous repair leave a diagnostic signature: a header pin with a trace going to it but no connection at the other end[1702].

## Practical judgement

Ribbon cables are cheap, flexible and fast to design in, and that is precisely why they proliferate past the point of good sense. Small four-way cables are fiddly to handle[1291]. Mounting a panel LCD on standoffs and running a ribbon to it wastes enclosure space and requires extra hardware, where a PCB-mount display can be fixed rigidly with no mounting screws at all[644][258]. A ribbon crossing to a front panel to serve a single switch is effort spent that a differently placed switch would not have required[1278].

Against that, the ribbon frequently is the right answer. Running a flat cable at right angles to reach a rear-panel connector is easier than designing a right-angle connector and getting it out the back[1237]. Where board-to-board interconnects are used instead, contact reliability depends on the boards being rigidly held; if they are only located by rear-panel screws, moving one board can disturb the others[1395]. And a ribbon is what makes a second-source design possible: a spare unpopulated footprint paralleled with the existing display connector lets the same board accept whichever TFT panel is available, by moving the cable across[295].
