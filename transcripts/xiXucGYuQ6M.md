In this inquiry, we've examined the evidence from submissions and our own research, and also had looked at the experience and policies of those countries overseas that have implemented a right to repair in one form or another.

**Dave Jones:** But what is a right to repair? There are a lot of definitions, we've heard a few today, floating around. But for the purposes of our inquiry, we've settled on this and may change for the final.

**Dave Jones:** A right to repair is the ability of consumers to have their products repaired at a competitive price by the repairer of their choice. Note that this does not mean that a consumer has the right to have a product repaired at any cost.

**Dave Jones:** We need to balance competing interests, those of consumers, manufacturers, retailers, and governments. For example, if my halogen light, if I had one, failed, it would be prohibitively expensive to have it repaired rather than to buy a replacement LED light.

**Dave Jones:** So, in this report, we've examined policies across a range of areas, product obsolescence and design standards, consumer law, competition in retail market, markets, intellectual property and e-waste. And I'll touch on these as we go.

**Dave Jones:** As you travel around Cuba, you'll see men hunched over cars repairing engines and fixing exhaust systems. Since the country lacks replacement parts, the locals are extremely crafty and adept in their repairs.
